H2O and group formation problems

Building H2O with semaphores and with a barrier, and the general pattern for releasing threads in groups of a fixed shape.

Asked atLinkedIn
Before this:Semaphores: permits as a resource limitBarriers and phasersCondition variables and monitors

H2O is the puzzle where threads may only leave in groups: exactly two hydrogens and one oxygen at a time, never mixed with the next group. Get it right and you own a pattern that also forms boat crews, game teams and fixed-size batches.

In plain words

A ride at a fair has cars that seat exactly two children and one adult, and the car leaves only when it is full in exactly that shape. Children and adults arrive at random. The ride operator does three jobs. She lets in only two children and one adult per car (admission). She keeps the first arrivals of a car waiting until the whole car is seated (rendezvous). And she does not start seating the next car until the current one has pulled away (separation).

Leave out any one of the three and the ride goes wrong: a car with three children, a car that leaves half empty, or a child from the next car squeezing into this one. These three jobs describe the successful path; fairness and failure handling need their own rules.

The problem (LeetCode 1117)

Threads of two kinds call into one shared object. Hydrogen threads call hydrogen(releaseHydrogen) and oxygen threads call oxygen(releaseOxygen). Each thread must wait until it can be part of a complete water molecule, then call its release function. The rule on the output: split the sequence of releases into consecutive groups of three, and every group has exactly two H and one O, in any order inside the group. All three atoms of one molecule release before any atom of the next molecule does.

So for 4 H threads and 2 O threads arriving in any order, "HHOHHO", "HOHOHH" and "OHHHHO" are all valid, and "HHHOOH" is not (its first group is HHH).

Clarify whether the interviewer requires only valid output triples or also requires every callback to wait for all three participants to arrive. Those are different contracts: two hydrogens can print H,H before any oxygen exists and still eventually produce valid triples. The first technique below demonstrates that weaker output contract; the barrier and monitor techniques also wait for a complete group to be available.

Unless a failure case is explicitly discussed, assume a balanced finite input, enough threads able to run, callbacks that finish without throwing or reentering the coordinator, and no cancellation after admission. Each callback records exactly one atom using thread-safe output; these synchronizers do not make two callbacks' ordinary writes safe by themselves.

The naive attempts

The first idea is a barrier of three: wait until three atoms are present, then release. It gets the size of the group right and the shape wrong, because nothing says which three:

The second idea limits each kind with a semaphore: two permits for hydrogen, one for oxygen, each atom releases its permit after printing. Now at most two hydrogen callbacks run simultaneously, but a consecutive output triple can still contain three hydrogens: H1 can print and give its permit to H3 before O1 has printed, so the output drifts into HHH runs. Admission without separation is not enough either.

Technique 1: ordered output with two counting semaphores

For the output-only contract, a short answer uses semaphores as counters of what has happened. hydrogenSlots starts at 2: the hydrogen places left in the current molecule. hydrogensOut starts at 0: the hydrogens of this molecule that have already released. An oxygen waits for both hydrogens to be out, releases, and only then hands the two hydrogen places to the next molecule.

This version always prints H,H,O, so it meets the output-order rule. Admission is hydrogenSlots, and returning slots after oxygen prints separates consecutive triples. It does not implement an all-member rendezvous: the first two hydrogen callbacks can run before oxygen arrives. For the stronger waiting requirement in the problem statement, use the barrier or monitor version.

There is a trap in the translation to C++, and it is the most instructive line in this lesson. Java's acquire(2) is all or nothing: the thread waits until two permits are free and takes both at once. C++ std::counting_semaphore has no such call, and two separate acquire() calls are not one atomic step. Without the extra mutex, this happens:

The oxygenGate_ mutex serializes oxygen collectors, because only one oxygen can be collecting hydrogens at a time. That is safe here: a molecule needs only one oxygen anyway.

Technique 2: slots and a barrier

The second classic answer names the three jobs directly. Two semaphores do admission (two hydrogen slots, one oxygen slot), a barrier of three does the rendezvous, and each atom gives its slot back only after its release, which does the separation: the next molecule cannot assemble at the barrier until all three slots of this one have come back.

Why can a hydrogen from the next molecule not slip in early? To reach the barrier it needs a slot, and a slot comes back only after a current atom has released. The next barrier round needs two hydrogen slots and the oxygen slot back, so it can only trip after all three atoms of this molecule have released. The barrier must be reusable for this to work: Java's CyclicBarrier and C++20's std::barrier both reset for the next round by themselves, while a one-shot latch would not.

Java

Under the Java 21 barrier contract, interruption or timeout during an unfinished barrier wait breaks that generation. Peers still waiting there get BrokenBarrierException, and future waits fail until reset. This does not cancel threads still blocked on admission semaphores, nor roll back callbacks already run. The sample propagates interruption, converts the broken-barrier exception, and returns acquired permits; it does not implement whole-job recovery.

C++

std::barrier is C++20. In C++17 interviews, write the barrier yourself as a monitor with a count and a generation number (the build-it-yourself lesson has it), or use the gate below, which needs only a mutex and a condition variable.

The C++ output callback takes a short mutex around the shared string. It both prevents concurrent string mutation and gives ThreadSanitizer a visible ordering for those accesses on this Mac, where libc++ barrier synchronization is partly inside an uninstrumented library. No application payload relies solely on that hidden barrier ordering here.

Technique 3: a monitor that forms groups of any shape

The semaphore tricks are tuned to "two of one kind and one of another". When the shape changes (H2SO4, a boat of four with rules about who may share it, a team of one captain and four players), you want one monitor that does all three jobs explicitly. The arriving thread, or the last member leaving the previous group, does the forming: it sees that enough threads of every kind are waiting and opens exactly the right number of seats for each kind. Waiting threads take a seat of their kind, do their work, and leave; the next group forms only when the last member of this one has left.

Three lines carry the three jobs. Admission: a group forms only when every kind has enough waiters. Availability: all required kinds are waiting before seats open. The gate does not bind identities at that point; for a true all-selected-members rendezvous, reserve specific waiters and wait for their arrivals. Separation: inGroup > 0 blocks the next group until the last member has called leave(). The waiters of one kind are interchangeable, so a newly arrived hydrogen may take a seat ahead of one that has waited longer. That never breaks a molecule, but it can starve an individual under an endless stream of arrivals. If fairness or team identity matters, queue waiter records per kind and assign named members to a generation.

The invariant is that inGroup counts both unclaimed seats and admitted members not yet finished. It reaches zero only after exactly the required number of successful admissions have left. run balances admission with finally in Java and a scope guard in C++; that prevents leaked membership on callback failure, but does not turn a failed callback into a successfully emitted atom. Waiting counts must fit in int, and the gate must outlive every user.

The general pattern

Group formation problems all reduce to the same three questions, and each technique answers them with a different tool:

JobQuestionTools
AdmissionWho may join the group being formed?A counting semaphore per kind sized to the shape, or seats handed out by the thread that completes the group
RendezvousHow does each member wait for the rest?A reusable barrier, or forming the group only when all members are already waiting
SeparationHow do you stop the next group mixing in?Combine slots returned after work with a reusable barrier, or keep an unfinished-member count that blocks the next group

Two more classics from Allen Downey's Little Book of Semaphores have the same skeleton. In the roller coaster problem, passengers board a car of capacity C, the car runs only when full, and nobody boards the next run until everyone has left the current one. In river crossing, a boat carries exactly four people, and the legal crews are four of one kind or two of each, never three and one.

Worked example: river crossing with the group gate

A boat holds exactly four people, hackers and serfs. Safe crews are four hackers, four serfs, or two and two. Three of one kind with one of the other is never allowed. How does the gate change?

For crew selection, change the forming rule. Instead of one fixed shape, try the legal shapes against the waiting counts; boarding and rowing need additional phases described below:

In formGroupIfPossible, call choose with the waiting counts and, if it returns a crew, open that many seats of each kind. The seats and inGroup still enforce composition and separation, but a real boat needs boarding and departure phases too. Elect one admitted member as captain under the monitor mutex, count four completed boardings, then let only the captain row. Everyone waits for that generation's trip-complete flag before leaving, so the next crew cannot start early. The forming thread cannot automatically be captain: it may be the last departing member of the previous crew.

A lone serf with no future serf arrivals cannot join any legal crew; fairness cannot invent a compatible partner. To prevent avoidable starvation, queue people per kind and choose a feasible shape containing the oldest eligible waiter. Use generation-specific membership, since a new arrival must not steal a reserved seat.

This extension also explains why a callback that completes after enter is not automatically a group action. Admission makes a seat available; a boarding barrier proves that all selected members have actually boarded.

Common mistakes

  • A barrier alone. It counts threads, not kinds, so three hydrogens can form a "molecule". Add admission per kind.
  • Admission without separation. Per kind semaphores alone allow consecutive output groups to mix when permits are reused. The slots-and-barrier version is safe because the next full barrier round cannot complete early.
  • Two acquire() calls where you meant one. In C++ (or Java with acquire() called twice), two oxygens can each take one permit and wait forever for a second. Use Java's acquire(2), or a mutex so only one thread collects at a time.
  • A one-shot latch for a repeating group. CountDownLatch and std::latch cannot be reset; every molecule needs a fresh rendezvous, so use a cyclic barrier or a generation number.
  • Waiting with if. In the monitor, a woken thread must re-check that a seat of its kind is still open; another thread of the same kind may have taken it.
  • Abandoning a promised seat. If a waiter can give up (timeout, interrupt) after a group was formed around it, the group can never complete. Either disallow cancellation once committed, or use a coordinated failed-generation protocol. Returning one permit alone cannot undo peers' callbacks or barrier arrivals.

In the interview

Building H2O is a reported LinkedIn question for senior roles in Bangalore: two 2024 reports name it as the concurrency round's problem, one notes that no hints were given, and one candidate was rejected after getting stuck on it. LeetCode's crowd reported tags also list Building H2O for Google and Apple. Rubrik's bathroom and playground problems (next lesson) exercise the same counting and admission skills with fairness added.

What the interviewer listens for: that you separate admission, rendezvous and separation, and that you can show why the next molecule cannot leak in.

I will split it into three jobs. Admission: two hydrogen slots and one oxygen slot as semaphores. Rendezvous: a reusable barrier of three, so each molecule waits for itself. Separation: every atom returns its slot only after it has released, so the next molecule cannot assemble at the barrier until this one is completely out. This barrier version needs only one acquisition per atom. In the separate output-only design, oxygen collects two hydrogen completions, so C++ needs a mutex around those two acquires.

Follow ups to expect: "Do it without a barrier" (the gate; the two-semaphore trick meets only the weaker output contract). "Make it H2SO4" (the gate with need = {2, 1, 4}). "What if a thread is interrupted while waiting?" (define whether cancellation precedes reservation or aborts the entire generation). "Make hydrogens enter in arrival order" (fair Java semaphores or tickets per kind). "Make callbacks execute in that order" (also serialize callback turns; fair admission alone does not do that). "How would you test it?" (shuffle the start order, check every group of three, repeat many times, run C++ under ThreadSanitizer).

Senior depth

The barrier action is a free hook. Java's CyclicBarrier takes an optional action that runs once per round, in the last thread to arrive, before anyone is released; C++20's std::barrier takes a nothrow-invocable completion function, typically a noexcept lambda, that runs once per completed phase with waiters. Use that for a group action when the API permits one thread to do it. H2O's individual callbacks still belong to their calling atom threads; logging also needs synchronization against any unrelated logger users.

Group formation is batching. Outside puzzles, the same skeleton forms fixed-size batches for a downstream system that is efficient only in groups, matches players into teams with role constraints, or packs work into a container of fixed capacity. For best-effort batching, a deadline may permit a smaller batch. A chemical shape or team-role constraint cannot be relaxed silently: on timeout, fail or cancel the group unless the API explicitly allows a partial result. Say so when the interviewer asks how you would run it in production.

Cancellation is a protocol. Before reservation, a monitor can remove an interrupted waiter under its mutex and wake others. After reservation, track the generation and a broken flag, stop admission, wake all affected waiters, and report failure. Callbacks that already caused external effects cannot be rolled back merely by resetting counts. Java barrier reset requires coordination so old-generation threads cannot join a new one; C++ std::barrier has no built-in broken state or timed wait, and dropping a party would change the required shape.

Enough runnable participants matter. A fixed pool can deadlock even with perfect synchronization: two hydrogen tasks occupy both workers while the oxygen task waits in the executor queue. Use a coordinator that queues atom requests without blocking scarce workers, or provide execution capacity and scheduling that let every required role run. Unbalanced finite input also leaves unmatched atoms waiting unless you define shutdown.

Passing the mutex. Downey's semaphore solution has a neat trick: the thread that completes a molecule keeps holding a mutex that blocks the next molecule from forming, and the oxygen releases it after the barrier. A lock released by a different thread from the one that took it must be a semaphore, which is the same lesson as the readers-writers lightswitch.

Key takeaways

  • State admission, rendezvous and separation, then add explicit fairness and failure contracts.
  • Two semaphores can enforce H,H,O output; per kind slots plus a reusable barrier also wait for all three arrivals.
  • Java's acquire(2) is atomic; two C++ acquire() calls are not, and two oxygens can deadlock on half a molecule each.
  • For any shape, use a monitor where the thread that completes a group opens exactly the right seats, and the next group forms only after the last member leaves.
  • Fair admission does not order callbacks. Timeouts may fail a group; partial groups require explicit permission in the contract.

Quick check

Answer each question, then read why. Your score is saved for your module test.

H2O is built with only a CyclicBarrier(3) (or std::barrier of 3): every atom waits at the barrier, then releases. Which output can appear?
Why. A barrier counts parties, not kinds. Three arriving hydrogens can all pass and print HHH, so valid composition is not guaranteed. A full barrier does make progress, and it imposes no oxygen-only rule. Admission per kind fixes the shape; separation is also needed to keep consecutive rounds from mixing their callbacks.
In the two semaphore H2O, the oxygen takes two permits from hydrogensOut. In C++ it calls acquire() twice with no extra lock. What can go wrong with two oxygen threads?
Why. Java's acquire(2) takes both permits atomically. Two separate acquires let two oxygens interleave and each hold half. No oxygen can call its release callback until it has collected both permits, so the bug strands participants rather than emitting an extra oxygen. Serializing the oxygen collectors prevents the split.
The two semaphore version starts with hydrogenSlots = 2 and hydrogensOut = 0; hydrogens acquire a slot, release, and add to hydrogensOut; the oxygen takes two from hydrogensOut, releases, and returns two slots. Inside each molecule, what is the order of releases?
Why. The oxygen cannot release until both hydrogens have released, and the next hydrogens cannot start until the oxygen returns the slots. That meets the output-only contract, but a hydrogen can still release before any oxygen arrives; it does not prove a full rendezvous.
In the slots and barrier version, an atom returns its slot only after it has released. Why does that matter?
Why. That is the separation job. If slots came back before callbacks finished, the next complete barrier round could pass while this molecule was still releasing. The barrier is reusable independently of permit timing, the semaphores are not fair, and their API permits earlier release even though this protocol does not.
Why is a CountDownLatch (or std::latch) of 3 the wrong tool for the rendezvous in H2O?
Why. After the first molecule counts it down to zero, every later wait passes immediately. Each molecule needs a fresh rendezvous, which a reusable barrier or a new generation provides. Many threads may await a latch, any permitted participant can count it down, and it does not require a semaphore.
In the group gate, a new group forms only when inGroup == 0. What breaks if you remove that check?
Why. The check is the separation job in the monitor version. Without it, a later formation can overwrite outstanding seats and add to inGroup before the current group finishes. Groups can mix and accounting can strand waiters; type-indexed seats still do not let hydrogens take oxygen seats, and there is no FIFO rule.
The river crossing boat takes four people. Which crews are legal?
Why. The rule forbids three and one. So the forming step must try the legal shapes against the waiting counts and, with three of one kind and one of the other waiting, form nothing and wait for more arrivals.

Checkpoint

Write your own answer first. Then compare with the model answer, or ask Bar Raiser to mark it like an interviewer would.

Write code
Implement the H2O output-only contract without a barrier, using semaphores and an oxygen mutex if needed. Explain why triples cannot mix, and which stronger requirement it does not meet.
Model answer

At most two hydrogens can be inside the current molecule because there are only two slots, and the slots come back only after the oxygen has released, so the next molecule cannot start before this one is complete. The oxygen releases only after both hydrogens have, and only one oxygen can collect hydrogens at a time, so every group of three releases is exactly H, H, O. Hydrogen may release before oxygen arrives, so use the earlier barrier or monitor when all participants must be present first.

Explain
Explain admission, rendezvous and separation, and point to the line that does each job in the slots and barrier version of H2O.
Model answer

Admission decides who may join the group being formed: hydrogenSlots with two permits and oxygenSlot with one mean that at most two hydrogens and one oxygen can be heading for the current molecule. Rendezvous makes the members wait for each other: bond.await() (or arrive_and_wait()) on a barrier of three releases nobody until all three are present, and because admission fixed the kinds, those three are exactly two hydrogens and an oxygen. Separation keeps the next group out until this one is done: on the successful path each atom returns its slot only after its release callback finishes, so the next molecule cannot gather three parties at the barrier until all three atoms of this one have released. Remove admission and you get HHH; remove the rendezvous and atoms release before their molecule is complete; remove separation and two molecules interleave.

Design
A game server must form teams of exactly 5 from a queue of players: 1 healer and 4 others. Players arrive on many threads and call joinTeam(role), which must return only when their team is complete. Design it, and say what you would change for production.
Model answer

This is group formation with need = {healer: 1, other: 4}, so I would use the group gate: one lock, waiting counts per role, open seats per role, and a count of members in the current team. The thread whose arrival makes the counts reach one healer and four others forms the team by opening one healer seat and four other seats and signalling all. Waiters take a seat of their role. For joinTeam to return only when the team is complete, I add a rendezvous: a team id assigned at forming time and a counter of seated members, and each member waits until all five seats are taken. Separation is not even required here, because teams are independent; several teams may form at once, but I cannot just drop the gate's inGroup check: I need separate membership, seats, completion state and cancellation state per team, with waiter records atomically removed from the shared role queues.

For production I would add a timeout so that a healer does not wait forever when no one else joins (return a "no team" result or relax the shape), fairness by arrival time so the same player is not passed over (tickets per role, take the oldest), and limits on how long a player may wait before being matched with a less ideal team. I would also make the forming step pick players by skill, which turns "any four others" into a choice, so the waiting players would be kept in a sorted structure instead of a count.

How a senior would say it
I use two hydrogen permits, one oxygen permit, and a reusable barrier of three. Each atom first takes a permit of its own kind, then waits at the barrier. That fixes both the size and composition of the group before any callback runs. After the barrier, each atom runs its callback and returns its permit only when that callback finishes. A fast hydrogen can finish, return its slot, and let a new hydrogen reach the next barrier phase. That is safe: the new hydrogen cannot pass that phase until two hydrogen slots and the oxygen slot have been returned. Those three returns prove that every callback from the previous molecule has finished. A barrier alone could release three hydrogens; permits alone could recycle hydrogen slots before oxygen prints. There is also a shorter scheme that always emits H,H,O, but its hydrogens can print before oxygen even arrives. I would clarify which waiting contract is required. This proof assumes balanced input, callbacks that complete without throwing, and no admitted participant disappearing. Failure handling needs an additional protocol.

Likely follow up: What happens if the only oxygen is queued behind two blocked hydrogen tasks in a two-worker executor?

Out loudabout 90 s
"Your group gate works in tests, then one player cancels and the service hangs. How would you debug and redesign that?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Distinguish cancellation before reservation from cancellation after a seat is committed
  • Explain that unfinished membership includes reserved seats, so abandoning one can strand the group
  • Remove unreserved waiters under the mutex and recheck feasible groups
  • Use generation-specific membership and failure state after reservation; wake all affected waiters
  • Explain why returning a permit or resetting a barrier cannot undo completed callbacks
  • Add role queues for fairness and test cancellation, missing roles and executor starvation
How a senior would say it
I would first find out whether the player cancelled before reservation or after the group committed a seat to them. Those need different transitions. The unfinished-member count includes reserved seats, not just people already doing work, so silently abandoning a reservation can keep that count above zero forever. Before reservation, I can remove the waiter under the monitor mutex and reconsider which groups are feasible. After reservation, I need explicit membership and a generation identifier. A cancellation marks that generation failed, stops successful completion, wakes its affected waiters, and makes every member observe the same outcome. Any replacement policy must be defined before letting the group proceed. Returning a permit is not enough: another member may already have called an external service, which a counter update cannot undo. Similarly, resetting a Java barrier without coordinating the old participants can put them into a new generation by mistake. For fairness I would keep a queue per role and reserve named waiters. I would test cancellation at each transition, a missing role, a callback failure, and an executor whose workers are all blocked on incomplete groups. Finally, a timeout should fail the team unless the product explicitly permits changing its required composition.

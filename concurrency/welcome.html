Welcome to Tandem

How Tandem works for the two of you, how every lesson is built, what the side panel does, and how to study so it sticks.

Tandem is built for two people preparing together: Piyush in C++, Shubhi in Java, the same course on the same days, each in their own language. The goal is blunt. After this section, no concurrency concept, question type or follow up in an interview should be new to either of you, because the practice here is meant to be harder than the interview.

In plain words

Think of two batters sharing one net session. Same pitch, same bowling machine, same coach, but different stances. The coach bowls at each one's weak spots, a scorer writes down what to fix and reminds you to come back to it, and a senior player stands by to answer any question between deliveries.

Tandem is that net session. The course is the pitch, Bar Raiser is the coach, Logbook is the scorer, and Navigator is the senior player. The matches you are training for are interviews, so everything here ends in something you can say, write or run under pressure.

Two learners, two languages, one course

The switch at the top right of every page says who is learning: Piyush (C++) or Shubhi (Java). It decides which language each code sample opens in, which language specific notes and questions you see, and whose progress, notes and test scores are saved. The other language is always one click away in the tabs on every sample.

Java here means Java 21. C++ means C++20, with the C++17 alternative mentioned wherever interviewers still expect it. Every idea in the course is shown in both languages, and every runnable sample is compiled and run before it reaches you.

The refresher first, then the course

There are two refreshers, C++ for concurrency and Java for concurrency, five modules each. They are not general language courses. They cover exactly what threaded code and interview code stand on: for C++, lifetimes, RAII, move semantics, smart pointers, lambdas and captures, std::chrono, the sanitizers and how std::thread maps to pthreads; for Java, references and the heap, final and immutability, equals and hashCode, the collections, InterruptedException, the JDK tools and what the JVM does under the hood.

Then come object oriented design for LLD and machine coding rounds, and a toolkit module that walks through every threading API in your language: in C++ std::thread and std::jthread, mutexes and lock guards, condition variables, std::atomic and memory orders, futures, and the C++20 semaphores, latches and barriers; in Java threads and virtual threads, synchronized and wait, volatile and the atomic classes, the java.util.concurrent locks, executors and CompletableFuture, and the concurrent collections and synchronizers. Each refresher lesson has a box called "Why this matters for concurrency". Do your own refresher first (your study plan schedules it), then come back to single lessons whenever the course links to them.

How every lesson is built

Every core lesson follows the same order, so you always know where you are:

  1. The lead: one or two sentences on why this matters.
  2. In plain words: an everyday picture, a kitchen, a railway station, a cricket scorer, before any jargon.
  3. The precise version: definitions, the mechanism, code in both languages, timelines and simulators.
  4. A worked example, then common mistakes with their fixes.
  5. In the interview: how the topic is asked, the follow ups, and quoted sentences written to be said out loud.
  6. Senior depth and under the hood: the details that separate a senior answer from a correct one.
  7. Key takeaways, then the quick check and the checkpoint.
  8. Say it out loud: two or three questions the way an interviewer asks them, which you answer by speaking, then practice this.

Each lesson carries a level. Core is required for everyone. Senior is senior round depth and still required. Stretch is useful for infrastructure and systems roles and can wait for a second pass. An "Asked at" badge appears only where candidate reports show a company asking about that topic.

Worked example: predict, run, explain

Take the first program and do what every lesson asks of you: predict the output before you press Run, then explain it. The prediction: the two hello lines can come in either order, and "Both finished" is always last. Here is why, step by step.

  1. Either order. start() in Java, or constructing a std::thread in C++, only makes the new thread runnable. The scheduler decides which one runs first, so the thread started second can print first.
  2. "Both finished" is always last. A join waits until that thread has finished, and everything the thread did happens before join() returns. Main prints only after both joins, so no hello line can come after it.
  3. Whole lines. In Java, System.out.println takes a lock inside PrintStream, so each line comes out whole. In C++ the standard says two threads writing to std::cout is not a data race, but their characters may interleave inside a line, so the sample prints under a mutex. (This Mac's libc++ also makes ThreadSanitizer flag unlocked std::cout from two threads, so every C++ sample on Tandem prints from one thread at a time.)
  4. What would break it. Join only piyush, and in Java "Both finished" may now print before shubhi's line. In C++, a std::thread destroyed without a join calls std::terminate, so the program crashes instead.

That is the method behind every "what can this print?" question you will meet: list what is ordered (here, only by join), and assume everything else can happen in any order.

How to study so it sticks

  1. Retrieve, do not reread. Answer each quick check before scrolling back. After a lesson, close it and write its main idea from memory. Pulling an idea out of memory strengthens it far more than reading it again.
  2. Space it out. Anything shaky goes into Logbook as revise later:. It comes back after 1, 3, 7 and 21 days, just as you are about to forget it.
  3. Write code every day, in your language, and run it. Rewrite each lesson's key sample from memory. Reading a correct solution teaches you to recognize it, not to write it under pressure.
  4. Explain out loud. Interviews are spoken. Answer each lesson's Say it out loud questions before you read the model answer, and say its interview sentences in your own words, to each other or to the microphone. Speaking like a senior shows how a strong spoken answer is built.
  5. Predict before you run. Guess the output, then press Run. For timelines, cover the last line and say what goes wrong.
  6. Log every mistake. Each wrong answer becomes a mistake: note saying why the right answer is right. These notes are your most valuable revision list.

Studying as a pair adds three habits that a single learner cannot have. Spend ten minutes a day teaching each other one idea from that day's lesson, each asking the other one follow up. Read each other's language now and then: debugging rounds hand you someone else's code, and the more code you can read, the less any of it can surprise you. And from the middle of the plan onwards, interview each other on problems, using the follow ups on each problem page. Your study plan turns all of this into a weekly rhythm.

Common mistakes

  • Reading without doing. A lesson read and not practised is mostly forgotten within days. Every lesson ends with a quick check and a checkpoint for this reason.
  • Skipping the checkpoint, or reading the model answer first. The model answer only helps after you have committed to your own.
  • Only running code that works. Run the broken samples too, and read the ThreadSanitizer report or the thread dump line by line. That is what the debugging round tests.
  • Starting hard problems before their lessons. Each problem names the lesson that teaches it. Without the lesson, the problem becomes guesswork.
  • Trusting one clean run. Concurrency bugs depend on timing. A correct output proves little; the tests, the sanitizer and your reasoning prove more.
  • Answering spoken questions in your head. Knowing an answer and saying it clearly, in order, inside 90 seconds are different skills. Record yourself, listen back, and answer again, even when it feels awkward.
  • Leaving mock interviews for the last week. Mocks show you what you cannot yet say under time pressure. Start them while there is time to fix it.

In the interview

Concurrency rounds are graded on what you say as much as on what you write: naming the shared state, explaining the interleaving that breaks, saying why you chose a primitive and what it costs. Every lesson's "In the interview" box ends with sentences in a quote block, written to be said out loud. Practise saying them until they sound like you, not like a script.

Before I write any code, let me name the shared state and the invariant I need to protect, then pick the simplest primitive that makes that invariant hold, and then walk through the interleavings that could break it.

That opening works for almost every concurrency question you will get, and by the end of this course you will be able to back up every part of it. Expect the follow up that comes after almost any concurrent solution, "how do you know it is correct?". One clean run is not an answer; the invariant, the interleavings you checked, and a stress test or a sanitizer run are.

Quick check

Answer each question, then read why. Your score is saved for your module test.

Your partner's C++ sample printed exactly the right total, but the run also shows a ThreadSanitizer data race report. What does that mean?
Why. A data race is undefined behavior in C++, so a correct answer on one run proves nothing about the next. ThreadSanitizer reports the race because it saw the unsynchronized accesses happen, whatever the output. Fix it with an atomic or a mutex, then run again until the sanitizer is quiet.
In a Tandem timeline, what does a row starting with ! in the source, shown in a red tone, mean?
Why. Rows marked ! are bad outcomes, + marks a good one and ? a risky moment. The full width line at the bottom states the lesson. Time always flows down, one step per row.
You want a note about memory orders to come back for spaced revision. How do you write it in Logbook?
Why. Revise later notes are the ones Logbook schedules for spaced revision, shown in the "Revise today" list when they fall due. Plain notes are kept and searchable, but they are not scheduled.
What is the most effective way to use a checkpoint question?
Why. Committing to your own answer first is what makes the comparison teach you something: you see exactly what you missed. Reading the model answer first feels productive but mostly trains recognition, and interviews need recall.
You press Run on a sample and it never finishes. What do you get back after a few seconds?
Why. A hang usually means a deadlock, a lost wakeup, a thread nobody told to stop, or a busy loop. The runner captures the state of every thread before stopping the program, so you can see the cycle or the missing signal for yourself.
In "Your first threaded program", main starts both threads, joins both, and then prints "Both finished". Which outputs are possible? Select all that apply.Select all that apply
Why. Starting a thread only makes it runnable, so the scheduler may run either one first: both orders of the hello lines are possible. join() returns only after that thread has finished, so main cannot print "Both finished" until both hello lines are out. The third option is the tempting one: it becomes possible only if main joins piyush and forgets shubhi.
In "A deadlock on purpose", thread a locks left then right, and thread b locks right then left. Which single change guarantees the program can never deadlock?
Why. A deadlock needs a cycle: each thread holds one lock and waits for a lock another thread holds. If every thread takes its locks in one global order, no cycle can form. Changing the nap does not remove the cycle: a longer nap in b still leaves each thread holding one lock, and removing the nap only makes the unlucky timing rarer, so the bug survives and comes back under load. Reentrancy lets a thread take again a lock it already holds; here each thread wants the lock the other holds, so it changes nothing (Java's synchronized is already reentrant, and the demo still hangs). In C++, a std::scoped_lock that takes both mutexes in one call is the other standard fix.

Checkpoint

Write your own answer first. Then compare with the model answer, or ask Bar Raiser to mark it like an interviewer would.

Explain
Your partner says: "My C++ counter printed 200000, exactly right. The ThreadSanitizer warning must be noise." What do you tell them?
Model answer

The warning is not noise. ThreadSanitizer reports a data race when it sees two threads access the same memory, at least one of them writing, with nothing ordering one access before the other: no lock both took, no atomic, no join. In C++ that is undefined behavior, which means the language makes no promise about the result at all. This run produced the right total by luck of timing. The next run, a different machine, or a higher optimization level can produce a wrong total, or something stranger, because the compiler is allowed to assume races never happen.

ThreadSanitizer's rare false alarms come from synchronization it cannot see, such as code compiled without the sanitizer or hand-written fences. A plain += on a shared int is not one of them. The fix is to make the shared update safe: std::atomic<int> with fetch_add, or a std::mutex around the read-modify-write. Then run it again under ThreadSanitizer until the report is clean. The rule is: a correct output never proves a concurrent program correct, but a sanitizer report on ordinary code like this proves it wrong.

Write code
Without looking at the sample above, write a program in your language that starts two threads, each printing its own name, and waits for both to finish before printing "done".
Model answer

Check yourself on three points. Both threads are started before either is joined, otherwise they would run one after the other. Both are joined before "done" is printed, so "done" is always last. And in C++, every std::thread is joined before it is destroyed; a joinable thread that is destroyed calls std::terminate. The order of the two "I am" lines can change from run to run, and that is correct.

Say it out loud

Interviews are spoken. Answer each question out loud as if the interviewer just asked it. Tandem transcribes you on this Mac, word for word, measures your pace, fillers and pauses, and Bar Raiser scores what you said and how you said it.

Out loudabout 90 s
"Your concurrent code passed every test you ran. How do you convince yourself, and me, that it is actually correct?"
What a strong answer covers
  • A passing run proves little: the failing interleaving can be rare and depend on timing, cores and load
  • Reason first: name the shared state and its invariant, and check every access is under the same lock or is one atomic step
  • Walk the risky interleavings on purpose: read-modify-write, check then act, and the order locks are taken in
  • Tools: ThreadSanitizer for C++ data races, where any report on ordinary code means it is broken
  • Stress tests with many threads and iterations, exact invariants checked at the end, and a timeout that fails with a thread dump instead of hanging
How a senior would say it
Passing tests are the weakest evidence I have, because a concurrency bug lives in one unlucky interleaving that may show up once in a million runs, or only on a busier machine. So I start with reasoning. I name the shared state and the rule it must keep, say the balance always matches the history, and I check that every read and write of that state happens under the same lock, or is a single atomic step. Then I walk the risky interleavings on purpose: two threads doing read, modify, write, a check followed by an act, and the order in which locks are taken. Then tools. In C++ I run the tests under ThreadSanitizer, and any report on ordinary code means it is broken, even if the output looked right. Java has no race detector in the JDK, so there I lean on stress tests: many threads, many iterations, exact final counts, and a timeout that fails the test and prints a thread dump instead of hanging. Reasoning tells me why it is correct. The tests and the sanitizer catch what my reasoning missed.

"Two threads each print one line, and main joins both, then prints 'done'. Walk me through every output that is possible. What changes if you forget one of the joins?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Starting a thread only makes it runnable; the scheduler picks the order, so the two lines can come either way
  • join waits for the thread to finish, and everything it did happens before join returns, so "done" is always last
  • Forget one join in Java: "done" can print before that thread's line, and the JVM still waits for the non daemon thread before it exits
  • Forget one join in C++: destroying a joinable std::thread calls std::terminate; std::jthread joins in its destructor
  • Whole lines are not free: Java's println locks the stream, but in C++ characters from two threads can interleave unless you lock
How a senior would say it
There are exactly two possible outputs: the two thread lines in either order, then "done". Starting a thread only makes it runnable. The scheduler decides who runs first, so the thread I started second can print first. "Done" is always last because join waits until that thread has finished, and everything the thread did happens before join returns. Main prints only after both joins. If I forget one join, it depends on the language. In Java, "done" can now print before that thread's line, and the program still waits for the thread before it exits, because it is not a daemon. In C++ it is a crash: destroying a std::thread that was never joined calls std::terminate. That is one reason I like std::jthread, which joins in its destructor. One more detail: in C++, two threads writing to std::cout can interleave characters inside a line, so I print under a lock.

Out loudabout 90 s
"A service hangs under load, and CPU is close to zero. What do you look at first, and what are you hoping to find?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Near zero CPU plus a hang means threads are blocked, not spinning: a deadlock, a lost wakeup, or a wait for something that never arrives
  • First move is evidence: dump every thread's stack (jstack or jcmd <pid> Thread.print in Java, a debugger or sample for C++), twice, a few seconds apart
  • A cycle of threads, each holding one lock and waiting for another's, is a deadlock; the JVM prints the cycle for you
  • No cycle: threads waiting on a condition nobody will signal, pool threads all waiting on tasks queued behind them in the same pool, or calls to a slow dependency with no timeout
  • The fix follows the cause: one global lock order, signal under the lock and wait in a loop, a separate pool, or a timeout on every remote call
How a senior would say it
Near zero CPU tells me the threads are not spinning, they are parked, so I am looking for what they are all waiting on. My first move is evidence, not guessing. I take a dump of every thread's stack: in Java that is jstack, or jcmd with Thread.print; in C++ I attach a debugger and print every thread's backtrace, or use sample on a Mac. I take two dumps a few seconds apart, so I know the threads are stuck and not just slow. Then I look for one of three shapes. A cycle, where thread A holds lock one and waits for lock two while B holds two and waits for one, is a deadlock, and the JVM prints that cycle for me. Threads waiting on a condition that nobody will ever signal is a lost wakeup. And every pool thread blocked on the result of a task still sitting in the same pool's queue is pool starvation; a slow dependency with no timeout looks similar. The fix follows the cause: one lock order, signaling under the lock with waits in a loop, a separate pool, or timeouts.

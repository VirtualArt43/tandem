Reentrant lock from scratch

Build a primitivemedium30 minjava.util.concurrent.locks.ReentrantLock, rebuilt on a plain monitor

"Implement a reentrant lock from just a lock" (a Google L6 report from 2021) and "write a custom lock without using synchronized" (a Microsoft senior report from the same year) are the same question underneath: what does a lock have to remember about who holds it?

The task

Build a reentrant lock: a mutual exclusion lock that the thread holding it may take again without blocking itself. The lock remembers its owner and a hold count; it becomes free only when the owner has called unlock() as many times as it called lock().

  • lock() takes the lock, blocking while another thread owns it. If the caller already owns it, it adds one hold and returns at once.
  • tryLock(timeout) does the same but gives up after timeout and returns false. A timeout of 0 means "try once". The owner always succeeds at once.
  • unlock() removes one hold; at zero holds the lock is free and one waiting thread may take it. A thread that does not own the lock may not unlock it: throw IllegalMonitorStateException (Java) or std::system_error with std::errc::operation_not_permitted (C++, the same error std::unique_lock::unlock uses).
  • isHeldByCurrentThread() and getHoldCount() answer for the calling thread; a thread that is not the owner has a hold count of 0.

Rules.

  • Java: build it on a plain monitor, synchronized with wait and notify. Your class is named ReentrantLock, but the library class of that name, and ReentrantReadWriteLock, StampedLock, Semaphore and AbstractQueuedSynchronizer, are off limits. C++: build it from std::mutex and std::condition_variable; std::recursive_mutex and std::recursive_timed_mutex are off limits. The runner rejects code that names them.
  • Java: lock() is not interruptible, like the library's: if the waiting thread is interrupted, it keeps waiting, gets the lock, and returns with its interrupt flag still set. tryLock(timeout) is interruptible: it throws InterruptedException and does not take the lock.

What the tests check

  • One thread locks twice and tries once: the hold count goes 1, 2, 3, and three unlocks free the lock. A lock that is not reentrant blocks on the second lock(), and the test reports it instead of hanging.
  • The owner holds twice: another thread stays blocked after the first unlock() and gets in right after the second.
  • Unlock by a thread that does not own the lock throws, and leaves the owner's holds untouched; so does unlocking a free lock. A non-owner sees isHeldByCurrentThread() false and a hold count of 0.
  • tryLock(200 ms) against a held lock returns false after about 200 ms; tryLock(5 s) returns true soon after the lock is released.
  • Java: an interrupted lock() keeps waiting, gets the lock in turn and keeps its interrupt flag; an interrupted tryLock throws InterruptedException and takes nothing.
  • A stress run: 8 threads in random start order take the lock with random nesting depth (1 to 3, through both lock and short tryLock calls), check that nobody else is inside and that the hold count matches the depth, and add to a plain counter that must end exact. In C++, ThreadSanitizer must stay quiet.

Taught in: Reentrant and recursive locks

Open them one at a time. Each one gives away a little more.

Two fields describe the lock completely: who owns it, and how many holds the owner has. A thread id (Thread, std::thread::id) is a fine owner value, and "nobody" is null or a default constructed std::thread::id.
lock: under the monitor, if the owner is me, add a hold and return. Otherwise wait in a loop while there is an owner, then become the owner with one hold. unlock: check the caller is the owner (else throw), remove a hold, and at zero clear the owner and wake one waiter. Why is waking one enough here?
For the Java interrupt rules: in lock(), catch InterruptedException around wait(), remember it, keep looping, and call Thread.currentThread().interrupt() after you own the lock. In tryLock, let the exception propagate; you have not changed any state yet, so there is nothing to undo.

What the interviewer asks after your first version works. Try to answer each one out loud before you open it.

Microsoft (2021): now write it without synchronized. What do you use?
An AtomicReference<Thread> owner and compareAndSet(null, me) to take a free lock. The hold count needs no atomics, because only the owner ever touches it. For waiting you either spin with Thread.onSpinWait() (fine when holds are very short) or keep a queue of waiting threads, park them with LockSupport.park and unpark the next one in unlock. That queue plus CAS design is exactly what AbstractQueuedSynchronizer implements under the library lock.
Google (L6, 2021): build a reentrant lock from "just a lock", a plain non-reentrant mutex and nothing else.
Keep an atomic owner and a plain count next to the mutex. lock: if the owner is me, add one and return; otherwise lock the mutex, then set owner to me and count to 1. unlock: subtract one; at zero, clear the owner and unlock the mutex. Reading the owner without the mutex is safe only because it is atomic and because the one value you care about, "me", can only have been written by your own thread. This is how many recursive mutexes are built. In C++, the mutex is always unlocked by the thread that locked it, as std::mutex requires.
Why must unlock by a non-owner be an error and not just ignored, or allowed?
Because it is always a bug, and silently allowing it breaks mutual exclusion: thread B frees a lock that A still believes it holds, a third thread walks in, and A and C are now inside together. Ignoring it hides the bug instead. Ownership is the difference between a lock and a binary semaphore; std::mutex makes the same mistake undefined behaviour, and debug checks or error checking mutexes turn it into an error.
A thread holds the lock three times and calls await() on one of its conditions. What should happen?
The wait must release the lock completely, all three holds, or the thread that is supposed to signal could never get in, and the waiter would sleep forever. On wakeup it must take the lock back with exactly three holds. Java's library lock saves and restores the hold count around await for this reason.
Is your lock fair? What would make it first come, first served?
No: a thread that arrives just as the lock is released can take it before the woken waiter does (barging), and notify picks an arbitrary waiter. For FIFO, keep a queue of waiting threads (or tickets) and hand the lock directly to the head in unlock, so nobody else can grab it in between. The library offers a fair mode this way; it trades throughput for bounded waiting.
Reentrant locks are convenient. Why do some engineers avoid them?
Reentrancy lets a method call back into the same object while an outer method is half way through changing it, so the inner call can see a broken invariant even though "the lock is held". It also hides lock ordering and makes it easy to forget how many holds you have. Non-reentrant locks (like std::mutex) force you to separate "lock and call" from "assume locked" methods, which many teams prefer.

Try for at least twenty minutes first. Reading a solution feels like learning; writing one is learning.

The idea

A lock that knows its owner. Two fields under one monitor: owner and holds. The invariant: holds == 0 exactly when there is no owner, and only the owner changes holds. Reentrancy is the first if in lock; ownership is the first if in unlock; the rest is an ordinary condition wait for "no owner".

Why each line is there

  • The reentrant shortcut comes first. If the owner is me, add a hold and return without waiting; waiting would mean waiting for myself, a self-deadlock.
  • Wait in a loop for "no owner". Spurious wakeups, and another thread barging in between the wake and the re-check, both mean the condition must be checked again.
  • Become owner with exactly one hold. Holds belong to an owner; a fresh owner starts at one.
  • Check ownership before touching anything in unlock. A non-owner must not change the count or the owner, so the throw comes first.
  • Wake one waiter at zero holds. Every waiter waits for the same thing, "no owner", and one free lock can serve exactly one of them. With synchronized that means notify; it is safe here because nothing else waits on this monitor. In C++ it is notify_one.
  • Interrupt handling in Java. lock() swallows the interrupt only to keep waiting, then restores the flag; returning early would mean returning from lock() without the lock, the worst possible bug. tryLock throws before it changes any state.

Cost and trade-offs

Every call takes the internal monitor once, so an uncontended lock costs one monitor enter and exit plus a few field writes. Production locks make the uncontended path a single CAS on an owner word and only fall back to a queue and parking when there is contention. This version is unfair; a fair one hands the lock to the head of a queue at the cost of a context switch per handover.

How to say it in the interview

The lock keeps an owner and a hold count under one monitor. lock checks first whether I already own it and if so just adds a hold; otherwise it waits in a loop until there is no owner and takes it with one hold. unlock throws if I am not the owner, otherwise removes a hold, and at zero clears the owner and wakes one waiter, since any one waiter can use the free lock. tryLock is the same wait against a deadline. lock is not interruptible, so it remembers an interrupt and restores the flag once it owns the lock; tryLock is interruptible and throws before changing anything. Without synchronized I would use a CAS on an atomic owner plus a park and unpark queue, which is what AQS does.

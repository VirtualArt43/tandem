The dining philosophers

Why everyone grabbing the left fork deadlocks, four ways to break the cycle, and who starves under each of them.

Asked atApple
Before this:Deadlock: the four conditions and how to break eachTaking several locks safelySemaphores: permits as a resource limittryLock, timeouts, interruptible locking and fairness

The dining philosophers problem is the textbook deadlock. Interviewers use it to see whether you can name the circular wait, break it on purpose with a reason, and then talk honestly about who can still starve.

In plain words

Five friends sit at a round table, each with a plate of noodles. There is one chopstick between every pair of neighbors, so five chopsticks in all, and you need two to eat. Everyone follows the same polite rule: pick up the chopstick on your left, then the one on your right, eat, put both down.

One evening all five sit down at the same moment and pick up their left chopstick together. Now every chopstick is taken, and each friend waits for the right one, which is in the neighbor's left hand. Nobody is rude and nobody is broken, yet nobody will ever eat, because each one waits for the next one around the table.

The problem, precisely

N philosophers, with N >= 2, sit in a ring with N forks. Philosopher i's left fork is fork i and its right fork is fork (i + 1) mod N, so fork i is shared by philosophers i and (i + N - 1) mod N. Each philosopher loops: think, pick up both forks, eat, put both down. The full problem asks for four properties; a deadlock-free fix alone need not satisfy all four:

  • Mutual exclusion: a fork is used by at most one philosopher at a time.
  • No deadlock: the table never freezes with everyone waiting.
  • No starvation: a hungry philosopher eventually eats.
  • Concurrency: two philosophers who share no fork can eat at the same time. With five, at most two eat at once (in general, N / 2 rounded down).

Each fork is a mutex. Picking up two forks is taking two locks, so this is really the general question "how do you take several locks without deadlocking", dressed up for dinner. That is why it matters beyond the puzzle: two opposite-direction transfers can create the same two-lock cycle as two philosophers.

Why everyone grabbing the left fork deadlocks

The naive code is exactly the polite rule: lock left, lock right, eat, unlock both. Many schedules complete, so testing alone can miss the bad one. The demo below forces the unlucky timing with a latch, so that every philosopher holds the left fork before anyone reaches for the right one. It uses three philosophers to keep the thread dump short.

All four conditions for deadlock are on the table at once, and naming them is the first thing an interviewer wants to hear:

ConditionAt the table
Mutual exclusionA fork is held by one philosopher at a time
Hold and waitA philosopher keeps the left fork while waiting for the right one
No preemptionNobody can take a fork out of a neighbor's hand
Circular waitP0 waits for P1, who waits for P2, who waits for P0

Break any one of the four and the deadlock is impossible. Every solution below is a choice of which condition to break, and the choice decides what you pay. Mutual exclusion is the one you cannot break (a fork really cannot be shared), so the shared-memory fixes below remove circular wait or blocking hold and wait. Ordinary mutexes cannot safely be confiscated from their owners.

Fix 1: order the resources

Number the forks and make one rule for everyone: always pick up the lower numbered of your two forks first. Philosophers 0 to 3 notice no change, because their left fork already has the lower number. Philosopher 4 sits between fork 4 and fork 0, so it now reaches for fork 0 first. That single change makes a cycle impossible. This is lock ordering, and it is the first answer to give.

Worked example: proving resource ordering cannot deadlock

An interviewer will often say "prove it". Here is the whole proof, short enough to say out loud.

  1. Suppose the table is deadlocked. Then there is a cycle of philosophers, each holding one fork and waiting for a fork held by the next philosopher in the cycle.
  2. Every philosopher takes the lower numbered fork first, so a waiting philosopher holds fork a and waits for fork b with a < b.
  3. The fork it waits for (b) is the fork the next philosopher holds, so that philosopher holds b and waits for some c > b, and so on around the cycle.
  4. Going around, the numbers strictly increase: a < b < c < ... < a. A number cannot be less than itself, so the cycle cannot exist.

The same argument works for any set of locks with a strict global order, provided every acquisition follows it, including locks already held on entry. Use unique stable ids or hierarchy levels with a tie rule. For unrelated object pointers in C++, use std::less if ordering by address; built-in pointer < does not provide a portable total order. That is why "always take locks in one global order" is the general cure, not just a dinner trick.

One philosopher can reverse the order. On this simple ring, making philosopher N - 1 take right then left is exactly the lower-number-first rule. Reversing any one philosopher also breaks the ring's directed cycle; that shortcut is specific to the ring, while a global resource order works for arbitrary sets of locks.

Fix 2: a waiter who seats at most N minus 1

Put a waiter at the door who lets at most four of the five philosophers reach for forks at once. The proof uses the ring structure: a wait cycle must include every philosopher. With at least one philosopher outside, the chain ends at a free fork or at a philosopher already able to finish eating. Under eventual scheduling and finite meals, someone can advance and release forks. In code the waiter is a semaphore with N - 1 permits, and the philosophers may keep the naive left-then-right order.

Admission must happen before acquiring either fork, and the permit must be returned after releasing both. The extra semaphore adds contention and scheduling overhead, although the capacity of four does not reduce the theoretical maximum of two simultaneous eaters. This N - 1 proof is specific to the ring: a smaller cycle can still deadlock inside an arbitrary resource graph. Notice what the waiter does not fix: a lock order checker that only looks at the order forks are taken still sees left-then-right everywhere, so it may still report a potential cycle that the semaphore makes unreachable. Prefer ordering when you can; admission limits help only when you can prove that the limit excludes every possible cycle. C++ std::latch and std::counting_semaphore require C++20; in C++17 use a mutex and condition variable as in building a semaphore and latch.

Fix 3: try for both, or put the left one down

Break hold and wait instead: pick up the left fork, try the right fork without waiting, and if it is busy, put the left fork back and try again later. In this protocol a philosopher can block on its first fork, but never blocks waiting for its second, so no cycle of waiting can form. In Java this is tryLock() on a ReentrantLock; in C++ it is try_lock, here through std::unique_lock with std::try_to_lock. See tryLock.

The price is a new failure mode. If all five pick up the left fork, all fail on the right, all put down, and all retry after the same fixed delay, they can repeat that dance forever: busy, polite, and getting nowhere. That is livelock. Random backoff and jitter reduces repeated collisions but is not a deterministic progress guarantee: an unlucky philosopher can still lose again and again. These waits pace retries; correctness does not depend on their duration. The requested delays are illustrative, and the runtime may resume much later. Retry counts in the output are only an example.

C++

C++ supplies a deadlock-avoiding multi-lock operation: std::scoped_lock both(fork_[left], fork_[right]); uses std::lock for multiple mutexes; the implementation chooses its lock/try/release strategy. This C++17 guard is the idiomatic answer when the mutexes are distinct and the caller holds neither. It does not promise fairness, randomized backoff, or protection from cycles involving other locks already held.

Java

Java has no built in "lock these two" call like C++'s std::scoped_lock. Your options are ordering (the usual answer), tryLock with backoff as above, or tryLock(timeout, unit) followed by releasing the first fork on failure. A timeout on the second lock alone does not bound the whole operation: first-lock acquisition and repeated retries also need a deadline and cancellation policy.

Fix 4: Chandy and Misra, for when there is no table to look at

The first three fixes use shared-memory locks; ordering and try-and-release do not need a central observer. Chandy and Misra's message-passing solution extends dining to an arbitrary graph of neighbors sharing forks. Start the philosophers thinking. Give every fork one owner, initially the endpoint with the lower unique id, and mark every fork dirty. That initial priority relation is acyclic.

A hungry philosopher requests missing forks. A requested dirty fork is cleaned and sent only when its owner is not eating; requests during eating are deferred. A requested clean fork is retained until its owner eats. Eating marks the owned forks dirty, and after the meal deferred requests are serviced.

A hungry owner that gives away a needed fork requests it back. The clean/dirty rules maintain an acyclic priority relation and rotate priority away from philosophers that have eaten. Deadlock and starvation freedom depend on finite meals, eventual message delivery and request processing, and processes that keep taking steps. A crashed owner or lost fork message violates those assumptions. The senior follow up is to explain ownership, deferred requests and those progress assumptions before attempting the full distributed protocol.

Fairness and starvation

Deadlock freedom excludes a permanent circular lock wait. With eventual scheduling, finite meals and no other failure, blocking schemes can keep making progress; retry schemes can still livelock. Neither statement guarantees progress for each person. A philosopher can starve even in a deadlock free solution if its neighbors keep winning the forks.

SolutionDeadlockStarvationWhat it costs
Left then rightPossiblePossibleIt is the bug
One global lock around eatingImpossibleDepends on that lock's fairnessOnly one philosopher eats at a time
Resource orderingImpossible (no circular wait)Impossible with fair, first in first out forks; possible with barging locksA shared ordering convention; waiting with one fork can reduce concurrency
Waiter with N - 1 permitsImpossible (the cycle cannot close)Impossible with a fair semaphore and fair forksOne more synchronizer on every meal
tryLock both or releaseImpossible (no hold and wait)Possible; randomized retries still give no deterministic progress boundWasted work on retries
Chandy and MisraImpossibleImpossibleMessages and per fork state

The progress guarantees in this table assume that runnable threads eventually execute, meals finish, and requests are not abandoned while retaining resources. Fair locks govern lock admission, not CPU scheduling; Java's untimed tryLock() can barge even on new ReentrantLock(true), so it does not inherit FIFO admission.

The classic textbook monitor solution, where a philosopher may eat only when neither neighbor is eating, shows the gap clearly: it never deadlocks, but philosopher 1 can starve if philosophers 0 and 2 take turns eating so that one of them is always eating. For ordered acquisition, fair fork locks suffice under those assumptions: the highest-ranked fork cannot be held while waiting for a higher one, so its holders finish, and the argument works down the order. Java supplies new ReentrantLock(true); the waiter design also needs fair semaphore admission, such as new Semaphore(N - 1, true). C++ std::mutex has no fairness option; a ticket lock orders admission but spins, so a blocking request queue is usually preferable for long meals.

A starvation-free waiter can queue whole meals. Put hungry requests in FIFO order under one monitor. Grant the head only when both forks are free, reserve both atomically, remove that request, then grant further heads whose forks are free. Eating happens outside the monitor; on return release both reservations and retry admission. No new conflicting request can bypass a blocked head, so existing finite meals drain and the head eventually proceeds. This guarantee starts once a request is enqueued and assumes eventual access to the coordinator lock.

This preserves some nonadjacent concurrency but can leave usable forks idle behind a blocked head. Remove cancelled requests and rerun admission under the same lock.

A run where everyone eats 2,000 times does not prove starvation freedom. Track fork ownership in callbacks, assert that neighbors never eat together, exercise reversed starts, and arrange two nonadjacent eaters to overlap. Use bounded joins and a timeout for failures; use a proof for every-schedule progress. In Java, interruptions while acquiring the second ordered lock must release the first, as the checkpoint shows.

Common mistakes

  • "It ran a million times without hanging, so it is fine." The naive version needs everyone to grab the left fork in the same window, which testing may miss. A deadlock you cannot reproduce is still a deadlock; the proof is the cycle in the code.
  • Thinking fair locks prevent deadlock. Fairness decides who gets a free lock next; it does nothing when every lock is held and every holder waits. Fair locks with left-then-right still deadlock.
  • tryLock without releasing the first lock. If a failed tryLock loops back while still holding the left fork, nothing was fixed: the philosopher holds a fork and waits, just more busily.
  • Retrying with a fixed delay. Workers can retry in lockstep and livelock. Randomized backoff reduces that risk; it does not establish a hard waiting bound.
  • A waiter with N permits. Admitting all five changes nothing. Use a positive limit no greater than N - 1; zero permits would admit nobody.
  • Ordering by something that can tie. When you order real objects, use a unique key such as an id. If two objects can compare equal (two equal hash codes), add a tie breaker lock, or ordering silently stops covering those pairs.

In the interview

It is asked directly in some loops: an Apple candidate reported a dining philosophers question in one team's onsite, and LeetCode's crowd reported tags list The Dining Philosophers (LeetCode 1226) for Microsoft and Bloomberg, and for Google at low frequency. More often it appears in disguise: "transfer money between two accounts", "a job needs two machines", or a debugging round where a simulation takes two resources in different orders.

What the interviewer listens for: the four conditions named, the circular wait identified as the one to break, a fix with a proof, and an honest word on starvation.

The naive version deadlocks because of a circular wait: everyone holds their left fork and waits for the right one. I will break the cycle by ordering the forks and always taking the lower numbered one first. Then a cycle would need fork numbers that increase all the way around the table, which is impossible. That gives deadlock freedom; for starvation freedom I would add fair admission and assume every scheduled holder finishes its meal. Fairness can cost throughput.

Follow ups to expect: "Prove it cannot deadlock." "Can someone still starve?" "How many can eat at once?" (two of five, N / 2 rounded down in general). "Solve it without changing the order" (the waiter, or try and release). "What does the thread dump of the naive version show?" (every philosopher thread blocked on a fork whose owner is the next philosopher, and in Java a "Found one Java-level deadlock" section). "Now generalize it to transfers between accounts."

Senior depth

The general rule behind the puzzle. For these exclusive single-owner resources, deadlock corresponds to a cycle in the wait-for graph, and every fix removes one of the four conditions. In a large codebase you rarely control the order of every lock, so teams assign each lock a level (a lock hierarchy) and only allow taking a lock of a higher level than any you hold. Some runtimes can check this for you at debug time.

Ordering objects you did not number. For two accounts, order by account id. If there is no natural id, Java code often orders by System.identityHashCode, which can tie, so the standard trick is a third "tie breaker" lock taken first whenever the two hashes are equal. In C++, std::scoped_lock avoids ordering that set explicitly, provided all mutexes are distinct and initially unowned by the caller. Handle a transfer to the same account separately, and deduplicate accounts in a multi-account transfer.

Detection instead of prevention. Many database engines allow dynamically ordered locking, detect deadlocks and abort a victim transaction; applications can still reduce cycles with consistent ordering. In Java 21, ThreadMXBean.findDeadlockedThreads() detects cycles involving platform threads and monitors or ownable synchronizers. It does not detect virtual-thread cycles, nor every hang involving a semaphore or condition wait. Prevention is right for a small set of locks you own; detection and abort is right when transactions take locks in arbitrary order.

Key takeaways

  • Left-then-right deadlocks because all four conditions hold, and the circular wait closes when everyone grabs at once.
  • Resource ordering breaks the circular wait and is the first answer: lower numbered fork first, provable in four lines.
  • A waiter with N - 1 permits also breaks the cycle, and try-and-release breaks hold and wait at the cost of retries and possible livelock.
  • Deadlock freedom is not starvation freedom: add fair admission or a proved priority protocol, with scheduling and finite-work assumptions.
  • The same reasoning covers every "take two locks" problem, from bank transfers to job schedulers.

Resource ordering (always pick up the lower numbered fork first) prevents deadlock by breaking which condition?

Why. Any fork-wait cycle in this ring requires all five philosophers to participate. With one outside, a waiting chain ends at a free fork or a philosopher who can finish. The semaphore neither makes the locks fair nor serializes all meals: two nonadjacent philosophers can still eat. This is prevention, not a probability reduction.
Every philosopher locks the left fork, calls tryLock on the right one, and on failure unlocks the left fork and retries after exactly 1 ms. What can happen?
Why. Nobody blocks while holding a fork, so it cannot deadlock. But identical retry timing can repeat the same failing pattern forever. Random backoff reduces repeated collisions but does not prove eventual progress for every schedule. The locks still exclude neighbors, so sharing a fork is impossible, while the lockstep schedule disproves guaranteed liveness.
Spot the bug in this attempt at "try for both forks":
Why. The whole point of try and release is to put the first fork down when the second is busy. Here the left fork is locked again on every loop (a ReentrantLock just counts up), never released, so it can strand a neighbor. After any failed iteration, one final unlock is insufficient to return the hold count to zero. tryLock exists, onSpinWait is only a spin hint, and replacing the try with a blocking lock restores the circular wait.
All five fork locks are made fair (first in, first out) and philosophers still pick up left then right. What does fairness change?
Why. Fairness only orders who gets a lock when it becomes free. In the circular wait no fork is ever released, so the queue order is irrelevant. Fairness helps with starvation, not with deadlock.
With five philosophers, what is the maximum simultaneous eating allowed by fork exclusivity, assuming a solution that permits nonadjacent eaters?
Why. Each eater uses two of the five forks, and two neighbors cannot both eat. Two eaters use four forks; a third would need two more, but only one is left. Philosophers 0 and 2 show that two is achievable, so one is not the maximum. In general the limit is N / 2 rounded down.
transfer(from, to, amount) locks from and then to. The accounts are distinct and have unique stable ids; callers hold no account lock on entry. Thread A runs transfer(x, y) while thread B runs transfer(y, x). Which change fixes the deadlock without a global lock?
Why. This is the dining philosophers with two philosophers. Swapping the order for everyone just moves the cycle; ordering by a global key breaks it. Fairness cannot free a held fork, and volatile does not change lock acquisition. In C++, std::scoped_lock lock(from.m, to.m); also works under the stated distinct-lock assumptions.

Write code
Implement LeetCode 1226: wantsToEat(philosopher, pickLeftFork, pickRightFork, eat, putLeftFork, putRightFork) for 5 philosophers, called from many threads, with no deadlock. Assume callbacks finish without throwing or re-entering. Use resource ordering, and explain the additional requirements for starvation freedom.
Model answer

The callbacks only report the actions; the real exclusion is the two fork locks, taken in one global order (lower number first) and released when the scope ends. Philosopher 4 is the only one whose order differs from left-then-right, and that is enough to make a cycle impossible. In C++, std::scoped_lock both(fork_[left], fork_[right]); is another deadlock-free answer. These default locks do not promise starvation freedom; use the fair admission design above when that is required.

Java's nested finally blocks release the first lock even if acquiring the second is interrupted. In C++, guards release held locks during exception unwinding. That does not automatically pair reporting callbacks when eat throws: a production contract must define which successful pick callbacks require compensating puts, make cleanup nonthrowing, and report failure to the caller. The puzzle code deliberately assumes nonthrowing callbacks.

Explain
Explain to an interviewer why the naive solution deadlocks, using the four conditions, and compare two fixes by what they cost.
Model answer

A fork can be held by one philosopher (mutual exclusion); a philosopher keeps the left fork while waiting for the right one (hold and wait); nobody can take a fork away (no preemption); and if all five pick up the left fork together, each waits for the next one around the table (circular wait). With all four present, that state is permanent.

Resource ordering breaks the circular wait: everyone takes the lower numbered fork first, so a cycle would need ever increasing fork numbers that come back to the start, which is impossible. It needs no extra synchronizer and is my default, though holding one fork while waiting can reduce concurrency. Try and release breaks hold and wait: take the left fork, try the right without blocking, and put the left down on failure. It needs no global order, which helps when you cannot impose one, but it wastes work on retries, can livelock even though randomized backoff reduces the risk, and does not stop one unlucky philosopher from starving. Neither fix alone guarantees starvation freedom; for ordered blocking acquisition I would add fair locks, or use a FIFO allocator for whole fork pairs. Fair locks do not make an untimed try-and-release loop starvation-free.

Design
A payments service runs transfer(a, b, amount) on many threads, and a transfer must hold both account locks. Sometimes the service freezes. Diagnose it and design the fix, including what happens when a transfer touches the same account twice.
Model answer

Diagnosis: two transfers in opposite directions, x to y and y to x, each lock their source first, so each holds one account and waits for the other. That is a two philosopher dining table. A thread dump would show two threads each blocked on the lock the other owns.

Fix: lock in a global order, the account with the smaller id first, regardless of which is the source. Then no cycle can form between any number of concurrent transfers. Edge cases: if a == b, lock once (or reject the transfer), because recursively acquiring a C++ std::mutex violates its precondition and may deadlock. In Java, if accounts had no unique id, I could order by System.identityHashCode and use a tie breaker lock when the hashes are equal. In C++ I could use std::scoped_lock after handling the same-account case, while holding neither mutex on entry. If transfers can involve many accounts, the same rule extends: deduplicate the accounts, sort by id and lock them in that order. For starvation I would make the locks fair only if monitoring showed some transfers waiting far longer than others, because fairness costs throughput.

How a senior would say it
I would break the circular wait by numbering the forks and always acquiring the lower numbered fork first. In the broken state, each fork is exclusive, each philosopher holds one while waiting for another, nobody can confiscate a fork, and the waits form a cycle. Those are the four deadlock conditions. With ordering, a philosopher holding fork a can wait only for a higher numbered fork b. Following a supposed cycle would give strictly increasing fork numbers that eventually return to a. That is impossible, so the cycle cannot exist. Every code path must obey the order, including any locks the caller already holds. For this ring, the philosopher between forks zero and four changes acquisition direction. I still allow nonadjacent philosophers to eat together; with five forks, at most two can eat at once. This proves deadlock freedom, not starvation freedom. An unfair lock can keep bypassing one hungry philosopher. If eventual service is required, I would add fair admission and explicitly assume that runnable holders get CPU time and finish their meals.

Out loudabout 90 s
"Your solution finishes a million meals without a hang. Can you claim that nobody starves? What would make that claim defensible?"
What a strong answer covers
  • A finite successful run does not prove starvation freedom
  • Show how an unfair admission policy can repeatedly bypass one hungry philosopher
  • Ordered blocking acquisition with fair locks needs eventual scheduling and finite meals
  • Java untimed tryLock bypasses fairness; C++ mutex has no fairness option
  • A FIFO waiter can reserve both forks atomically and prevent later requests bypassing a blocked head
  • Explain the concurrency cost and use tests as evidence alongside a proof
How a senior would say it
No. A successful finite run shows progress on the schedules we tested, but it cannot prove that every hungry philosopher eventually eats. With unfair locks, neighbors can repeatedly win while one philosopher keeps waiting, even though the table remains active. For ordered blocking acquisition, I would use fair fork admission and state the remaining assumptions: runnable threads eventually execute, holders finish their meals, and nobody abandons a held resource. The highest ranked fork cannot depend on a higher one, so its holders finish; the argument then works down the order. In Java, fair ReentrantLock instances support that policy, but untimed tryLock can still barge. C++ mutexes provide no fairness switch. Another design is a FIFO queue of whole meal requests. Under a monitor, reserve both free forks for the queue head, then consider the next head. Eating happens outside that monitor. Later requests cannot bypass a blocked head, so existing finite meals drain and it eventually gets its forks. The tradeoff is that a blocked head can leave unrelated forks idle, although consecutive compatible requests can still eat together. I would test ownership, overlapping nonadjacent eaters and bounded completion, but I would use the admission argument, not the meal count, to justify starvation freedom.

"We replaced fork locking with try-and-release, but the threads stay busy and nobody eats. What is happening?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Identify livelock when failed attempts release resources but repeatedly collide
  • Describe the lockstep acquire, failed try, release and retry schedule
  • Verify release on every failure path and use randomized backoff only to reduce collisions
  • Do not claim fairness or a deterministic waiting bound from random retries
  • Use a deadline for the whole attempt or choose ordered blocking acquisition or queued admission
How a senior would say it
This can be livelock. The threads are taking steps, but none completes a meal. All philosophers can acquire their left forks, fail to try the right forks, release the left forks, and repeat the same pattern. I would first verify that every failed attempt actually releases its first fork, including exception paths; otherwise we still retain resources while waiting. Randomized backoff can reduce repeated collisions, but it is not a proof of eventual service or a fixed waiting bound. In particular, a fair Java lock does not make untimed tryLock respect its queue. If the operation must give up, I would apply one deadline to the whole attempt, including first-lock acquisition and retries, and return holding nothing. If starvation freedom matters, I would prefer ordered blocking acquisition with fair admission, or a waiter that queues whole fork pairs.

Lock granularity, contention and lock striping

How much one lock should guard, why one hot lock caps your throughput, and how splitting and striping let threads work in parallel.

Asked atDatabricksMetaNetflixApple
Before this:Mutexes and RAII guardsThe rules of locking

A correct program with one big lock can still fall over under load: add cores and nothing gets faster. Choosing how much each lock guards is how you turn "thread-safe" into "thread-safe and scales", and it is where many "now make it fast" follow ups go.

In plain words

A housing society keeps everyone's parcels in a cupboard of 64 small drawers. With one padlock on the whole cupboard, only one resident can open it at a time, even if everyone wants a different drawer. With a padlock on every drawer, nobody waits for anyone else's drawer, but the guard now carries 64 keys, and a resident who needs three drawers must open three locks.

The middle way: 8 padlocks, each covering one row of 8 drawers. Your drawer number tells you which padlock to open. Two residents wait for each other only when their drawers share a row. That middle way is lock striping, and choosing between the three is choosing the lock's granularity.

Granularity: how much one lock guards

Lock granularity is the amount of state a single lock protects. It is a trade-off, not a virtue:

Coarse (one lock for the whole structure)Fine (many locks, each guarding a part)
CorrectnessEasy: every invariant is under one lockHarder: an invariant that spans parts needs several locks
ParallelismNone: every operation waits for every otherOperations on different parts run together
Whole-structure operations (size, iterate, resize)TrivialNeed many locks at once, or accept an approximate answer
Deadlock riskNone from this lock aloneReal, whenever one operation takes two locks
MemoryOne lockOne lock per part

The rule that keeps you safe: a lock can be no finer than the invariant it protects. If a balance and its transaction history must agree, they need one lock, however tempting two would be. Granularity decisions are about state that is truly independent.

Contention, and Amdahl's law in miniature

Contention is threads trying to take a lock that another thread holds. An uncontended lock is cheap (tens of nanoseconds, as an order of magnitude); a contended one makes threads spin or sleep, and each sleep and wake costs microseconds.

Here is the arithmetic every senior engineer should be able to do on a whiteboard. If every operation must hold one global lock for a time t, then at most one operation per t can complete, however many threads or cores you have. Hold a lock for 2 microseconds per request and the whole process tops out at about 500,000 requests per second, on 4 cores or on 64.

The same idea, as a fraction, is Amdahl's law. If a fraction s of the work is serial (here, the time spent inside the global lock), then N threads speed it up by 1 / (s + (1 - s) / N) times, and never more than 1 / s times. Ten percent of the time under one lock means a hard ceiling of ten times; the formula gives only about 6.4 with 16 threads.

One precision an interviewer may test: the formula models a single job whose serial part runs while everything else waits. In a server, one request's time inside the lock overlaps with other requests' work outside it, so throughput can climb closer to the ceiling than the curve suggests (the bound is the smaller of N and 1 / s). The ceiling itself does not move: nothing gets past 1 / s.

Real locks fall short of these ceilings, because handing a lock between threads is not free: when waiters sleep, each hand off pays a wake-up, and that time is serial too. The fixes all attack the serial fraction.

Four ways to reduce contention

  1. Shrink the critical section. Do everything that does not touch shared state (parsing, formatting, computing, and above all I/O) before taking the lock, and hold it only for the shared update. This is the cheapest fix and often the biggest.
  2. Lock splitting. If one lock guards two independent pieces of state, give each its own lock. Java's LinkedBlockingQueue is the classic example: one lock for the head (consumers) and one for the tail (producers), so producers and consumers do not block each other.
  3. Lock striping. For one big collection, use N locks and let each key use lock number hash(key) mod N. The rest of this lesson is about this one.
  4. Stop sharing, or stop locking. Per-thread data merged later, atomics, LongAdder, or a concurrent map that is already finely locked.

Lock striping

Striping keeps the simplicity of "each key has exactly one lock" while letting unrelated keys proceed in parallel. The cleanest version is N small maps, each with its own lock: a key always goes to the same stripe, so every per-key operation needs exactly one lock and one small map.

With four threads, the striped Java version runs about four times faster: almost no two threads want the same stripe at the same moment, so the serial fraction nearly disappears. The C++ version usually shows a bigger gap (closer to ten times on the Mac that runs these samples). Four threads cannot do the work more than four times faster, so the extra is pure contention cost: the single contended std::mutex keeps putting waiters to sleep, and every hand off pays a wake-up. How big that cost is depends on the platform's mutex. Under ThreadSanitizer both times are much larger, so compare the ratio, not the milliseconds.

Choosing the stripe and the stripe count

  • Use a power of two and a mask. hash & (n - 1) is fast and never negative. In Java, hashCode() % n is negative for negative hash codes, so indexing the stripe array with it throws ArrayIndexOutOfBoundsException; use Math.floorMod if n is not a power of two.
  • Mix the hash first. A mask keeps only the low bits, and many hash codes differ mostly in the high bits (Java's Integer.hashCode is the value itself). Folding the high half into the low half, as ConcurrentHashMap does, spreads keys evenly.
  • Pick N for collisions, not for keys. What matters is how often two busy threads land on one stripe. With T threads each holding a stripe a fraction f of the time, the chance that an operation finds its stripe taken is about 1 - (1 - f/N)^(T - 1). Sixteen to a few hundred stripes is typical; more stripes cost memory and make whole-map operations slower.

Operations that touch every stripe

Striping makes per-key operations cheap and whole-map operations hard. size(), clear(), iteration and resizing all touch every stripe. You have three honest options:

  • Approximate. Visit the stripes one at a time, as total() above does. Cheap, never blocks the whole map, but the result is not a snapshot: it can be a number that the map never actually held. Document it.
  • Exact. Acquire every stripe's lock, always in index order so two whole-map operations cannot deadlock, then compute, then release. Correct, but it stops the world.
  • Maintain it. Keep a separate concurrent counter (LongAdder, or an atomic) updated on every insert and remove. Fast to read, but it can briefly disagree with the stripes.

The same ordering rule covers any operation that needs two stripes, such as moving an entry from one key to another: compute both stripe numbers, lock the smaller one first, and take one lock if they are equal. That is lock ordering, which you will meet properly in taking several locks safely.

When striping does not help

  • Hot keys. If one key gets most of the traffic, its stripe is still one lock. Split the value instead (several sub-counters for that key, summed on read, which is what LongAdder does for one number).
  • Tiny critical sections. If the protected work is a 20 ns map update, a single unfair lock can match or even beat stripes: the lock and the data stay in one core's cache while the threads take turns in bursts, whereas stripes spread the same shared entries across cores and pay cache misses. Run the demo above with the work call removed and you may see exactly that. Measure before striping.
  • Many tiny acquisitions in a row. A thread that updates 1,000 entries by taking and releasing the lock 1,000 times pays 1,000 acquisitions and invites 1,000 hand offs. Taking the lock once per small batch is coarser on purpose, and often faster; keep the batch bounded so other threads are not shut out for long. HotSpot's JIT does a version of this itself, merging adjacent synchronized blocks on the same object into one (lock coarsening).
  • False sharing between stripes. Locks packed next to each other in memory can share a cache line, so two threads on different stripes still fight over the line. Give each stripe its own cache line (the alignas(64) above); the details are in caches and false sharing.

How ConcurrentHashMap evolved

Java's ConcurrentHashMap is the best-known striped structure, and "how does it achieve concurrency?" is a real interview question (one Databricks round spent most of its time on exactly that).

  • Java 5 to 7: segments. The map was an array of segments (16 by default, set by concurrencyLevel). Each segment was its own small hash table and was itself a ReentrantLock. A write locked one segment; reads mostly took no lock, relying on volatile fields. size() first tried summing the segments without locking, checking that nothing changed meanwhile, and locked all segments only if it kept changing. This is classic lock striping.
  • Java 8 and later: per-bin locking. The segments are gone. There is one table of bins, and the "stripe" is a single bin. Inserting into an empty bin is a compare-and-swap with no lock at all; inserting into a non-empty bin locks that bin's first node with synchronized. get() takes no lock. Bins that collect many keys turn into small balanced trees, resizing is done cooperatively by the threads that run into it, and the size is kept in a striped counter like LongAdder, so size() is an estimate while writers are active. concurrencyLevel survives only as a sizing hint.

Know the coarse alternatives too, because "how is it different from Hashtable or Collections.synchronizedMap?" is a standard opener. Both use one lock for the whole map: every method of Hashtable is synchronized on the table, and synchronizedMap wraps each call in synchronized on one mutex object (the wrapper itself by default). They are coarse locking, with the coarse ceiling. Iterating them is also on you: you must hold that same lock around the whole loop yourself, or the iterator can throw ConcurrentModificationException. ConcurrentHashMap iterators are weakly consistent instead: they never throw it, and they may or may not show updates made during the loop.

Two consequences come up in interviews. First, computeIfAbsent, compute and merge run their function while holding the bin's lock: they are atomic per key, but the function must be short and must not update other keys of the same map (since Java 9 a detected recursive update throws IllegalStateException). Second, no null keys or values: in a concurrent map, get returning null must mean "absent", because a follow-up containsKey could already be stale.

And the companion question, "why is a plain HashMap not thread-safe?": two threads inserting into the same empty bin can both link their node and one is lost; the size field is an ordinary read-modify-write; and a resize can be seen half done. Before Java 8, a concurrent resize could even link a bucket into a cycle so that a later get() looped forever at 100 percent CPU. Java 8 changed resizing so that particular loop is gone, but the lost entries and corrupted structure remain.

C++

C++ has no standard concurrent hash map. In interviews you stripe your own (an array of mutex plus std::unordered_map stripes, exactly like the demo), or use std::shared_mutex stripes when reads dominate and are not tiny. In production, libraries such as oneTBB's concurrent_hash_map and Folly's ConcurrentHashMap exist; say that you know they exist rather than guessing at their APIs.

Sharding: the same idea at system scale

Striping is sharding inside one process. A database partitioned by key hash across machines, a cache cluster that routes each key to one node, a service that sends all events of one customer to one worker: each shard owns its keys and has its own lock (or its own thread, or its own leader), so unrelated keys never contend. The same problems follow you up a level, too. Hot keys overload one shard. Operations that span shards need coordination, just as a move between two stripes needs two locks in a fixed order. And "count everything" means asking every shard and accepting either a slow exact answer or a fast approximate one. Being able to say "this is striping, one level up" is a strong senior signal in design rounds.

Worked example: a cache on one machine with 32 cores

A Meta system design report asked for memcache on a single machine with given cores and memory, and the interviewer praised splitting the hash table into roughly one part per core so it could be locked efficiently. Here is how that reasoning goes.

  1. One global lock. Each get or set holds it for, say, 200 ns (hash, probe, update the eviction list). Ceiling: about 5 million operations per second for the whole box, no matter that it has 32 cores. If the network layer can deliver more than that, the lock is the bottleneck.
  2. Shard the table. 64 or 128 shards, chosen by key hash, each a complete small cache: its own hash table, its own LRU list, its own lock, its own slice of the memory budget. Per-key operations take one shard lock. With 32 busy threads and 128 shards, collisions are rare.
  3. Accept what changes. Eviction becomes per shard, so the cache evicts the least recently used item of one shard, not of the whole cache: an approximation of LRU that is fine in practice. Global stats are summed across shards.
  4. Name the leftovers. A viral key still lands on one shard: handle it with a small replicated hot-key cache or by letting reads of that key skip the LRU update. Multi-key operations, if offered, lock shards in index order.

Common mistakes

  • Locks finer than the invariant. Separate locks for a balance and its history, or for a map and its size, let other threads see them disagree. One invariant, one lock.
  • hashCode() % n as a stripe index. Negative hash codes give negative indexes in Java. Use a power of two with a mask, after mixing the high bits in.
  • Whole-map operations that pretend to be exact. Summing stripes one at a time is an estimate. Either lock all stripes in a fixed order or say "approximate".
  • Taking two stripe locks in argument order, or even in key order. move(a, b) locking a's stripe first deadlocks against move(b, a). Key order fails too, because it is not stripe order: with 16 stripes, keys 3 and 20 land on stripes 3 and 4, while keys 4 and 19 land on stripes 4 and 3, so two moves in ascending key order still lock the same two stripes in opposite orders. Order by stripe index, and take one lock when the stripes are the same.
  • Check-then-act across calls on a concurrent map. if (!map.containsKey(k)) map.put(k, v) is still a race on a ConcurrentHashMap. Use putIfAbsent, computeIfAbsent or merge.
  • Slow work inside computeIfAbsent. The function runs under the bin lock, blocking every other update to that bin. Do not call a network service there; store a future and complete it outside.
  • Striping a hot key. One key, one stripe, one lock. Split the value, not the map.

In the interview

The usual path is: you make a structure thread-safe with one lock, and the interviewer asks "what happens with 64 threads?" or "how would you make it scale?" In a thread about a Netflix concurrency round, replies compared one lock with a partitioned concurrent hash map; Apple has asked candidates to write a thread-safe concurrent hash map; Databricks has spent most of a round on ConcurrentHashMap internals; and Meta design rounds hide the same question inside "design a cache on one machine". A Rubrik LRU report drew the same debate: one global lock against finer locks with an ordering rule.

A single lock makes the critical section the serial part of every request, so throughput is capped at one over the time inside the lock, whatever the core count. First I shrink the critical section and move anything slow out of it. If it is still hot, I stripe: N locks, each guarding its own sub-map, chosen by a mixed hash masked with N minus one. Per-key operations take one lock; anything that needs several stripes takes them in index order; and whole-map operations like size are either approximate or lock every stripe in order.
Striping does nothing for a hot key, so for skewed traffic I split the value itself, the way LongAdder does, or add a small hot-key layer. And I would measure: with tiny critical sections a single lock can win, because the data stays in one cache.

Follow ups to expect: "How does ConcurrentHashMap do it?" (segments before Java 8, per-bin CAS and locking since), "How is that different from Collections.synchronizedMap?" (one lock for everything, and you lock around iteration yourself), "Why no nulls?", "How many stripes?", "How do you implement size()?", and "What about resizing a striped map?" (resize each stripe's own table independently, which is one reason the N small maps design is popular).

Senior depth

  • Contention is a cache problem too. Even uncontended in the "nobody waits" sense, a lock word written by many cores ping-pongs between their caches. That is why striping helps even before threads actually block, and why a lock-free design that still writes one shared word (a single AtomicLong) does not scale.
  • Fine-grained structures need a story for structural changes. Per-node locks in a linked list or tree need hand-over-hand locking (lock the next node before releasing the current one) and careful deletion. Per-bucket locks need a plan for resizing the bucket array. Interviewers probe these edges once you propose fine locking.
  • Read-mostly plus striping. Stripes can be read-write locks when reads are long, or each stripe can hold an immutable snapshot for lock-free reads. Combine techniques per workload instead of picking one dogma.
  • Little's law view. If operations arrive at rate λ and each holds a lock for time t, that lock is busy a fraction λt of the time. As λt approaches 1, queueing delay explodes; striping into N divides each lock's load by roughly N, provided keys spread evenly.

Key takeaways

  • Granularity is how much one lock guards. Coarse is simple and serial; fine is parallel and complex. Never finer than the invariant.
  • A global lock held for t caps throughput at 1/t; Amdahl's law caps the speedup at 1/s for a serial fraction s.
  • Reduce contention by shrinking the critical section first, then lock splitting (independent state), then lock striping (one big collection), then by not sharing.
  • Striping: power-of-two stripes, mixed hash, one lock per key, multi-stripe operations in index order, and an honest choice for size and iteration.
  • ConcurrentHashMap moved from 16 locked segments (Java 7) to per-bin CAS and locking with lock-free reads (Java 8).
  • Sharding is striping at system scale, with the same hot-key and cross-shard problems.

Quick check

Answer each question, then read why. Your score is saved for your module test.

Ten percent of each request's time is spent inside one global lock. What is the most this service can speed up by adding threads, however many?
Why. Amdahl's law: with a serial fraction s, the speedup is at most 1 / s, here 1 / 0.1 = 10. The formula gives about 6.4 for 16 threads; a server can get closer to 10 than that, because one request's work outside the lock overlaps another's time inside it, but it can never pass 10. In practice lock hand offs keep it lower still. 90 times confuses the parallel fraction with the ceiling.
Every operation holds one global lock for 2 microseconds. Roughly what is the maximum throughput of the whole process, on any number of cores?
Why. Only one operation can be inside the lock at a time, so at most one completes every 2 µs: 1 / 0.000002 = 500,000 per second. More cores cannot raise that ceiling; only shrinking or splitting the critical section can.
One key receives 90 percent of all updates. You replace a single map lock with 64 stripes. What happens to throughput for that traffic?
Why. Striping spreads different keys over different locks; it cannot split one key. The fix is to split the value (several sub-counters summed on read, like LongAdder) or to batch updates to the hot key.
A striped map holds 100 entries. Its size() visits the stripes in index order, locking each only while counting it. Meanwhile one thread inserts key B (in stripe 0) and then removes key A (in stripe 5), so the true size goes 100, 101, 100. Which values can size() return? Select all that apply.Select all that apply
Why. 100 if the count runs entirely before or after both operations. 101 if it counts stripe 0 after the insert and stripe 5 before the remove. 99 if it counts stripe 0 before the insert and stripe 5 after the remove: a size the map never had at any instant. 102 would need two extra entries, which never exist. Stripe-by-stripe totals are estimates; an exact size must hold all stripes at once.
A queue protects its head with one lock (used by consumers) and its tail with a different lock (used by producers). What is this technique, and what does it buy?
Why. Splitting gives independent pieces of state their own locks. Striping partitions one collection by key. Java's LinkedBlockingQueue uses exactly this split; the shared element count is an atomic so both sides can read it, and the two ends meet only when the queue is empty.
Java onlyIn Java 8 and later, how does ConcurrentHashMap.put synchronize?
Why. Segments were the Java 5 to 7 design. Since Java 8 the unit of locking is a single bin, empty bins need no lock at all, and get is lock-free. concurrencyLevel is now only a sizing hint.
C++ onlyA striped map in C++ declares struct alignas(64) Stripe { std::mutex m; std::unordered_map<K, V> map; };. Why the alignas(64)?
Why. Cache lines are typically 64 bytes. Without alignment, the end of one stripe and the start of the next can share a line, and every lock or unlock of one stripe invalidates that line for the thread using the other. C++17 also offers std::hardware_destructive_interference_size for the value.

Checkpoint

Write your own answer first. Then compare with the model answer, or ask Bar Raiser to mark it like an interviewer would.

Design
Design the locking for an in-memory cache with get, set and LRU eviction on one machine with 32 cores, as asked in a Meta-style design round. Say what you give up.
Model answer

One global lock would make every get and set serial, because even a get updates the LRU list, so throughput would be capped at one over the critical section time regardless of the 32 cores. I would shard the cache into, say, 128 shards chosen by a mixed hash of the key. Each shard is a complete small cache: its own hash table, LRU list, lock and share of the memory budget. A get or set locks exactly one shard for a short critical section: hash lookup and list relink, with no allocation or I/O inside.

What I give up: eviction is LRU per shard, an approximation of global LRU, which is fine for a cache; global statistics are sums over shards, approximate unless I lock all shards in order; and multi-key operations, if offered, must lock shards in index order. Hot keys still land on one shard, so for heavy skew I would let gets of very hot keys skip the LRU relink (sampled or delayed recency updates) or add a small replicated hot-key layer. I would size the shard count so collisions between 32 busy threads are rare, and check for false sharing between shard headers.

Numbers
Each request takes 5 µs of CPU, 1 µs of it inside one global lock, and you run 32 threads on 32 cores. Estimate the throughput ceiling, the Amdahl speedup at 32 threads, and the chance that a request finds its stripe busy if you stripe the lock 128 ways with keys spread evenly.
Model answer

Ceiling from the lock: one request per microsecond, so at most about 1,000,000 requests per second, whatever the core count. Amdahl: the serial fraction is s = 1/5 = 0.2, so the formula gives 1 / (0.2 + 0.8 / 32) = 1 / 0.225, about 4.4 times one thread. One thread alone does 1 / 5 µs = 200,000 per second, so about 890,000 per second. Because requests overlap (one thread's 4 µs outside the lock runs while another holds it), a real server can get a little closer to the 1,000,000 ceiling than that, but no further: the lock, not the 32 cores, sets the limit, and the cores sit mostly idle.

With 128 stripes, each of the other 31 threads holds some stripe 20 percent of the time, and that stripe is ours with probability 1/128. The chance that none of them is on our stripe at that moment is about (1 - 0.2 / 128)^31, roughly 0.95, so about 5 percent of requests find their stripe busy, and then only briefly. The serial fraction almost vanishes, and the throughput approaches 32 times 200,000, about 6.4 million per second, until some other shared resource becomes the bottleneck.

Debug
A Java service caches user profiles in a ConcurrentHashMap with cache.computeIfAbsent(id, this::fetchFromProfileService), where the fetch is an HTTP call of about 100 ms. Under load, requests for unrelated users become slow. Explain why, and fix it.
Model answer

computeIfAbsent runs the mapping function while holding the lock of the key's bin. The 100 ms HTTP call therefore holds that bin locked, and every other update to any key in the same bin waits, including inserts for unrelated users whose ids hash there. Worse, so does every other computeIfAbsent on that bin, even a cache hit for a user who is already stored there: it takes the bin lock unless the key happens to be the bin's first node (a plain get would stay lock-free, but this code never calls one). During a resize, more operations can end up waiting too. The atomicity is right, but the critical section is enormous.

Fix: keep the per-key atomicity but make the critical section tiny by storing a future instead of the value, in a ConcurrentHashMap<Long, CompletableFuture<Profile>>. Create CompletableFuture<Profile> mine = new CompletableFuture<>() and call CompletableFuture<Profile> existing = cache.putIfAbsent(id, mine). If existing is null, this thread won: it runs the fetch outside the map and completes mine (on failure it calls completeExceptionally and cache.remove(id, mine) so a later call can retry). Otherwise it just waits on existing. Concurrent misses for the same id share one fetch (single-flight), and the bin lock is held only for the nanoseconds a putIfAbsent takes. In C++ the same pattern is a striped map of std::shared_future values, with the load done outside the stripe lock.

"Coarse or fine-grained locking: how do you decide how much one lock should guard?"
What a strong answer covers
  • A lock can be no finer than the invariant it protects: state that must agree shares one lock
  • Start coarse, because one lock is simple and correct, then measure contention before splitting
  • One hot lock caps throughput at one over its hold time, so shrink the critical section first
  • Then split truly independent state, or stripe one big collection by a hash of the key
  • Name the costs of fine locking: multi-lock ordering, whole-structure operations like size, more code
  • Know when coarse wins: tiny critical sections, and a hot key that striping cannot split
How a senior would say it
I start from the invariants. A lock can be no finer than the rule it protects, so if a balance and its history must agree, they share one lock, however tempting two would be. Within that limit I start coarse, because one lock is simple, easy to reason about, and cannot deadlock with itself. Then I measure. One lock caps throughput at one over the time it is held, whatever the core count, so if it is hot, the first fix is to shrink the critical section and move formatting and I/O out of it. If it is still hot, I split state that is truly independent, like the head and the tail of a queue, or I stripe one big collection: N locks, chosen by a hash of the key. Finer locking has a price. Anything that needs two parts must take the locks in a fixed order, operations like size become approximate or stop the world, and there is more code to get wrong. And sometimes coarse wins. With a twenty nanosecond critical section, one lock can beat stripes because the data stays in one cache, and no amount of striping helps a single hot key.

"Explain how Java's ConcurrentHashMap gets its concurrency. Then tell me how you would build the same idea yourself."
What a strong answer covers
  • Up to Java 7: segments, 16 by default, each its own small hash table and its own ReentrantLock: classic striping
  • Since Java 8 the stripe is one bin: a CAS fills an empty bin, a non-empty bin is locked by synchronizing on its first node
  • Reads take no lock: they rely on volatile reads of the table and the nodes
  • Size lives in striped counter cells, so it is an estimate while writers run; computeIfAbsent and merge run your function under the bin lock
  • To build it: a power-of-two array of stripes, each a lock plus a small map, picked by a mixed hash masked with N minus one
  • Operations on two stripes lock them in index order; a hot key still serializes on one stripe
How a senior would say it
It is lock striping, refined over two generations. Up to Java 7 the map was an array of segments, sixteen by default. Each segment was its own small hash table and was itself a ReentrantLock, so a write locked one segment, and writes to different segments ran in parallel. Java 8 removed the segments and made the stripe a single bin. If the bin is empty, a put installs its node with one compare and swap and takes no lock at all. If the bin already has nodes, the put synchronizes on the first node, so only writers to that one bin contend. Reads take no lock; they rely on volatile reads of the table and the nodes. The size is kept in striped counter cells, so size is only an estimate while writers are active. Two consequences matter in practice. computeIfAbsent and merge run your function while holding the bin lock, so keep it short. And nulls are banned, because a null from get must mean absent. To build the same idea myself, in either language, I would make an array of stripes, a power of two in size, each one a mutex plus a small hash map. A key goes to the stripe given by its hash, with the high bits mixed in, masked with N minus one, so every per-key operation takes exactly one lock. Anything that needs two stripes locks them in index order. And size either sums stripe by stripe and says it is approximate, or locks every stripe in order for an exact answer.

"We moved this service from 8 cores to 32 and throughput did not move. The CPUs sit mostly idle. What do you suspect, and how do you confirm it?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Flat throughput with idle cores means threads are waiting, not working: suspect a serial bottleneck, usually one hot lock
  • The arithmetic: a lock held for t caps the process at about 1/t operations a second on any number of cores
  • Confirm with several thread dumps under load: many threads blocked or parked on the same lock, one owner
  • Look at what runs inside that critical section, and move out anything slow such as I/O, logging or serialization
  • Then split or stripe, and check for a hot key, which striping cannot fix
  • If no lock shows up, check other single points: a small connection pool, one writer thread, a downstream limit
How a senior would say it
Flat throughput with idle cores means the threads are waiting, not working, so I suspect a serial bottleneck, and the usual one is a single hot lock. If every request holds one lock for two microseconds, the whole process tops out near five hundred thousand requests a second, on eight cores or on thirty-two. To confirm it, I take a few thread dumps under load. In Java, dozens of threads blocked on the same monitor, or parked on the same ReentrantLock, with one owner, is the signature. In C++ I would look at all the thread stacks for threads waiting in the same mutex. Then I look at what runs inside that critical section. Often it is something that never needed the lock, like logging, serialization or a network call, so the first fix is to move that out. If the lock is still hot, I split independent state or stripe the structure by key, and I check whether one key gets most of the traffic, because striping cannot help a hot key. If no lock shows up at all, I look for the other single points: a small connection pool, one writer thread, or a downstream limit.

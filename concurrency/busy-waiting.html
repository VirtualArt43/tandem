Busy waiting, and when spinning is right

Three ways to wait for an event: spin, poll with sleeps, or block. What each costs in CPU and latency, and the narrow cases where spinning wins.

Asked atAppleNvidia
Before this:Thread states and what blocking really doesHow locks are built: CAS, spinning, parking and futexes

After "only one thread at a time" comes the other half of concurrency: "not until this has happened". The most tempting way to wait is a loop that keeps checking, and it is usually the worst: it burns a whole core and can even delay the very thing it is waiting for.

In plain words

You put dal in the pressure cooker and need to turn off the gas after the third whistle. You could stand in front of the cooker staring at it: you will react the instant it whistles, but you get nothing else done (that is spinning). You could go to the next room and come back every ten minutes to check: you waste little effort, but you may be up to ten minutes late (that is sleep polling). Or you can sit down with a book and let the whistle call you: no wasted effort and no delay (that is blocking).

The whistle is the trick. Waiting well needs someone to tell you when the thing has happened, and the next lessons are about building those whistles.

Three ways to wait for something

  • Busy waiting (spinning): a loop that checks a condition over and over, while (!ready) {}. The thread stays on the CPU the whole time.
  • Sleep polling: check, sleep a fixed interval, check again, while (!ready) sleep(10 ms). The thread is off the CPU most of the time, but it only notices the event at its next check.
  • Blocking: ask the system to put the thread to sleep until another thread signals that the event happened, using a latch, a condition variable, a future, a blocking queue, or C++20 std::atomic::wait. The signalling thread wakes the waiter directly.

Measure all three on the same event, which happens 200 ms after the waiter starts. The program reports how late each waiter noticed the event and how much CPU it burned while waiting:

Read the three lines as a table of trade-offs. Spinning noticed within microseconds and burned the entire 200 ms of CPU doing it. Polling burned almost nothing but, in this run, was nearly 20 ms late, because the event landed just after a check; with a 20 ms interval it can be anywhere from 0 to about 20 ms late, plus the sleep's own overshoot. Blocking was both prompt and nearly free. That is why blocking is the default, and spinning needs a reason.

What spinning really costs

  • A core per waiter. Ten threads spin-waiting on a downstream reply occupy ten cores doing nothing. In a service, that CPU is taken from requests that could have run.
  • It can slow the thing you wait for. If cores are scarce, the spinning thread competes with the very thread that would set the flag. On one core it is absurd: the spinner keeps the CPU for its whole time slice while the producer cannot run at all.
  • Collateral damage. A spinning hyperthread steals execution resources from its sibling on the same core, and a busy core draws power and heats up, which can lower the clock speed of its neighbours.
  • Correctness traps. The flag must be volatile or an atomic in Java, and a std::atomic in C++. In Java, a plain field can be read once and cached in a register by the JIT, so the loop never sees the write and spins forever. In C++, a plain flag read while another thread writes it is a data race, which is undefined behavior. You will see why in visibility.

Sleep polling: cheap, but late, and still wasteful

Polling with a sleep fixes the CPU cost and creates a latency cost: an event that happens just after a check waits a full interval to be noticed. Shrink the interval and you burn more CPU on pointless wake-ups; grow it and you react later. You cannot have both low latency and low cost with polling. Inside one process, where threads can signal each other, polling is almost always the wrong tool; a "sleep to wait for another thread" in an interview answer is a red flag.

Polling is legitimate when nobody can signal you: checking the status of a remote job, a file that another program will create, a service that offers no notification. Then poll with exponential backoff and jitter and jitter, and a deadline, so a thousand clients do not hammer the remote side in lockstep.

Blocking: let someone wake you

Blocking moves the waiting into the operating system: the waiter is parked, costs no CPU, and the thread that makes the event happen wakes it directly. Everything in the rest of this module is a way to block correctly:

  • A one-time event: CountDownLatch(1) in Java, std::latch (C++20), or a future.
  • A condition on shared state, such as "the queue is not empty": a condition variable, the subject of the next lesson.
  • A value change on one atomic word: C++20 std::atomic::wait and notify_one, which use futex-style waits underneath (see how locks are built).
  • Handing items between threads: a blocking queue.

Blocking has one cost of its own: waking a parked thread takes microseconds (the "block" line above shows tens of microseconds of lateness). For almost every wait that is negligible. For a wait that is itself only a few microseconds long, it is not, and that is where spinning earns its place.

When spinning is right

Spin only when all of these hold:

  1. The wait is very short: shorter than a park and wake round trip, which is microseconds. If you cannot bound the wait, do not spin unboundedly.
  2. The thread you wait for is running on another core right now. Spinning for a thread that is descheduled or blocked on I/O is pure waste.
  3. There are spare cores, so spinning does not steal CPU from useful work.

Those conditions describe a few specific places: inside lock and synchronizer implementations, many of which spin briefly before parking; the retry loops of lock-free algorithms, which retry immediately because another thread just made progress; operating system kernels, which spin on locks held for a handful of instructions; and latency-critical systems that dedicate whole cores to polling, trading CPU for microseconds on purpose. Ordinary application code waiting for another thread is almost never one of them. Spinlocks themselves, with backoff, get their own lesson: spinlocks and when spinning beats blocking.

When you do spin, do it politely and briefly: put a spin-wait hint in the loop, and fall back to blocking after a bounded number of iterations. That combination, spin then block, is what adaptive locks such as HotSpot's synchronized already do internally:

The 1,000 is a demo value. A real spin budget is about the cost of a park and wake, a few microseconds, and is better set in time or calibrated on the machine, because one spin-wait hint costs very different amounts on different CPUs (see Senior depth).

The spin-wait hint is not a yield

Thread.onSpinWait() (Java 9) and the x86 PAUSE or ARM YIELD instruction tell the CPU "this is a spin loop": it can save power, avoid an expensive pipeline flush when the loop finally exits, and give more resources to a sibling hyperthread. The thread stays on the CPU; nothing is handed to the operating system. That is different from Thread.yield() and std::this_thread::yield(), which ask the scheduler to run someone else. Yields are system calls, are only hints (the scheduler may ignore them), and are not a fix for anything: a loop of yields is still a busy wait, just a noisier one.

Common mistakes

  • Spinning on a plain field. In Java the loop may never see the write; in C++ it is a data race. Use volatile or an atomic, and then ask whether you should spin at all.
  • Sleep to wait for another thread. Thread.sleep(100) "to give the other thread time" is a race that usually wins, until the day it does not. Wait for the event itself.
  • Unbounded spinning in application code. If the wait can be long, a spin loop turns a slow dependency into a CPU outage. Spin briefly, then block.
  • Treating yield as a fix. Yielding inside a loop still burns CPU and system calls, and offers no guarantee about who runs next.
  • Polling another process without backoff. Fixed-interval polling from many clients creates synchronized load spikes. Back off exponentially, add jitter, set a deadline.

In the interview

Busy waiting rarely appears as its own question; it appears as a bug in your answer. You write while (!done) {} or while (queue.isEmpty()) sleep(10) inside a producer-consumer, a crawler's termination check or an ordering puzzle, and the interviewer asks "what does this cost?" or "can you avoid the sleep?" An Apple report includes the direct version: why use wait() instead of a busy loop. Ordering puzzles such as print in order are where candidates spin most often.

A busy loop burns a full core for the whole wait and can even delay the thread that would end it, and sleep polling trades that for up to one interval of extra latency. So I block on something the other thread signals: a latch for a one-time event, a condition variable for a condition on shared state, or a blocking queue for hand-offs. I only spin when the wait is shorter than a park and wake, the other thread is running on another core, and there are spare cores, which in practice means inside lock implementations or lock-free retry loops, and even then I spin briefly with a pause hint and then block.

Follow ups to expect: "What does Thread.onSpinWait do?" (a CPU hint, not a yield), "What if there is only one core?" (never spin), and "Why must the flag be volatile or atomic?" (otherwise the loop may never see the write, and in C++ it is undefined behavior).

Senior depth

  • Virtual threads make spinning worse. A Java virtual thread runs on a small pool of carrier threads and only gives up its carrier when it blocks. A virtual thread that spins never blocks, so it holds a carrier for the whole wait and starves other virtual threads queued behind it. Blocking calls are exactly what virtual threads are designed for.
  • Spinning and priorities do not mix. If a high-priority thread spins while a lower-priority thread holds what it needs, a strict priority scheduler may never run the holder. That is priority inversion, covered in priority inversion and convoys.
  • The rule of thumb for spin then block. Spin for about as long as a block and wake would cost, then block. If the event comes during the spin, you paid what an oracle would have paid; if not, you paid at most twice the cost of blocking at once. So you are never worse than twice the best choice made with hindsight (the classic ski rental argument). Bound the spin in time, not in an iteration count tuned on one machine: Intel documents one PAUSE at about 10 cycles on older cores and up to about 140 on Skylake.
  • Not every mutex spins first. HotSpot's synchronized spins adaptively and AQS spins a little for a woken waiter, but glibc's default mutex, the one under a Linux std::mutex, parks as soon as its CAS fails; spinning there is the opt-in PTHREAD_MUTEX_ADAPTIVE_NP type. Either way a plain mutex beats a hand-written spinlock in ordinary code, because it stops burning CPU when the wait turns out to be long.
  • Devices face the same choice. A driver can wait for hardware by polling a status register, which is busy waiting, or by sleeping until the device raises an interrupt, which is blocking. Interrupts win at low event rates. At very high packet rates the per-interrupt overhead dominates, so Linux's NAPI keeps a busy network card in polling mode and returns to interrupts when traffic drops, and kernel bypass frameworks such as DPDK dedicate cores to polling. It is the same trade of CPU for latency and per-event cost, one layer down.
  • Measure the wake-up, not just the CPU. If a latency budget is a few microseconds end to end, the microseconds of a futex wake matter, and dedicated spinning cores become a deliberate design. Say that it is a deliberate trade of CPU for latency, and when you would not make it.

Key takeaways

  • Spinning reacts instantly but burns a core and can delay the event it waits for; polling saves CPU but adds up to one interval of latency; blocking is prompt and free, at the cost of a microsecond-scale wake-up.
  • Default to blocking: latches, condition variables, futures, blocking queues, std::atomic::wait.
  • Spin only for very short waits on a thread that is running on another core, with spare cores, and then only briefly before blocking.
  • A spin-wait hint (Thread.onSpinWait, PAUSE) helps the CPU; it is not a yield. Yields are not a fix.
  • Any flag you spin on must be volatile or atomic.

A thread spins in while (!done) {} for one second before another thread sets done. Roughly how much CPU time does the waiting thread use?
Why. An empty loop still runs instructions as fast as the core allows, so a one-second wait costs a core-second of CPU. The demo measured about 200 ms of CPU for a 200 ms spin. An empty body does not make it free; it just makes it pointless work.
A consumer polls with while (queue.isEmpty()) sleep(100 ms). An item arrives just after a check. How late can the consumer notice it?
Why. Nothing wakes a sleeping poller early, so an item that arrives right after a check waits a full interval: about 100 ms worst case, 50 ms on average. Shorter intervals cut the latency but burn more CPU on empty checks.
On a machine with a single core, thread W spin-waits for a flag that thread P will set. What does the spinning achieve?
Why. With one core, the waiter and the thread it waits for cannot run at the same time. The spin delays P by up to a whole time slice. A pause hint saves power but does not hand the core over. Blocking would let P run immediately.

Checkpoint

Write your own answer first. Then compare with the model answer, or ask Bar Raiser to mark it like an interviewer would.

Explain
In a code review you find while (!jobDone) { Thread.sleep(10); } (or std::this_thread::sleep_for(10ms) in C++) used to wait for a worker thread. The author says "it works and costs no CPU". How do you respond?
Model answer

It mostly works, but it trades CPU for latency and hides the real dependency. The waiter notices the job up to 10 ms late on every wait, which adds directly to request latency, and it wakes up a hundred times a second for nothing. If someone shortens the interval to fix the latency, it starts burning CPU instead; there is no good setting. It also only works if jobDone is volatile or atomic; otherwise in Java the loop may never see the update, and in C++ it is a data race.

Since the worker is in the same process, it can signal completion directly. For a one-time completion I would use a CountDownLatch(1) or a CompletableFuture in Java, and a std::latch, a std::promise and std::future, or a condition variable in C++; if the worker is started per task, simply joining it may be enough. The waiter then blocks with no CPU cost and wakes within microseconds of the job finishing. If the wait must be bounded, use the timed version (await(timeout), wait_for) instead of a sleep loop.

Write code
Write a one-time event class with set() and await() (blocks until set() has been called, returns immediately if it already was) that never busy-waits.
Model answer

In production Java I would simply use CountDownLatch(1), and in C++20 std::latch(1); the point is that the waiter blocks on a state (isSet), not on a notification, so a set() that happens before await() is never missed. The C++ version relies on atomic::wait checking the value before sleeping and returning only after it has changed.

Numbers
A service on 16 cores runs 64 request threads. Each request waits on average 2 ms for a downstream reply, and someone implemented that wait as a spin. At 20,000 requests per second, what happens to CPU, and what changes if the wait blocks instead?
Model answer

Each request spins for 2 ms, so the spinning alone needs 20,000 times 2 ms, or 40 core-seconds of CPU per second: 40 cores' worth, on a 16-core machine. The machine saturates on spinning before doing any real work; the threads that would process replies queue for CPU, replies are handled late, the spins get longer, and latency spirals. At most 16 threads can spin at once, so throughput collapses toward what the leftover CPU allows.

If the wait blocks, waiting threads use no CPU. By Little's law about 20,000 times 0.002 = 40 requests are in the waiting state at any moment, which 64 threads can hold, and the CPU is spent only on actual work plus a few microseconds per wake-up. The fix costs nothing but the right primitive: a future, a latch or a condition variable instead of a loop.

Say it out loud

Interviews are spoken. Answer each question out loud as if the interviewer just asked it. Tandem transcribes you on this Mac, word for word, measures your pace, fillers and pauses, and Bar Raiser scores what you said and how you said it.

Out loudabout 90 s
"One thread has to wait until another finishes some work. Compare spinning, sleeping in a loop and blocking. Which do you use, and when would you pick another?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Blocking is the default: parked at no CPU cost, woken directly by the signaller within microseconds
  • Spinning notices in nanoseconds but burns a whole core and can delay the thread it waits for
  • Sleep polling saves CPU but adds up to one interval of latency, and no interval gives both
  • Names the primitive: a latch or future for a one-time event, a condition variable for a condition, a blocking queue for hand-offs
  • Spin only for waits shorter than a park and wake, with the other thread running on another core and spare cores; briefly, with a pause hint, then block
  • Poll only when nobody can signal you, with backoff, jitter and a deadline
How a senior would say it
I block by default, because it is the only option that is both prompt and cheap. Spinning notices the event within nanoseconds, but it burns a whole core for the entire wait, and if cores are scarce it competes with the very thread that would end the wait. Sleeping in a loop fixes the CPU but adds up to one sleep interval of latency, and there is no good interval: shorten it and you burn CPU on empty checks, lengthen it and you react late. Blocking parks the waiter, which costs no CPU, and the thread that finishes the work wakes it directly within microseconds. So for a one-time event I use a latch or a future, for a condition on shared state a condition variable, and for handing items over a blocking queue. I would spin only when the wait is shorter than a park and wake, the other thread is running on another core right now, and there are spare cores, which in practice means inside lock implementations. Even then I spin briefly with a pause hint and fall back to blocking. And I poll only when nobody can signal me, like a remote job, with backoff, jitter and a deadline.

Likely follow up: What changes if the machine has only one core?

Out loudabout 85 s
"After a deploy, CPU on a service is pinned near 100 percent, yet throughput fell and latency rose. A thread dump shows dozens of threads RUNNABLE inside a loop that checks a flag. What is happening, and how do you fix it?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Diagnosis: busy waiting; spinning threads stay RUNNABLE and burn CPU while doing no work
  • Feedback loop: spinners take cores from the threads that would set the flags, so waits get longer and more threads spin
  • Check how the flag is declared: a plain field can spin forever in Java and is a data race in C++
  • Fix: the thread that finishes the work signals the waiter (latch, future, condition variable, blocking queue, atomic::wait)
  • Confirm: CPU drops to the real work, waiters show as WAITING or parked in the next dump, latency recovers
How a senior would say it
That is busy waiting, and it is feeding on itself. A thread that spins on a flag never leaves the CPU, so the dump shows it RUNNABLE even though it is doing no useful work. Dozens of them occupy most of the cores. The threads that would actually do the work and set those flags now queue for CPU, so each wait lasts longer, more requests pile up spinning, and throughput falls while CPU stays pinned. Before anything else I would check how the flag is declared. If it is a plain field in Java, some of those loops may never see the write at all, and in C++ it is a data race. The fix is to stop spinning: the thread that completes the work should signal the waiter, through a latch or a future for a one-time result, a condition variable for a condition, a blocking queue for hand-offs, or atomic::wait in C++20. After the change I would expect CPU to drop to the real work, the waiters to show as WAITING or parked in the next dump, and latency to recover.

Likely follow up: Suppose the wait really is only a few microseconds. Would you still block?

Out loudabout 75 s
"You are building a pipeline stage that must react within a few microseconds of new data arriving. Would you ever spin on purpose? Defend the design."
What a strong answer covers
  • Yes, as a deliberate trade of CPU for latency: a park and wake costs microseconds, which is the whole budget
  • Dedicated cores: one pinned spinner per core, never more spinners than cores, other work kept off them
  • The loop reads an atomic or volatile value with a pause hint, and never locks or blocks
  • The price: those cores run at 100 percent and burn power even when idle, and it does not scale to many waiters
  • Spin only on that hot path, measure tail latency to prove it pays, block everywhere else
How a senior would say it
Yes, but only as a deliberate design, not a habit. If the budget is a few microseconds end to end, the microseconds it takes to wake a parked thread are the whole budget, so the consumer stage spins. I would give each spinning thread its own core, pin it there, and keep other work off those cores, so there are never more spinners than cores and nothing has to preempt them. The loop reads an atomic or volatile sequence number with a pause hint, and never takes a lock or makes a blocking call. The price is explicit: those cores show 100 percent busy and burn power even when no data arrives, and the design does not scale to hundreds of waiters. So I would spin only on that hot path, measure tail latency to prove it pays, and use ordinary blocking everywhere else, including on this same path if the budget were milliseconds.

Likely follow up: What does the pause hint do in that loop, and how is it different from yield?

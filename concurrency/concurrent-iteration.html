Iterating while others write

Fail-fast iterators and ConcurrentModificationException, weakly consistent and snapshot iterators, C++ iterator invalidation, and the safe ways to iterate shared data.

Asked atApple
Before this:Concurrent hash mapsCopy-on-write and immutable snapshots

A walk through shared data can span thousands of steps, giving another thread time to change it midway. Choose the consistency guarantee before choosing the iterator: safe traversal and an exact snapshot are different promises.

In plain words

You are counting the people in a wedding hall while guests keep walking in and out. You have four choices. You can lock the doors while you count: exact, but everyone queues outside. You can take a photo and count the people in the photo: exact for that moment, and the doors stay open. You can just walk around and count, accepting that someone who slipped in behind you is missed and someone who left after you counted them is still in your total. Or you can hire a counter who shouts and gives up when they notice a door opening, but might miss it.

Those are lock during iteration, snapshot iteration, weakly consistent iteration and fail-fast iteration. Each is right somewhere. The bug is using one while believing you have another.

Three iterator contracts, and the C++ rules

Fail-fast iterators and ConcurrentModificationException

In OpenJDK 21, ArrayList and HashMap use a modification counter (modCount) to detect structural changes. Their iterators remember its value and check it in next(); a mismatch throws ConcurrentModificationException. This is an implementation mechanism, not a rule for every collection: for example, replacing an ArrayList element with set is not structural and need not trigger it. That is a fail-fast iterator.

The surprise is that a ConcurrentModificationException can involve just one thread. It is one thread removing elements inside its own for-each loop, behind the iterator's back:

The second case deserves a moment: removing the second to last element shrinks the list so that the iterator thinks it has reached the end. There is no exception, and the last element is silently skipped. In Java, remove through the iterator itself (it.remove()), use removeIf, or collect what to remove and remove it afterwards. In C++ use the iterator that erase returns. std::erase_if needs C++20; the C++17 vector fallback, with <algorithm>, is a.erase(std::remove_if(a.begin(), a.end(), pred), a.end()). These are single-threaded removal techniques, not protection against concurrent writers.

Across threads, fail-fast is only best effort. The counter is a plain field, so another thread's change may never become visible to the iterator: no exception, and the iterator walks an array or a bucket table that is being changed under it. You may see stale values, unexpected nulls or exceptions instead of a meaningful traversal. The documentation says plainly that fail-fast behavior cannot be guaranteed and should be used only to detect bugs. A ConcurrentModificationException reports an invalid modification pattern, possibly within one thread; its absence proves nothing about thread safety.

Weakly consistent iterators

Many java.util.concurrent collections give weakly consistent iterators. They support traversal alongside writers without ConcurrentModificationException, but may reflect later insertions, replacements and removals. Do not interpret this as retaining every element present at construction: one removed before it is reached may be missed. For a ConcurrentHashMap, entries that remain unchanged throughout the walk are visited once, but the entries observed need not coexist at a single moment.

That makes weakly consistent iteration useful for metrics, monitoring and diagnostics. Cleanup needs care: ConcurrentHashMap iterators support removal, but removing a key after inspecting an old value can delete a fresh replacement. For immutable, versioned values, map.remove(key, observedValue) atomically removes only a matching mapping; mutable expiry state needs its own synchronization. Weak traversal is wrong for guarantees that must hold across entries: a total of balances, an exact count, an "all or nothing" report. The striped map from the concurrent hash maps lesson has the same property when it visits its stripes one at a time.

The balance example has another boundary: two individual map updates do not make a transfer atomic. A correct transfer and an exact report must share a protocol covering both balances. Copying a concurrent map through its weak iterator does not turn that walk into a snapshot, and synchronized (map) excludes only writers that also take that monitor.

Snapshot iterators

A snapshot iterator walks a captured version of the collection. CopyOnWriteArrayList iterators retain the backing array present at iterator creation; later structural changes publish another array. Traversal needs no writer lock and never throws ConcurrentModificationException, but writing and retaining old arrays cost time and memory. Its iterator.remove() throws UnsupportedOperationException, because this iterator does not support modifying the list. Its list iterator also rejects add and set. In C++ the same thing is iterating a std::shared_ptr<const std::vector<T>> snapshot, as in the copy-on-write lesson, or a copy you made under a lock.

A structural snapshot is usually shallow. A copied Java list still refers to the same objects; a C++ vector of pointers still refers to the same pointees. To freeze their fields too, use immutable values, copy the values under the lock that guards them, or capture a versioned immutable object graph. List.copyOf makes the list unmodifiable and rejects null elements; it does not freeze mutable elements.

C++: invalidation and data races are separate rules

C++ containers have precise iterator invalidation rules for a single thread. A std::vector::push_back that reallocates invalidates every iterator, pointer and reference; an insert without reallocation or an erase invalidates iterators and references at or after that point, including the old end iterator. An insert that reallocates invalidates all of them. A std::unordered_map insert that rehashes invalidates iterators but not references. A std::map insert never invalidates anything, and an erase invalidates only the erased element.

Iterator validity does not by itself prove concurrent access is safe. A vector loop that reads end() while another thread calls push_back races on structural state even if capacity is sufficient. A conflicting unsynchronized access is a data race, which is undefined behavior; const supplies no synchronization. ThreadSanitizer can find exercised races, but cannot prove their absence or catch every invalid iterator use.

There are permitted cases: concurrent read-only access is safe after publication, and different existing elements can be modified independently, except for std::vector<bool>. Reading an element while another thread changes that same non-atomic element still races. Keep the structure stable for these cases; reserving capacity does not make concurrent push_back, size and end safe. Iterator-debug facilities are library-specific; the GCC flag -D_GLIBCXX_DEBUG does not enable checks in Apple libc++.

Five safe ways to iterate shared data

  1. Lock during the whole iteration. Exact and simple. Writers wait for the entire walk, so keep the walk short, and never call unknown code (listeners, callbacks) or do I/O while holding the lock. With Collections.synchronizedList, hold the returned wrapper's monitor throughout iteration. For a view of Collections.synchronizedMap, lock the map wrapper, not keySet() or entrySet(). Writers must use the wrapper too.
  2. Copy under the lock, iterate outside. Hold the lock only for an O(n) copy, then iterate a private, consistent copy at leisure. The most common good answer. Costs: the copy's time and memory, and the copy is a snapshot that may be stale by the time you use it.
  3. Iterate a published snapshot. Copy-on-write avoids a reader-side full copy; traversal and acquiring ownership still cost work, and old snapshots retain memory. Writers that change the array pay O(n).
  4. Accept weak consistency. Iterate a concurrent collection when the answer does not need a single moment.
  5. Do not share it. Let one owner thread hold the collection, mutate it and iterate it, and have other threads send it messages, as in single writer and actors.

Every copy is a prefix 1, 2, 3 ... k of the final log, possibly empty or complete, because the copy holds the writer's lock. The reporter performs a fixed 32 walks so the demo does not rely on lock fairness to end a polling loop. Integer elements are immutable in Java and copied by value in C++, so these snapshots freeze the values too.

Worked example: many iterators over one tree

An Apple coding round asked for a BST iterator and then pushed on concurrency: many iterators reading the same tree at once, and then what changes when there are writers.

  1. Readers only. Each iterator's state (its stack of nodes) is private to that iterator, and the tree is only read. That is safe with no locks at all, provided the tree was safely published before the readers started (built before the threads were created, or handed over through a lock or a volatile or atomic reference).
  2. Add writers, first idea: a read lock for the whole iteration. Correct, but a long or abandoned iteration blocks every writer, and with some read-write locks a stream of iterators starves writers forever.
  3. Second idea: a read lock per next(). Between two steps a writer may rotate the tree, so the node pointers on the iterator's stack can be stale. Either detect it (keep a version counter that every write bumps, check it under the lock in next(), and throw like a fail-fast iterator) or avoid it.
  4. The neat way to avoid it: for unique, immutable keys, make next() retain only a copy of the last key it returned, and find the successor of that key with a fresh search from the root under the read lock. No stale pointers can exist, keys come out strictly increasing so none repeats, and each step costs O(h), or O(log n) in a balanced tree. It is a moving view: keys inserted behind the cursor are missed, while keys inserted ahead can be seen if they remain until reached. Continuous insertion ahead can extend the walk indefinitely, so this is not a bounded-time snapshot.
  5. For exact snapshots: a persistent tree with path copying. A writer copies only the path from the root to the change and publishes a new root; each iterator walks the root it acquired, so it sees one version without holding a tree lock. Publish the root safely, keep shared nodes immutable, and retain old nodes until readers finish, using ownership or a reclamation scheme in C++.

Common mistakes

  • Removing inside a for-each loop. Use it.remove(), removeIf, or C++ std::erase_if and the iterator erase returns.
  • Treating "no exception" as "no race". Fail-fast is best effort; cross-thread modification may go unnoticed and silently corrupt the walk.
  • Iterating a synchronized wrapper without its lock. Each call on synchronizedList is locked, but the iteration as a whole is not.
  • Treating a ConcurrentHashMap walk as a snapshot. Totals and cross-entry invariants can be off; lock, snapshot, or redesign.
  • Calling listeners while holding the lock you iterate under. A slow listener blocks every writer, and one that calls back into the registry or takes another lock can deadlock. Copy, unlock, then call.
  • Returning the internal collection. Callers then iterate it with no lock. Return a copy or an immutable snapshot.
  • C++: keeping iterators or references after the lock is released. A later writer may invalidate them or race with their use; a stable address alone is not a synchronization or lifetime guarantee.

In the interview

It comes as a classic Java question ("why do I get ConcurrentModificationException with only one thread?"), as a follow up on a structure you just built ("now other threads modify it while someone iterates"), and as Apple's many-iterators-over-a-tree question. Listener notification ("make fireEvent thread-safe") is the same question in disguise.

First I would decide what the caller needs: an exact view of one moment, or just every element that was there for the whole walk. For an exact view I would copy under the lock and iterate the copy outside it, or publish immutable snapshots if reads dominate. If a moving view is fine, a weakly consistent iterator over a concurrent collection is cheaper. Either way I never call out to unknown code while holding the lock.
In C++ I check both iterator validity and conflicting memory access. For structural writers I would protect the entire traversal with the same lock or use a snapshot; sufficient vector capacity and const iterators do not protect the loop's reads of end() or size().

Follow ups: "what does a ConcurrentHashMap iterator see?", "why can removing the second to last element skip the last one?", "how does CopyOnWriteArrayList iterate?", "how would you notify listeners safely?" and "how would you iterate a tree while writers rebalance it?"

Senior depth

Removing a listener does not revoke an existing snapshot. A callback captured earlier may run after unregister returns. Promise exclusion from future snapshots, or add per-registration enabled state and active-call tracking, coordinating callback admission with removal and waiting outside the registry lock. Handle self-unregistration without waiting for the current callback to finish itself. In C++, retain ownership through each callback; copying raw pointers does not keep the listeners alive.

A collection safe for concurrent access does not make a single iterator safe to share. Give each reader its own iterator or serialize iterator access, and define whether callbacks may overlap or whether a listener exception stops delivery. Parallel streams do not upgrade a source's consistency: a weakly consistent source still has no single snapshot moment.

Key takeaways

  • Java: fail-fast (best-effort exception), weakly consistent (safe, no single moment) and snapshot (exact, possibly stale) iterators. C++: respect both iterator validity and the data-race rules; structural mutation needs a synchronization protocol.
  • ConcurrentModificationException can come from one thread modifying inside its own for-each loop.
  • Fail-fast is a bug detector, not a safety mechanism: across threads it may stay silent while the walk goes wrong.
  • Safe options: lock during iteration, copy under the lock and iterate outside, iterate a published snapshot, accept weak consistency, or give the collection one owner thread.
  • Never call listeners or other unknown code while holding the lock you iterate under.

An iterator over a sorted tree with unique, immutable keys remembers only the last key it returned, and each next() searches from the root (under a read lock) for the smallest key greater than it, returning a key by value. Why. Because each step starts from the root and asks for the successor of the last key, rotations and deletions cannot leave it holding a stale node, and the output is strictly increasing. It is weakly consistent rather than a snapshot. Throwing on change would be the fail-fast design instead.

Why. Calling unknown code while holding a lock lets that code block or wait on something that itself needs the lock, which is a deadlock, and makes the lock's hold time unbounded. Debug
A metrics reporter thread iterates a plain HashMap (C++: an unordered_map) of endpoint to request count every 10 seconds, while request threads update counts with no lock. In Java it occasionally throws ConcurrentModificationException and sometimes prints strange numbers; in C++ it has crashed twice. Explain what is happening and give two fixes.
Model answer

The map is shared mutable state with no synchronization. Request threads insert new endpoints and update counts while the reporter walks the buckets. In Java the fail-fast iterator sometimes notices a structural change (a new endpoint, a resize) and throws, but because the modification counter is not synchronized it often notices nothing and reads half-updated buckets, stale counts or lost entries; the count updates themselves are also lost updates. In C++ the concurrent insert and iteration are a data race, so the behavior is undefined, and a rehash invalidates the traversal's iterators, so a crash is one possible symptom. Rehash does not itself invalidate references to retained elements; it is not accurate to say it necessarily frees those nodes.

Fix one: make the map safe for concurrent updates and accept a moving view, a ConcurrentHashMap with merge or LongAdder values in Java, or a striped map in C++, with the reporter copying each stripe while holding that stripe's lock. This moving view is fine for approximate metrics.

Fix two: keep one lock around the map, update counts under it, and have the reporter copy the map under the lock and format the report from the copy outside it, which gives an exact snapshot at the cost of an O(n) copy every 10 seconds.

A third option for metrics is per-thread atomic counters that a live reporter sums, or private plain counters merged only after their owners finish. Thread-local writes alone do not make concurrent reads of plain counters safe.

Explain
Explain fail-fast, weakly consistent and snapshot iteration to an interviewer, with one Java collection for each, and say what C++ gives you.
Model answer

A fail-fast iterator, such as ArrayList's or HashMap's, records the collection's modification counter when it is created and throws ConcurrentModificationException if it sees the counter change. It is meant to catch bugs like removing inside a for-each loop, and across threads it is only best effort, because the counter is not synchronized, so a concurrent change may go unnoticed and corrupt the walk.

A weakly consistent iterator, such as ConcurrentHashMap's, is safe to use while other threads write: it never throws ConcurrentModificationException, sees unchanged entries once, and may or may not reflect concurrent insertions, replacements and removals, so it has no single moment and totals across entries can be off.

A snapshot iterator, such as CopyOnWriteArrayList's, walks the exact array that existed when it was created, so its captured references are stable, but later changes to mutable elements need separate synchronization. It does not support removal. C++ standard containers provide no general concurrent structural iterator contract: conflicting unsynchronized access is a data race, so you either hold one lock for both, iterate a copy taken under the lock, or iterate an immutable snapshot.

Say it out loud

Interviews are spoken. Answer each question out loud as if the interviewer just asked it. Tandem transcribes you on this Mac, word for word, measures your pace, fillers and pauses, and Bar Raiser scores what you said and how you said it.

Out loudabout 75 s
"Why can a thread-safe map give me the wrong total when I iterate it, and what would you change?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Thread-safe entry reads do not make a whole traversal an atomic snapshot
  • Read a source before a transfer and a destination after it to overcount
  • Choose weak consistency for approximate metrics and an exact snapshot for invariants
  • Capture under a lock used by every relevant writer, then process outside it
  • A copy of a concurrent map or a shallow copy of mutable values is not automatically an exact value snapshot
How a senior would say it
The map can make each access safe without making the whole report describe one moment. Suppose Alice and Bob each have a hundred. I read Alice, then a writer moves thirty from Alice to Bob, then I read Bob. I report two hundred and thirty although the intended total is two hundred. Every individual read can be valid and the report still be wrong. For approximate monitoring, I would document that moving view and keep the concurrent iterator. For an exact report, I would capture the data under a lock that every relevant writer also uses, then release it before formatting or doing I/O. The transfer itself must follow that same protocol if both balances form one invariant. Merely constructing a new map from a concurrent map does not help: the copy can collect the same mixed observations. I would also check whether the values are immutable. A shallow copy of mutable accounts freezes their references, not their balances. The contract has to say exactly which state the snapshot freezes.

Likely follow up: How would you avoid taking one global lock if the data is divided into stripes?

Out loudabout 75 s
"Design listener notification while other threads register and unregister. What does unregister returning guarantee?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Copy the registry under its lock and invoke listeners after releasing it
  • Unknown callbacks can block, re-enter or wait for a thread needing the registry lock
  • An in-flight snapshot can still invoke a removed listener after unregister returns
  • A stronger removal contract needs coordinated active-call accounting and a wait protocol
  • Keep listeners alive through the callback and synchronize their mutable state separately
How a senior would say it
I would copy the listener registry under its lock and call listeners after releasing the lock. That keeps registry updates independent of slow callbacks. It also avoids a deadlock if a callback waits for another thread that needs the registry lock. The basic unregister contract is that future snapshots exclude that registration. It does not promise that no callback occurs after unregister returns, because an earlier notification may already hold a snapshot containing it. If the caller needs that stronger guarantee, I would track each registration's enabled state and active calls. Starting a callback and disabling the registration must coordinate; unregister can then wait for active calls to finish without holding the registry lock. Self-unregistration needs a special rule so a callback never waits for itself. In Java the snapshot retains references; in C++ I would retain shared ownership or another explicit lifetime guarantee through invocation. Neither choice makes the listener's own mutable fields thread-safe. I would document removal and callback concurrency separately before implementing the stronger contract.

Likely follow up: What happens if one listener throws, or two notifications invoke the same listener concurrently?

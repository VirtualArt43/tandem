Rate limits versus concurrency limits

Distinguish request quotas from resource limits, combine them safely, and size local and adaptive admission controls without losing sight of fleet capacity.

Asked atRubrikLinkedInAtlassian
Before this:Bulkheads, timeouts, retries and circuit breakersThread-safe rate limiters

"We had a rate limiter, and we still fell over when the database got slow" describes a failure a rate limit alone cannot prevent. The reason is one line of arithmetic, and the fix is a different kind of limit.

In plain words

A car park has two ways to control its entrance. A rate limit is a barrier that lets one car in every 6 seconds. A concurrency limit is a sign that counts free spaces and closes the barrier when the car park is full. Suppose the car park has 1,000 spaces. On a normal day cars stay an hour: 600 arrivals per hour fit comfortably.

On the day of a big match, cars stay five hours. The barrier still lets in one car every 6 seconds, and the car park overflows into the street. Only the space counter notices that cars are staying longer.

Two different limits

A rate limit bounds how many requests may start per unit of time. A concurrency limit bounds how many may be in progress at once. They protect different things:

Rate limitConcurrency limit
BoundsRequests started per second (or minute)Requests in flight right now
Typical toolToken bucket, sliding window (see thread-safe rate limiters)Semaphore, pool size, connection pool
ProtectsQuotas, fairness between clients, cost per callThreads, memory, connections, the dependency's capacity
Blind spotSlow requests: how long each one takes is unlimitedFast requests: 20 in flight at 1 ms each is 20,000 per second
Typical HTTP policy429 Too Many Requests for a client quota503 for shared overload, or a short bounded wait

Concurrency is rate times duration

Little's law ties them together: mean in-flight work = admitted throughput × mean time in the measured system, in steady state. At 100 completed requests per second and a mean 50 ms each, mean concurrency is 5. At the same throughput and a mean 5 seconds each, it is 500. Blocking requests can consume workers and buffers while waiting for a connection; async requests also retain state, even without a thread each.

Use matching boundaries: database service time for active database queries, and queueing plus service time for the whole request. If there are only 50 usable connections, 500 queries cannot execute on them at once. Instead, the offered workload exceeds capacity and requests queue or are rejected; a growing, unstable queue has no steady-state mean to substitute into Little's law.

The reverse blind spot is just as real. A concurrency limit of 20 in front of a partner API that answers in 1 ms allows 20,000 calls per second, which can blow a per-minute quota or a per-call bill in seconds. Services with both quotas and scarce resources need both: rate limits for fairness, quotas and cost; concurrency limits for resources and for dependencies that can slow down.

See it: same rate, very different load

The demo targets 200 arrivals per second using a timer. Each one calls a dependency, first at 10 ms, then at 200 ms; the third run adds a concurrency limit of 10 with fail-fast. The program reports the peak number of calls in flight and compares it with rate × latency. (Sleeps stand in for the dependency and pace the arrivals.)

The offered schedule is the same in all three runs, but slower calls overlap much more. These are short runs reporting a peak; Little's law predicts a steady-state mean. Scheduler delays, JVM startup and catch-up bursts can change both the counts and timestamps, so the trace is illustrative rather than a benchmark. The concurrency cap is the invariant: no more than 10 admitted calls hold permits at once.

Under sustained load, if calls take 200 ms and 10 slots stay occupied, the ideal throughput ceiling is 10 / 0.2 = 50 per second. Of 200 offered per second, at least 150 must then wait elsewhere or be rejected. Admission overhead, unoccupied slots and scheduling can reduce useful throughput further.

Implementing a concurrency limit

The core is a semaphore: try to take a permit before the call, fail fast (or wait briefly) if none is free, and return the permit however the call ends. Put one in front of each dependency on the client side, and one at the front door of your own service. A connection pool limits checked-out connections; it bounds simultaneous queries only if each connection runs at most one query. Multiplexed protocols, parallel database execution and per-request fan-out need additional accounting. Bound both the acquisition wait and the number of waiters, using the request deadline. You will build one in the connection pool problem.

One useful admission policy checks the per-client rate first (cheap, and a 429 tells that client to slow down), then the shared concurrency limit (a 503 reports shared capacity pressure). These status codes express this API's policy, not a universal mapping; some APIs use 429 for per-client concurrency too.

The synchronous implementation below charges rate tokens for attempts that pass the rate check, including attempts later rejected by the concurrency limit. With burst 100 and refill 50 per second, it allows up to approximately 100 + 50 × t accepted rate checks over t seconds; it does not enforce a strict sliding-window quota of 50 calls every second. All clients have the same example policy.

Two details carry the correctness. The permit is released only on the path that acquired it (the finally comes after a successful tryAcquire; the C++ guard is created only then), because semaphores have no owner. Java accepts unmatched releases until its integer permit count overflows; C++ can increase above the intended initial count while within max(), and exceeding max() violates its precondition. Neither API enforces this application invariant for you.

Each client's bucket is created once and shared, through computeIfAbsent in Java and a locked map of heap-allocated buckets in C++. The controller must outlive all handlers. Its C++ references remain valid because buckets are never erased, but this means unbounded growth in the number of client IDs. Production needs bounded identity cardinality and coordinated idle eviction: naive removal can create two live buckets for one client, grant a fresh burst early, or leave a dangling C++ reference.

C++

std::counting_semaphore is C++20. Its try_acquire() may fail spuriously, so the example permits conservative rejection and its displayed successful admissions are typical rather than guaranteed. C++17 can use a mutex-protected count with a condition variable for timed admission. A noncopyable RAII permit guard avoids accidental double release.

Java

The first demo uses Java 21 virtual threads. They reduce the cost of waiting, but the semaphore still protects the dependency. These samples use nonfair admission: even a fair Java Semaphore allows barging through untimed tryAcquire(); a tenant fairness policy requires more than that flag.

Adaptive concurrency limits

A fixed limit should start from capacity tests, but the best value can change with traffic and service time. An adaptive concurrency limit adjusts it from feedback. Latency-based controllers compare recent response time with a periodically refreshed low-load baseline. A sustained increase suggests queueing, so they reduce admission; healthy, sufficiently loaded intervals allow cautious increases. Netflix's concurrency-limits library and Envoy offer variants of this idea.

Latency is a signal, not proof of queueing. A slower query mix, network delay or a removed index can change service time itself. Smooth measurements, refresh the baseline, set minimum and maximum limits, and do not increase just because a lightly used service is fast. A different algorithm, AIMD, increases additively under suitable success/load conditions and decreases multiplicatively on drops or timeouts. Do not describe all adaptive algorithms as the same rule.

Admission control

Admission control is the decision, at the front door, to take a request or turn it away, made before any expensive work. The signals can be in-flight count (a concurrency limit), time spent queued, CPU, or the request's priority, and the decision can be per tenant so one customer's burst cannot starve the others. The Google SRE book adds a client-side version, adaptive throttling: each client tracks, over the last couple of minutes, how many requests the application attempted, including local rejections, and how many the backend accepted. It rejects locally with probability max(0, (requests - K × accepts) / (requests + 1)), with K typically 2. When the backend rejects most requests, clients send fewer of them, reducing the cost of rejections at the server. Some requests still pass through so clients can detect recovery.

Worked example: the rate limit held, the database fell over

An API is rate limited to 2,000 requests per second in total. Each request runs one database query, normally 10 ms, so about 2,000 × 0.01 = 20 queries are in flight; the connection pool has 100 connections and the request pool 400 threads. A bad deploy drops an index and queries take 500 ms.

What happens. Sustaining 2,000 queries per second at 500 ms would require 1,000 simultaneous queries, but only 100 can hold connections. Assuming each query still takes 500 ms at that concurrency, the ideal completion ceiling is 100 / 0.5 = 200 per second. The backlog grows initially at roughly 1,800 per second. A 400-worker request pool can become fully occupied in a fraction of a second, with roughly 100 workers doing queries and 300 waiting; health checks sharing that pool can be delayed.

With concurrency limits. Use an admission cap of 150 before allocating request workers and a connection acquisition timeout of at most 20 ms, clipped to the request deadline. In this simplified model, useful throughput is at most 200 per second; most of the remaining 1,800 requests per second are rejected or degraded promptly. Reserve health-check capacity and measure rejection overhead. Admission bounds the damage; it does not repair the index or guarantee the full 200 per second. An adaptive controller may reduce the cap further as latency rises.

One more trap. If 10 instances each allow 100 database connections, the database sees up to 1,000. Autoscaling to 20 instances during the incident doubles that to 2,000 and makes things worse. The limit a dependency feels is the per-instance limit times the number of instances; size it from the dependency's capacity, and revisit it when the fleet grows.

Common mistakes

  • Relying on a rate limit to protect resources. It bounds starts, not in-flight work; slow requests pile up underneath it.
  • Relying on a concurrency limit to enforce a quota. Fast requests can blow through a per-minute budget.
  • Long waits for a permit or a connection. A 30 second acquire timeout turns the limit back into a queue.
  • Releasing a permit that was never acquired, for example in a finally that also runs after tryAcquire failed.
  • Per-instance limits that ignore the fleet size, so autoscaling multiplies the load on the dependency.
  • Treating virtual threads or async code as a replacement for a concurrency limit: cheap waiting does not make the database bigger.

In the interview

Rate limiters are a staple: Rubrik asked for one where each accepted request holds one of a fixed number of slots until an expiry time (a neat mix of the two ideas), LinkedIn staff candidates built thread-safe sliding window limiters and walked through the race where two threads both see 99 of 100, and Atlassian's code design rounds use them with "how would you handle this in a multithreaded environment" follow ups. The senior extension is knowing what a rate limiter does not protect.

A rate limit bounds how many requests start per second; it says nothing about how long they last. For stable matching boundaries, mean in-flight work is throughput times mean latency. If a dependency slows down, sustaining the same throughput needs more active work; without that capacity, requests queue or fail. So I would put rate limits at the edge for fairness and quotas, returning 429, and concurrency limits in front of each dependency and at the front door for resources, failing fast with a 503. Ideally the concurrency limits adapt to observed latency, and I would remember that a per-instance limit times the fleet size is what the dependency actually sees.

Follow ups: "What is the maximum throughput with a limit of 50 and 100 ms calls?" (an ideal ceiling of 500 per second). "Which one would have saved you in the slow database incident?" (the concurrency limit). "How would you make the limit adaptive?" (compare smoothed latency to a refreshed baseline, or choose a loss-based AIMD controller; explain which feedback drives your policy).

Senior depth

Queueing and backpressure are different. A short bounded queue can smooth bursts, but it becomes backpressure only when a full queue makes producers slow down or blocks their submissions. Immediate rejection is shedding; clients may use that signal to reduce offered load. Bound queue length and queue time from the request's latency budget, as in backpressure and load shedding.

Distributed versus local. A global API-key quota needs a shared atomic decision or explicitly allocated token budgets; independent full-rate buckets multiply the allowance. Divide a known dependency concurrency budget among callers, or coordinate global admission when a strict aggregate cap is required. Local adaptive controllers can improve utilisation, but do not guarantee a fair share or a hard fleet-wide ceiling, especially during autoscaling or uneven traffic.

Lifetime and fan-out. The sample's callback is synchronous: returning means its resource use has ended. If it launches async work, transfer the permit to an exactly-once terminal callback instead. Every parallel query, retry and hedge must acquire its own dependency permit. If a caller cancels while remote work continues, keep that work counted; otherwise the limit becomes fictional.

Fairness and changing limits. A shared cap does not reserve slots for small tenants; add tenant caps or a fair bounded admission queue when required. Taking tenant and global permits must use a consistent order and release the first if the second fails. To lower a live cap, let existing calls drain and reject new ones until occupancy is below the target; do not manufacture permits or forcibly kill holders. Track offered, admitted and rejected rates separately, plus queue time, active work, latency and cancellation lag.

Key takeaways

  • A rate limit bounds starts per second; a concurrency limit bounds work in progress. They protect different things.
  • For stable matching boundaries, mean in-flight work = throughput × mean latency; overloaded demand instead creates queues or rejections.
  • Use rate limits for quotas, fairness and cost (429); concurrency limits for threads, connections and dependencies (503, fail fast).
  • A concurrency limit L with mean permit-holding time W has an ideal throughput ceiling L / W; bound or reject the excess.
  • Adaptive limits respond to measured signals, not perfect knowledge; per-instance limits add up across the fleet.

Quick check

Answer each question, then read why. Your score is saved for your module test.

A service is rate limited to 100 requests per second. Each request calls a dependency that normally takes 50 ms, and one day takes 5 s. Assume it still completes 100 per second in steady state, with no other bottleneck. What is the mean number in flight after the slowdown?
Why. Little's law uses matching steady-state means: in flight = throughput × duration = 100 × 5 = 500, up from 100 × 0.05 = 5. The rate limit bounds starts, not duration, so it cannot see this. That is the gap a concurrency limit fills.
A concurrency limit of 20 sits in front of a dependency whose calls take 2 ms. With all slots continuously occupied and no other bottleneck, what is the ideal sustained throughput ceiling?
Why. Throughput = in flight / duration = 20 / 0.002 = 10,000 per second. A concurrency limit does not cap fast requests at a low rate, which is why quotas need a rate limit.
A partner API allows at most 6,000 calls in every rolling 60-second window per customer and bills per call. Which control enforces that agreement?
Why. The agreement specifies an exact rolling window, so enforce that window atomically. A token bucket refilling at 100 per second also permits its initial burst and is not automatically equivalent. A concurrency limit or a pool does not impose this window quota when calls are fast; a breaker reacts to failures, not to volume.
Ten instances each allow up to 40 concurrent queries to a database that handles 400 well. Autoscaling adds 10 more instances during a traffic spike. What is the risk?
Why. Per-instance limits add up. The dependency feels the per-instance limit times the instance count, so scaling the fleet raises the load it can receive. Size per-instance limits from the dependency's capacity and fleet size, or coordinate admission to maintain the aggregate budget. Adaptation alone does not guarantee a hard fleet-wide bound.
A latency-based adaptive concurrency limiter sees recent call latency stay well above its low-load baseline. What is a reasonable response, assuming the request mix and baseline remain comparable?
Why. A latency-based controller treats a sustained rise as a congestion signal and reduces admission. Raising or resetting the limit upward could worsen the queue; ignoring latency would discard this controller's signal. The inference is not proof: changed service time or request mix can raise latency too, so smooth samples, refresh baselines and use bounds.
In the admission-control demo, the cap is 1 and both clients have rate tokens. Assume the outer call acquires its permit. Its synchronous callback invokes handle again before returning, printing the inner verdict, then the caller prints the outer verdict. What can this print?
Why. The outer callback still holds the only permit. The inner client has a rate token but cannot acquire a concurrency permit, so it gets OVERLOADED without executing its callback. The inner call returns promptly; the outer callback then finishes, releases its permit and returns OK. A different client ID does not create another global permit, and try-acquire does not wait.
Both snippets have a shared semaphore limit. Assume acquisition fails. What is wrong with their permit accounting?
Why. The finally block and guard run after failed acquisition too. Construct the guard, or enter the try/finally, only after acquiring successfully. Semaphores have no thread ownership: Java adds the unmatched permit, while C++ can inflate the intended count or violate the maximum-count precondition. Try-acquire returns failure without waiting; automatic cleanup is correct only when it represents a resource actually acquired.

Checkpoint

Write your own answer first. Then compare with the model answer, or ask Bar Raiser to mark it like an interviewer would.

Numbers
An endpoint receives 3,000 requests per second. Each calls a search backend that normally answers in a mean 40 ms; the backend team says it degrades badly beyond 200 concurrent queries across all callers. Your service runs on 6 equally loaded instances and has an allocated budget of 180 concurrent queries out of that 200. (a) How many queries are normally in flight? (b) What per-instance concurrency limit would you set? (c) If mean query duration rises to 400 ms, what throughput do you get, and what happens to the rest?
Model answer

(a) 3,000 × 0.04 = 120 queries in flight across the fleet, 20 per instance.

(b) Our allocated budget is 180, so I would set 180 / 6 = 30 per instance, leaving the other 20 reserved for other callers, with a fail-fast acquire (or a wait of a few milliseconds). That gives normal traffic 50 percent headroom (120 in flight against 180 allowed). If we autoscale, reallocate the per-instance budgets or coordinate admissions so their sum stays at most 180. Adaptation alone cannot guarantee that ceiling.

(c) At 400 ms, the fleet can sustain at most 180 / 0.4 = 450 queries per second. At least 2,550 of the offered requests per second cannot be served under those assumptions; reject them promptly or give a degraded response (cached results, or a smaller result set from a fallback) rather than an error page. Without the limit, demand would be 3,000 × 0.4 = 1,200 queries in flight, six times what the backend tolerates, which would slow it further and take our threads with it.

Design
Design admission control for a multi-tenant API where one large tenant sometimes sends bursts ten times its normal volume, and the shared database is the bottleneck.
Model answer

Per-tenant rate limits at the edge, a token bucket per tenant sized from its plan (rate plus burst), returning 429 with Retry-After when exceeded. That bounds the tenant's offered rate and burst; choose a sliding window instead if the contract requires a strict rolling-window maximum. Slow requests still need a concurrency cap.

A concurrency limit per tenant and a global one in front of the database work. Allocate the database's tested capacity across all callers and divide our share across instances; use coordinated admission for a strict global bound. Each tenant may use at most a fraction of the local share, for example 30 percent, so even within its rate limit a heavy tenant cannot occupy all database slots with slow queries. When a tenant's share is full, its excess requests get a 503 quickly while other tenants proceed.

Priorities. Interactive requests are admitted before batch or export requests, which go to a separate pool with a smaller share (a bulkhead), and are the first to be shed under pressure.

Signals and recovery. I would watch per-tenant rejection rates, in-flight counts against limits, and database latency. Clients should use backoff with jitter, and our own clients can use adaptive throttling, rejecting locally when most of their recent requests were rejected. This way a burst costs the bursting tenant some 429s and 503s, while limiting its impact on other tenants. Shared CPU, cache and database contention still need measurement.

Say it out loud

Interviews are spoken. Answer each question out loud as if the interviewer just asked it. Tandem transcribes you on this Mac, word for word, measures your pace, fillers and pauses, and Bar Raiser scores what you said and how you said it.

Out loudabout 75 s
"We already allow only a hundred requests a second. Why would you add a concurrency limit as well?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Rate bounds starts per time while concurrency bounds simultaneous resource use
  • Compute mean occupancy of 5 at 50 ms and 500 at 5 seconds for 100 per second
  • State that Little's law uses matching stable means and overload can instead create queues
  • Show the reverse blind spot: a concurrency cap cannot enforce a strict time-window quota
  • Combine bounded admission, quota semantics and fleet-aware sizing
How a senior would say it
I would add it because the rate limit does not bound how long requests occupy resources. At a hundred requests per second and a mean fifty milliseconds, average occupancy is five. If duration becomes five seconds at the same completed throughput, average occupancy becomes five hundred. Little's law uses stable means at the same boundary; if the connection pool cannot support that throughput, requests queue or get rejected instead. A concurrency limit caps active work even when latency rises. It has the opposite blind spot, though: twenty slots with one millisecond calls can theoretically serve twenty thousand calls per second. That can exceed a partner quota or budget. I therefore use a rate policy matching the client's contract, including whether bursts are allowed, and a concurrency cap matching resource capacity. Admission either rejects promptly or waits in a small bounded queue within the deadline. I also sum limits across the fleet. Forty slots on each of twenty instances means up to eight hundred calls to the dependency, regardless of each instance looking healthy.

Likely follow up: Does a token bucket refilling at a hundred per second enforce exactly six thousand calls in every rolling minute?

Out loudabout 90 s
"Design admission control for a multi-tenant service with async calls to a shared database. What are the correctness traps?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Define separate tenant rate, tenant concurrency and shared dependency budgets
  • Atomically refill and consume each tenant bucket and explain rejected-attempt charging
  • Acquire permits before resource use and unwind partial acquisition on rejection
  • Keep async permits until resource use ends, including cancellation and fan-out
  • Coordinate bucket eviction and fleet budgets so they cannot duplicate capacity
  • Measure outcomes and treat adaptive feedback as a heuristic within hard bounds
How a senior would say it
I would enforce separate budgets for tenant traffic and database capacity. Each tenant gets a rate and burst allowance, plus a concurrency cap so slow queries cannot occupy all shared slots. The database also gets a shared concurrency budget allocated across every caller and instance. For each tenant, refilling and consuming a token must be one atomic operation. I would document whether requests rejected later still spend that token; charging attempts is a reasonable policy, but it differs from billing completed work. Then I acquire tenant and database permits in a consistent order, releasing the first if the second fails. Because the work is async, returning from the submission method cannot release those permits. An exactly-once completion path releases them when the protected resource is actually free. Cancellation of the caller's future is not enough, and each parallel query or hedge needs its own slot. I would also bound client-state memory. Naive bucket eviction can leave an old bucket in use while a new full bucket admits more requests for the same tenant. Finally, I monitor admissions, rejections, queue time, active queries and cancellation lag. Adaptive tuning can help, but I keep hard bounds and do not assume local controllers guarantee a fair global share.

Likely follow up: How would you lower the live concurrency limit below the number of requests already running?

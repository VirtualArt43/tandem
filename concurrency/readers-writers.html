Readers and writers, three ways

Reader preference, writer preference and fair versions, who starves under each, and the searchers, inserters and deleters problem Rubrik asks.

Asked atRubrikGoogleMicrosoftApple
Before this:Read-write locks and StampedLockCondition variables and monitorsSemaphores: permits as a resource limit

Using a read-write lock is easy. Building one is a recurring Rubrik question, and the interesting part is not who gets in. It is who can be kept waiting forever, and whether you chose that on purpose.

In plain words

A housing society has one notice board in the lobby. Any number of residents can read it at the same time. When the secretary needs to change a notice, everyone has to step away until the new sheet is pinned up, or someone may read half of the old notice and half of the new one.

Now the questions get interesting. If residents keep drifting in to read, one after another, does the secretary ever get a turn? If the secretary gets priority, how long do readers wait while a stack of notices is replaced? And can you be fair to both? Every readers-writers design is an answer to "who waits when both are waiting", and the policy determines whose progress you can guarantee.

The rules, and the one invariant

The readers-writers problem problem has two kinds of threads sharing one resource. Readers only look, so any number of them may be inside together. A writer changes the resource, so it needs it alone: no readers and no other writer. Written as an invariant over two counters that one lock guards:

writers is 0 or 1, and if writers is 1 then readers is 0.

A read-write lock enforces exactly this, and every correct version agrees on it. Versions differ only in the policy for the moment when readers are inside, a writer is waiting, and another reader arrives. That one decision sorts all designs into three families.

Policy 1: reader preference

The first answer, reader preference, lets the new reader in. The extra reader does not conflict with current readers, but it can prolong the writer's wait. The classic construction is the lightswitch: the first reader into the room locks it against writers, and the last reader out unlocks it, like the first person into a dark room switching on the light and the last one out switching it off.

Look at who releases roomEmpty. The first reader acquired it, but the last reader out releases it, and that is usually a different thread. That is why the room must be a semaphore, not a mutex: a ReentrantLock throws IllegalMonitorStateException when a non-owner unlocks it, and unlocking a std::mutex from a thread that does not own it is undefined behavior.

C++

The C++ versions in this lesson use the standard names lock_shared, unlock_shared, lock and unlock. That makes your class usable with std::shared_lock from <shared_mutex> and std::unique_lock from <mutex>, so callers get RAII: std::shared_lock<ReaderPreferenceLock> guard(rw);.

The price of reader preference is the writer. As long as readers overlap, the reader count never returns to zero, and the writer never gets the room:

Reader preference is fine when writes are rare and can wait, and when maximum read throughput matters more than how stale the data gets. It is wrong when a write must land within a bounded time, such as a configuration change or an access revocation.

Policy 2: writer preference

Writer preference flips the decision: once a writer is waiting, new readers wait too. Readers already inside finish, the count drains to zero, and the writer goes in. This needs one more counter, waitingWriters, and it is a useful first version to propose when asked to implement a reader-writer lock. It is a monitor: one lock, two conditions (readers wait for one thing, writers for another), and each acquisition checks its condition in a loop.

The output is illustrative: the maximum simultaneous reader count is scheduler-dependent and may be 1 to 4. This stress run checks the final values and torn reads; it does not prove fairness or guarantee reader overlap in every run.

Three lines carry the policy. Readers wait while writing || waitingWriters > 0; drop the second half and you have silently built reader preference again. unlockWrite wakes a writer if one is queued; otherwise it wakes every reader. A signal makes a waiter eligible, but does not hand it ownership. And the last reader out signals one writer, because only one writer can go in.

Java

Look at the inner finally in lockWrite. If a waiting writer is interrupted, it must take itself out of waitingWriters, and if it was the only waiting writer, the readers it was holding back must be woken. Bookkeeping that is not undone on the exception path is a classic way for a lock to hang a few hours after deployment.

Writer preference moves the starvation, it does not remove it: under a steady stream of writers, readers can wait forever. Choose this trade only when the workload and latency requirements justify it. The monitor does not order competing writers, so one writer can also be overtaken repeatedly by other writers; it prevents starvation caused by an unending stream of new readers, not every form of starvation.

Policy 3: fairness for both sides

Fairness limits overtaking, not elapsed time. Progress still assumes that holders finish, eligible threads are scheduled, and the internal mutex eventually admits them. There are two common shapes.

  • Ticket order. A thread takes a ticket under the monitor mutex, like a bank token; that assignment defines its place in line. A thread may enter only when its ticket is the one being served (and the room allows it); when it enters, the next ticket is served. A run of readers at the head of the line enters together, because each one that enters immediately makes the next one eligible. A writer at the head waits for the readers inside to drain, and readers behind it wait behind it. This is the same idea as a ticket lock, extended with reader sharing, and the full code is in the checkpoint below.
  • Phases. Alternate between "every reader waiting right now" and "one writer". When a writer finishes, all readers who were waiting at that moment go in together; readers who arrive later wait for the next reader phase if a writer is queued. A phase cutoff stops later readers extending the current batch forever, and writers need their own FIFO order. A writer can wait through several phases behind earlier writers; there is no universal one-phase or wall-clock bound.

The textbook semaphore version (from Allen Downey's Little Book of Semaphores) adds a turnstile that writers hold while they wait, so new readers queue behind a waiting writer. It is only as fair as the semaphore underneath: Java's new Semaphore(1, true) orders blocking acquisitions at internal queueing points, while untimed tryAcquire() may barge, but C++ std::counting_semaphore makes no ordering promise, so in C++ use the ticket monitor when fairness is a requirement.

The upgrade row deserves a sentence of its own. With Java's blocking lock(), a thread holding only a read lock waits for itself; untimed tryLock() instead fails. In C++, acquiring a std::shared_mutex you already own in either mode is undefined behaviour, not a portable deadlock demonstration. That is the lock upgrade and downgrade trap. If you might need to write, take the write lock from the start, or release the read lock, take the write lock, and re-check what you read.

Java's fair mode is approximate arrival order, and untimed tryLock() may barge even in fair mode. Its write lock supports newCondition(); the read lock does not. See the Java 21 contract and the C++ shared mutex requirements.

The contract of these teaching locks

The custom locks are non-reentrant: take one mode once, and release it exactly once on the same thread. They do not track owners, reject misuse, support downgrade, or expose timed acquisition. A nested read can deadlock when a writer is queued, even though an uncontended nested read might appear to work. Keep the lock alive until all users finish.

The monitor publishes the resource as well as its counters. A writer changes the payload before taking the monitor mutex in unlockWrite; its mutex release happens before a later reader's mutex acquisition in lockRead. The reader's payload access follows that acquisition. Similarly, each reader's payload accesses happen before a later admitted writer's changes. The counters enforce exclusion; the mutex establishes the memory ordering. Readers must still avoid modifying shared payload under a shared lock.

The counters change under one mutex: admitting a reader increments the active count, and admitting a writer sets the writing flag. These are the acquisition linearization points. C++ callers use guards; Java callers put releases in finally. The semaphore example needs C++20; the monitor versions work in C++17, including their RAII call sites.

Searchers, inserters and deleters

Rubrik has asked this one, the search-insert-delete problem, in 2026 reports, set on a shared singly linked list. It comes from Allen Downey's Little Book of Semaphores. Three kinds of threads share the list:

May run together?SearcherInserterDeleter
SearcherYesYesNo
InserterYesNo (one inserter at a time)No
DeleterNoNoNo

Read the table as a read-write lock with a twist. Searchers and inserters are both "readers" with respect to deleters, and inserters also exclude each other. So the five line answer is: searchers take the read lock; inserters take the read lock and an insert mutex; deleters take the write lock. Writer preference prevents new searchers and inserters from continually overtaking waiting deleters. Fair ordering is additionally needed to prevent deleters from overtaking one another indefinitely.

When the interviewer bans library read-write locks, build the same rules as one monitor. Count the searchers, flag the inserter and the deleter, and count the waiting deleters so that new searchers and inserters hold back while a deleter waits. That counter gives deleters priority, exactly like waitingWriters above; it does not order deleters or prevent searcher and inserter starvation.

There is one more trap when implementing the list itself. Searchers run while an insert is in progress, so the list itself must tolerate a reader walking past a half finished insert. The rule: build the new node completely, then link it in with one atomic write, and make the link field something that publishes safely: volatile in Java, std::atomic with release and acquire in C++. A deleter runs alone, so it may unlink and reclaim nodes while holding deletion permission, provided no pointer or iterator escapes a protected operation. It must repair tail when removing the last node; the fragments below show publication, not deletion.

Worked example: Rubrik's client wrapper

A reported Rubrik phone screen gives you a Client with init(), request() and close(), and asks for a thread-safe wrapper: init is exclusive and blocks requests and close; requests run concurrently once init has succeeded; close waits for in-flight requests to finish, runs only once, and never overlaps init or a request.

Read the requirements as a read-write lock and the design falls out. Requests are readers: many at once, and the read lock doubles as a count of requests in flight. Init and close are writers: alone, and the write lock waits for every in-flight request to leave, which is exactly "close waits for in-flight requests". A state field under the same lock gives "only once" and "requests fail after close". A fair lock stops a stream of requests from starving close.

Holding the read lock across client.request(...) is deliberate here, although calling slow code under a lock is usually a smell: the lock is how close knows a request is still running. Say that trade-off out loud. The client must support concurrent requests, outlive the wrapper in C++, and never call back into wrapper lifecycle methods while holding a request lock.

This version retries failed init, rejects requests before successful init, and makes close idempotent; a throwing close is attempted at most once. It does not promise immediate rejection from the instant a close caller arrives. For that stronger contract, use a monitor with a CLOSING state: set it before waiting for an active-request count to reach zero, reject new requests, perform close outside the monitor, then publish CLOSED and wake concurrent close callers. Failed init also needs an explicit cleanup or retry contract from the wrapped client.

Common mistakes

  • A lightswitch built on a mutex. The last reader out releases the room, and it is usually not the thread that locked it. Use a semaphore, or write the whole thing as a monitor with counters.
  • Updating the reader count without a lock. readers += 1 is a read-modify-write; concurrent updates can lose a reader or miss the last-reader transition, breaking exclusion or stranding waiters.
  • Writer preference that forgets the waiting writers. If readers only wait while a writer is active, you built reader preference by accident.
  • notify with mixed waiters on one condition. Wake a reader when only a writer can proceed and the signal is wasted. Use separate conditions, or notifyAll.
  • Upgrading a read lock. It deadlocks in Java and has no direct form in C++. Take the write lock up front, or release, re-acquire and re-check.
  • Assuming a read-write lock is faster. Every reader still writes the shared reader count, so for very short critical sections a plain mutex can win. It pays off when reads are long, frequent and concurrent, and writes are rare.
  • A read lock around an LRU get. An LRU lookup moves the entry to the front of the recency list, so it is a write. Read locks do not help there.

In the interview

This family is among the most reported at Rubrik: "implement a reader-writer lock" in a 2026 system coding round (the candidate was asked to "handle various cases" and got a redo after it went poorly), searchers, inserters and deleters on a linked list in 2026 rounds (one candidate's semaphore and ReentrantLock answer was rejected, and commenters pointed to races and suggested a read-write lock plus an insert mutex), and the client wrapper above in a 2022 phone screen. Elsewhere it arrives as a follow up: at Google, "one thread changes flight prices while many threads read them"; at Microsoft, a candidate wrote their own reader-writer class to make a key-value store thread-safe; an Apple Media Streaming report describes implementing a read/write mutex.

The interviewer is checking whether you know that a policy exists and that you picked one. Useful cases to raise are: say the policy, the starvation it allows, and what happens on upgrade, reentrancy, interrupts and an unlock by the wrong thread.

I will build a writer-preferring read-write lock as a monitor: one mutex, a reader count, a writing flag and a count of waiting writers. Readers wait while a writer is writing or waiting, so new readers cannot keep overtaking a waiting writer; the trade-off is that a steady stream of writers can starve readers. If both sides need bounded waiting, I would switch to ticket order, where assigned tickets are served in order and consecutive readers still share. Upgrading from read to write is not supported, because it deadlocks.

Follow ups to expect: "Who can starve in your version?" "Make it fair." "Can a reader upgrade?" "Is your lock reentrant?" "What if the thread holding the write lock dies?" (Java ReentrantReadWriteLock is not automatically released on thread death, so use finally; C++ RAII releases during normal scope exit and exception unwinding, but not after process termination). "When is a plain mutex better?"

Senior depth

The reader count is itself a hot spot. Every reader increments and decrements one shared counter, so with many cores the cache line holding it bounces between them, and a read-write lock over a tiny critical section can be slower than a mutex. Two escapes exist. One is to stop locking readers: copy-on-write snapshots, where readers grab an immutable version and writers swap in a new one (see copy-on-write and snapshots). The other is optimistic reading, where readers read without locking and check a version number afterwards, retrying if a writer intervened; that is the idea behind a seqlock and Java's StampedLock. In C++, retrying after racing on ordinary payload fields is still undefined behaviour; an optimistic protocol needs legal atomic accesses and safe object lifetimes (see read-write locks).

Fairness has a throughput price you can name. Strict ticket order makes readers that could have shared the room wait behind a writer that is itself waiting for earlier readers. Phase-fair designs admit batches of readers and order waiting writers. A cutoff prevents later arrivals from prolonging an older phase forever, but several earlier writers still imply several phases of waiting.

Read-mostly data often wants no lock at all. When a senior candidate is asked to make a configuration map thread-safe, "an immutable map behind a volatile or atomic reference, replaced on each update" is worth benchmarking against a read-write lock. Java readers can load the reference without updating a reader counter; C++ shared ownership may update reference counts and need internal locks, and a raw atomic pointer alone does not solve reclamation.

Key takeaways

  • All versions share one invariant: either one writer or any number of readers. They differ in who waits when both are waiting.
  • Reader preference can starve writers; writer preference can starve readers and does not order writers. Fairness claims need finite critical sections and scheduling assumptions.
  • The lightswitch's room must be a semaphore, because the last reader out is usually not the one who locked it.
  • Searchers, inserters and deleters is a read-write lock plus an insert mutex, and the list must publish each new node with one atomic write.
  • These locks do not support read-to-write upgrade, and a read-write lock is not automatically faster than a mutex.

Quick check

Answer each question, then read why. Your score is saved for your module test.

A reader-preference lock is in use. Readers arrive so often that at least one is always inside. A writer calls lockWrite(). What happens?
Why. Under reader preference, new readers join while any reader is inside, so overlapping readers can keep the room busy indefinitely. Holding back new readers while a writer waits is exactly what writer preference adds.
In the lightswitch, why is the room lock a semaphore rather than a ReentrantLock or std::mutex?
Why. A mutex has an owner and must be released by that owner: Java throws IllegalMonitorStateException and C++ has undefined behavior otherwise. A semaphore allows a different thread to release a permit, which is what the lightswitch needs. Neither primitive is inherently faster or fair, and holding one mutex while acquiring another is permitted when the lock ordering is safe.
Java onlyA thread holds only rwl.readLock() of a ReentrantReadWriteLock and calls rwl.writeLock().lock(). What happens?
Why. ReentrantReadWriteLock supports downgrading (write, then read, then release write) but not upgrading. A writer may also acquire a read lock, but a reader holding only read mode cannot acquire write mode. The blocking call does not throw an ownership exception.
C++ onlyA thread holds a std::shared_lock on a std::shared_mutex and then calls lock() on the same mutex. What does the standard say?
Why. std::shared_mutex is not reentrant and has no upgrade operation. Locking this mutex while already owning either mode is undefined behavior; neither a successful upgrade nor a diagnostic is guaranteed. lock() returns void, so returning false is not an option. Take exclusive ownership from the start if you might write.
In the searchers, inserters and deleters problem, which pairs may run at the same time? Select all that apply.Select all that apply
Why. Searchers share with each other and with one inserter; inserters exclude each other; a deleter excludes everyone. Hence searchers and inserters take a read lock, inserters also take an insert mutex, and deleters take the write lock.
A lock serves threads strictly in ticket order, and consecutive readers at the head of the line enter together. Readers R1 and R2 are reading a value initially 0. W1 takes its ticket before R3, writes 1 under the write lock, and unlocks. R3 reads and prints under its read lock. What can R3 print?
Why. R3 is behind W1 in the line, so it may not jump ahead to share with R1 and R2, and must observe W1's write through lock synchronization. Either order for R1 and R2 leaving still leads to 1; assuming holders finish, there is no wait cycle.
Spot the policy. A monitor protects all state and records each admission before unlocking. Readers wait with while (writing) await(); and writers with while (writing || readers > 0) await();. Which statement is true?
Why. Readers only check for an active writer, never for a waiting one, so they keep entering while a writer waits for the count to reach zero. Writer preference needs a waitingWriters counter in the reader's wait condition. There is no queue order for fairness, but the protected writing flag still excludes simultaneous writers.

Checkpoint

Write your own answer first. Then compare with the model answer, or ask Bar Raiser to mark it like an interviewer would.

Write code
Write a read-write lock in which neither readers nor writers can starve: assigned tickets are served in order, and consecutive readers may share. State the progress assumptions.
Model answer

Why it works: a thread may enter only when its ticket is being served, so admission follows ticket assignment order, not the time the method was first called. With eventual scheduling and mutex admission, finite critical sections, and no abandoned ticket, no assigned ticket can be overtaken forever. A reader that enters advances the line at once, so a run of readers at the head enters together; a writer at the head waits for those readers to drain, and everyone behind it waits for it. The Java version waits uninterruptibly on purpose: a thread that left the line with its ticket would block everyone behind it. To support interrupts, record abandoned tickets and skip them when advancing nowServing. If the cancelled ticket is already at the head, advance immediately under the mutex and wake successors. Java's uninterruptible wait preserves the interrupt flag for the caller to handle later. The single condition with notify all is simple but wakes everyone on each change; a queue with targeted handoff can reduce that herd. Ticket counters may wrap only if no outstanding ticket is reused; Java long and C++ uint64_t have defined wraparound here. These samples exclude synchronization-library failures after issuing a ticket, since recovering from one requires cancellation bookkeeping.

Design
A Rubrik interviewer says "implement a reader-writer lock" and, when you ask for details, only says "handle the various cases". List the cases you would raise, and say what your lock does for each.
Model answer

I would state each case and my decision:

  • Policy and starvation: writer preference by default, so a waiting writer holds back new readers; this does not order competing writers; I would mention that readers can starve under a stream of writers, and offer ticket order if both sides need bounded waits.
  • Upgrade: not supported, because a reader asking for the write lock waits for itself; callers that may write take the write lock up front.
  • Downgrade: not supported by the custom code above. An explicit downgrade operation would atomically replace the writer flag with one active reader under the monitor mutex; calling lockRead() while writing would self-deadlock. Java's library supports the acquire-read-then-release-write sequence.
  • Reentrancy: not supported in my simple version; I would need a writer owner and hold count plus per-thread read hold counts if callers need it. Existing readers must be allowed to reenter despite queued writers, or they can wait for themselves.
  • Unlock by the wrong thread or unlock without lock: the examples require correct callers and do not detect misuse. To detect it, track writer identity and per-thread read ownership, then reject unmatched releases before changing counters.
  • Interrupts and timeouts: waits are interruptible in Java, and an interrupted writer removes itself from the waiting count and wakes readers it was holding back; timed acquisition is an extension, using awaitNanos or a C++ steady_clock deadline with the same cleanup on timeout. Repeated wakeups must not restart the timeout budget.
  • Exceptions in the critical section: unlock in finally (Java) or with RAII guards (C++), otherwise one exception holds the lock forever.
Debug
A candidate's searchers, inserters and deleters code has searchers take the read lock, inserters take only an insert mutex, and deleters take the write lock. Which rule is broken? Give an interleaving and the fix.
Model answer

Inserters no longer exclude deleters. The write lock only waits for holders of the read lock, and an inserter holds none, so: inserter I takes the insert mutex and starts linking a new node after node X; deleter D takes the write lock (no readers are inside) and unlinks and frees X; I then writes X's next pointer, into a node that is no longer in the list (in C++, freed memory). The insert is lost, or memory is corrupted.

Fix: inserters must also take the read lock, before the insert mutex, so a deleter waits for any in-progress insert and inserters wait for any deleter. Writer preference blocks later searchers and inserters; fair ordering is needed as well to prevent one deleter being repeatedly overtaken by others.

Say it out loud

Out loudabout 90 s
"Design a read-write lock for me. Who can starve, and what would you change if both readers and writers need a turn?"
What a strong answer covers
  • State the invariant: one writer alone or multiple readers without a writer
  • Use one monitor mutex, active readers, a writing flag and waiting writers
  • Readers wait for active or waiting writers; writers wait for all active users to leave
  • Writer preference can starve readers and does not order competing writers
  • Ticket assignment orders admission and consecutive readers share; progress assumes holders finish and eligible threads run
  • State non-reentrancy, unsupported upgrade, and cleanup on interruption or exceptions
How a senior would say it
I would start with a writer-preferring monitor and state the policy explicitly. The invariant is one writer alone, or any number of readers with no writer. One mutex protects an active-reader count, a writing flag and a waiting-writer count. A reader waits while a writer is active or queued. A writer records that it is waiting, then waits until no reader or writer is active. Every wait rechecks its predicate. The last reader wakes a writer; a departing writer wakes another writer, or all readers if no writer remains. Notifications do not transfer ownership. This prevents new readers continually overtaking waiting writers, but readers can starve under continuous writes, and competing writers are not ordered. If both sides need a turn, I would assign tickets under the mutex. Consecutive reader tickets can enter together, but a writer ticket blocks later readers until earlier readers finish. That limits overtaking after ticket assignment; it does not promise a time bound without finite critical sections and eventual scheduling. My simple lock is non-reentrant and has no upgrade operation. Java interruption must undo waiting counts and wake newly eligible threads. Callers release in finally or through C++ guards, so an ordinary exception does not leak ownership.

Likely follow up: How would a timed-out ticket holder leave the queue without blocking every later ticket?

Out loudabout 75 s
"Searchers may overlap an inserter, but a deleter must run alone. Is a read-write lock plus an insert mutex enough to make the linked list safe?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Give searchers shared permission, inserters shared permission plus the insert mutex, and deleters exclusive permission
  • Explain why concurrent search and insertion still need safe publication of links
  • Build nodes fully, then publish Java volatile links or C++ release stores with acquire loads
  • Reclaim only under deletion permission and forbid pointers escaping protected operations
  • Separate deleter priority from individual fairness and mention the performance trade-off
How a senior would say it
Those locks enforce who may overlap, but the list also needs safe publication. Searchers take shared permission. Inserters take shared permission and the insert mutex, so only one inserts while searches continue. Deleters take exclusive permission, which excludes both kinds. The remaining hazard is a searcher reading the link an inserter changes. I would fully construct a node before linking it into the list. In Java the link is volatile. In C++ it is an atomic pointer, published with a release store and read with acquire loads. Otherwise a search could see an incompletely published node, and ordinary conflicting C++ pointer accesses would be a data race. Deletion can reclaim nodes while exclusive permission is held, provided no pointer or iterator escapes the protected operation. Removing the tail also has to repair the tail pointer. Finally, deleter priority stops new searches extending a wait forever, but it does not order competing deleters. A fair queue addresses that under normal progress assumptions, with extra coordination that I would benchmark against the simpler policy.

Likely follow up: What changes if search returns an iterator that the caller keeps after releasing the lock?

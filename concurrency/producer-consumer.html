Producer and consumer with a bounded buffer

Hand work between threads through a bounded buffer: blocking, many producers and consumers, shutdown, back-pressure and timeouts.

Asked atRubrikDatabricksLinkedInMicrosoftBloombergUber
Before this:Condition variables and monitorsSpurious and stolen wakeups: always wait in a loopLost wakeups, notify versus notifyAll, Mesa versus Hoare

One group of threads makes work, another group does it, and a buffer with a fixed size sits between them. This is the most common concurrency structure in real systems, the backbone of every thread pool and log writer, and one of the most asked concurrency coding questions.

In plain words

Picture the pass in a restaurant kitchen: the counter under the heat lamp where chefs put finished plates and waiters pick them up. It has room for six plates. If the pass is full, a chef with a plate in hand has to wait; if it is empty, a waiter has to wait. Nobody shouts across the room to coordinate. The counter itself does it.

Two things make it work. The counter is bounded: when waiters fall behind, the chefs are forced to slow down instead of stacking plates on the floor. And at closing time the manager says "kitchen closed": chefs stop cooking, waiters carry out whatever is still on the pass, and then everyone goes home. That is the whole lesson: a bounded buffer, two kinds of waiting, and a clean shutdown.

Why a buffer, and why a bounded one

The producer-consumer pattern separates making work from doing it. Producers (request handlers, a file reader, a crawler finding links) put items into a shared buffer; consumers (worker threads, a disk writer) take them out. The two sides can run at different speeds, and short bursts are absorbed by the buffer.

The buffer must have a limit. An unbounded queue hides a slow consumer until memory runs out: latency grows a little every second, then the process dies of an out of memory error hours later. A bounded buffer turns the same situation into back-pressure: when consumers cannot keep up, the buffer fills and producers are made to wait or are told no. The capacity is a decision about what happens under overload, not a tuning detail.

The state is tiny: the items and the capacity. The invariant is 0 <= size <= capacity, and items leave in the order they arrived. Every method locks, waits until it can act without breaking the invariant, acts, and wakes the threads that might now be able to act. That is the monitor pattern from the condition variables lessons, applied to the most useful structure there is.

Version 1: one monitor, wait and notifyAll

The shortest correct version uses one lock and one condition variable for both kinds of waiting. Producers wait while the buffer is full, consumers wait while it is empty, and since both kinds sleep on the same condition, every change must wake everybody.

This is correct with any number of producers and consumers. Three details carry the weight. The wait is in a while loop (in C++, the predicate overload is that loop), because a woken thread must re-check: another thread may have taken the item first, and wakeups can be spurious. The state change and the notify happen under the lock. And it is notifyAll, not notify, because of the next section.

Why notify is not enough with one condition

With a single condition, the threads asleep on it are a mix of producers and consumers. notify() (or notify_one()) wakes one of them, chosen by the runtime, and it may be the wrong kind. Two fixes: notifyAll (wake everybody and let the loop sort them out), or two conditions, so that every signal reaches a thread that can use it.

Version 2: two conditions, many producers, many consumers

A ReentrantLock can have several conditions, and in C++ you simply declare two std::condition_variable objects next to one mutex. Producers wait on "not full", consumers wait on "not empty". Now each condition only ever has one kind of waiter, so waking one thread is safe: after a put, any waiting consumer can make progress, and after a take, any waiting producer can.

The program below is the full version you should be able to write in an interview: two conditions, a close() method for shutdown, three producers and two consumers, and a check that every item arrived exactly once.

Read the two wait conditions carefully, because they are the whole shutdown design. A producer waits while the buffer is full and open. A consumer waits while it is empty and open, and it returns "nothing left" only when it is empty and closed. So a consumer that wakes after close() still takes the items that are left. Closing means "no more puts", not "throw away what is queued".

In C++, notice where the notify sits in close(): after the lock is released. Notifying while holding the lock is also correct; notifying after unlocking can save the woken thread from waking up only to block on the mutex. It is safe here because the buffer outlives every thread that waits on it: main joins the consumers before the buffer is destroyed. If a waiter could destroy the buffer as soon as it saw closed, you would notify under the lock instead, for the reason shown in the previous lesson. In put and take the notify is inside the lock for simplicity, which is always safe.

Why each consumer sums into its own slot

In the C++ demo, each consumer writes only sums[c] and counts[c], and main reads them after join(). No two threads write the same element, and the join gives main a happens-before edge to everything the consumer wrote, so no lock and no atomic is needed. That is the "do not share" fix from the lost update lesson, used on purpose.

Shutting down

Interviewers almost always ask "how does it stop?" because a producer and consumer system that cannot shut down cleanly either hangs on exit or drops work. There are three standard answers.

ApproachHow it worksGood forWatch out for
A closed flagclose() sets a flag under the lock and wakes everyone. put refuses; take drains, then reports "closed".Buffers you write yourself; any number of consumersClose only after all producers have finished, or their late puts fail
Poison pillsAfter the last real item, put one special "stop" item per consumer. A consumer that takes one exits.Library queues you cannot change, like ArrayBlockingQueueExactly one pill per consumer, after every producer is done; FIFO is what guarantees real work comes first
InterruptionInterrupt the consumer threads; take() throws InterruptedException and the loop exits.Stopping now, abandoning queued work (Java's shutdownNow style)Queued items are not processed; C++ has no thread interruption, so use a flag or a std::stop_token (C++20 std::condition_variable_any has wait overloads that take one)

With several producers, nobody can put the pills until all producers have finished. Usually the main thread joins the producers (or counts them down with a latch) and then puts the pills. A producer that puts a pill when it alone finishes is a classic bug: a consumer exits early while other producers are still adding work, and the last items are never processed.

Back-pressure: block, wait a while, or say no

A full buffer is the system telling producers "slow down". What a producer does next is a design decision, and the right answer depends on who the producer is:

  • Block (put): right for background producers, like a file reader feeding a parser. The reader simply goes at the parser's speed. Wrong for request threads, because a blocked request thread is a user waiting with no answer.
  • Wait with a timeout (offer(item, timeout)): try for a bounded time, then give up and let the caller decide: return an error, retry later, or count a drop. This is how services shed load instead of piling up.
  • Fail immediately (offer(item) or a try_put you write): when the item is optional and waiting is pointless, such as a sampled trace or a best-effort debug event.
  • Drop something: the newest item, or the oldest one (keep the freshest data). Always count the drops; silent loss is the worst outcome.

Two details matter here. In Java, awaitNanos returns the time still left, so a spurious or stolen wakeup does not restart the full timeout. In C++, wait_for with a predicate computes one deadline up front and handles the loop for you; without the predicate you would need wait_until with a fixed deadline in your own loop. poll(timeout) on the consumer side is the mirror image.

The library versions

In production Java you would not write this class. java.util.concurrent has it, in several flavors, and the method names tell you the overload policy:

ArrayBlockingQueue is Version 2 without close(): one lock and two conditions over a circular array. LinkedBlockingQueue uses two locks, one for the head and one for the tail, so a producer and a consumer can work at the same moment; its default capacity is Integer.MAX_VALUE, which means "unbounded" unless you pass one. The C++ standard library (through C++23) has no blocking queue at all, which is precisely why C++ candidates are asked to write one.

With semaphores instead of conditions

A common follow up is "can you build it with semaphores?" The textbook answer counts both kinds of room with two semaphores: freeSlots starts at the capacity, filledSlots starts at zero, and a lock still guards the deque itself, because two producers can get past freeSlots at the same moment. A semaphore remembers its releases, so there is no lost wakeup to reason about and no wait loop to write.

The trap is the order: acquire the semaphore first, then the lock. A consumer that locks first and then waits on filledSlots sleeps while holding the lock, so no producer can ever add the item it is waiting for, and the program deadlocks. The semaphore version is short, but it has no natural close(): a thread blocked in acquire can only be freed by extra permits (poison pills again) or, in Java, by interruption. That is why the condition version is the one to write when shutdown is part of the question, and the only one allowed when the interviewer bans semaphores, as one LinkedIn round did.

Worked example: sizing the buffer of a log writer

A service has 64 request threads that each call log(event). One background thread drains the buffer and writes to disk in batches, at about 50,000 events a second; the service produces about 20,000 a second at peak. How big should the buffer be, and what should log do when it is full?

  1. Normal operation. The writer is faster than the producers, so the buffer is nearly empty. Capacity barely matters here.
  2. A disk stall. Suppose the disk pauses for 200 ms. At 20,000 events a second, 4,000 events pile up. A capacity of 10,000 rides out a 0.5 s stall without anyone waiting.
  3. The latency it adds. By Little's law, time in queue = items in queue / drain rate. A full buffer of 10,000 drained at 50,000 a second adds up to 0.2 s before an event reaches the disk. A buffer of a million would "never fill", and would hide 20 s of lag and a lot of memory.
  4. When it does fill. Request threads should not block indefinitely on logging. Use offer with a short timeout (say 5 ms), and on failure drop the event and increment a droppedEvents counter that alerts. If the log is an audit log that must not lose events, block instead, and accept that a dead disk stops the service, which is the honest outcome.

This is the shape of the Databricks log writer question: producers, one writer, batching, and "what happens when the queue is full?" The buffer you just built is its core.

Common mistakes

  • if instead of while around the wait. A woken consumer finds the item already taken and removes from an empty queue. Always loop; in C++ always pass the predicate.
  • notify with one shared condition. The single notify can wake a thread of the wrong kind and the system stalls (the timeline above). Use notifyAll, or two conditions with signal.
  • Doing the work while holding the lock. take() should remove the item and return; processing it inside the lock serializes all consumers and blocks every producer.
  • Checking closed before checking for items. A consumer that returns as soon as it sees closed drops whatever is still queued. Drain first: return "nothing left" only when closed and empty.
  • Closing or sending pills too early. Close after every producer has finished, and send exactly one pill per consumer.
  • An unbounded queue "for safety". It moves the failure from a visible full buffer to an invisible memory leak. Note that new LinkedBlockingQueue<>() is unbounded.
  • Swallowing InterruptedException. Catching it and continuing the loop makes the consumer impossible to cancel. Exit the loop, or restore the flag with Thread.currentThread().interrupt().
  • Reading size() without the lock. It is a read of shared state like any other; in C++ it is a data race.

In the interview

This shows up in three forms. As a direct task: "design a bounded blocking queue" (LinkedIn has asked for it with plain wait and notify only, Bloomberg as a bounded concurrent queue, and Rubrik as the first step before building a thread pool on top, usually with library queues banned). As a debugging task: Rubrik's debugging round has handed candidates a C++ producer and consumer program with a critical section bug. And inside a bigger design: Databricks' log and event writer is producers, a queue and one writer thread, followed by "what happens when the queue is full?"

I will use one mutex for the queue and two condition variables, not full and not empty. Put waits in a loop while the queue is full, adds, and signals not empty; take is the mirror image. Each condition has only one kind of waiter, so signaling one thread is safe; with a single condition I would need notifyAll. For shutdown I will add a closed flag: put refuses after close, take drains what is left and then reports closed.

Follow ups to expect, roughly in order of frequency:

  • "Why while and not if?" Spurious wakeups, and another consumer may have taken the item first.
  • "Can you do it with one condition?" Yes, with notifyAll; explain the stall that notify causes.
  • "Add a timeout." awaitNanos or wait_for with a predicate; return false on timeout.
  • "How do you shut it down?" Closed flag or poison pills, and the ordering rules for each.
  • "What should a producer do when it is full?" Block, time out, or shed load, depending on who the producer is.
  • "Walk me through each step." LinkedIn interviewers have asked about the context switches at each step of a producer and consumer run. Practice saying it: "the consumer releases the lock inside await and sleeps; the producer acquires it, adds, signals; the consumer wakes, reacquires the lock, re-checks the condition."
  • "Can you do it with semaphores?" Yes: free-slot and filled-slot semaphores plus a lock for the deque, and the semaphore is always acquired before the lock.
  • "Is the order preserved?" Items leave in FIFO order, and each producer's items stay in that producer's order, but items from different producers interleave, and with several consumers the processing order is not the take order. If order matters per key, send each key to one consumer, as in ordering by ownership.
  • "How would you test it?" Several producers and consumers, every item tagged, check each arrives exactly once, capacity never exceeded, no hang (join with a timeout), and ThreadSanitizer in C++.

Senior depth

One lock or two. With one lock, producers and consumers serialize against each other. LinkedBlockingQueue splits it: putLock guards the tail, takeLock guards the head, and an AtomicInteger count connects them. A producer that sees the queue was empty must briefly take the take lock to signal a consumer, and signals cascade (a woken producer signals the next producer if there is still room). It is faster under heavy two-sided load and harder to get right; mentioning the design is a strong signal.

Batching. A consumer that takes one item per lock round trip pays the lock cost per item. drainTo(list, max) in Java, or a take_all that swaps the whole deque out under the lock in C++, lets a disk writer grab hundreds of items at once and write them in one system call. This is the heart of group commit in the log writer question.

Single producer, single consumer. With exactly one of each you can drop the lock entirely: a ring buffer where only the producer writes the tail index and only the consumer writes the head index, published with release stores and read with acquire loads. You will build the reasoning for that in memory orders.

Fairness. new ArrayBlockingQueue<>(n, true) makes waiting threads proceed in FIFO order at a throughput cost. Without it, a thread that just arrived can barge ahead of one that has waited a long time.

Key takeaways

  • A bounded buffer is one lock, the items, and two kinds of waiting: producers while full, consumers while empty.
  • Always wait in a loop. With one condition use notifyAll; with two conditions, signal (or notify_one) is safe.
  • The capacity is your overload policy: when full, block, time out, or shed load, and count what you drop.
  • Shut down with a closed flag that still drains, or one poison pill per consumer after all producers finish.
  • Never do the work inside the lock: take the item, release, then process.

Quick check

Answer each question, then read why. Your score is saved for your module test.

A bounded buffer uses one condition (one Java monitor, or one std::condition_variable) for producers and consumers, waits in a while loop, but wakes waiters with notify() (or notify_one()). With 2 producers and 2 consumers, what can happen?
Why. The loop makes the woken thread re-check safely, so nothing is corrupted, but it cannot recover a wakeup that went to a thread that cannot use it. A consumer that wakes a consumer, while producers sleep, leaves everyone waiting. The fix is notifyAll or two conditions.
A consumer's take() is written as if (items.isEmpty()) wait(); return items.removeFirst(); with notifyAll() after every put. There are two consumers. What can go wrong?
Why. notifyAll wakes both. The first to reacquire the lock takes the only item; the second returns from wait and, with if, does not re-check, so removeFirst throws NoSuchElementException (in C++, popping an empty deque is undefined behavior). A while loop sends it back to sleep.
Three producers and four consumers share a library blocking queue, and you shut down with poison pills. Which plan is correct?
Why. One pill stops one consumer, so you need one per consumer. They must go in after every real item, which means after every producer is done; FIFO then guarantees the real work is taken first. A producer putting its own pill can stop a consumer while other producers are still adding work.
In the demo's buffer, every producer has finished and close() is called while 5 items are still queued. One consumer then calls take() in a loop. What does it get?
Why. A consumer waits only while the buffer is empty and open, and reports "nothing left" only when it is empty and closed. Closing stops new puts; it does not throw away queued work. Checking closed before checking for items would lose the 5 items.
A web service's request threads put metrics events into a bounded buffer (losing a few is acceptable). The consumer is down and the buffer is full. Which behavior is best for the request threads?
Why. Request threads must not hang on a non-essential side task, so blocking forever is wrong. An unbounded queue just converts the outage into memory growth, and spinning burns CPU. A bounded wait plus a counted drop keeps the service responsive and makes the loss visible.
Why should a consumer process an item after take() returns, rather than inside the locked region of take()?
Why. The lock protects only the buffer's state. Work done while holding it serializes all consumers and stops producers too, so the pattern loses its parallelism. Remove the item, release the lock, then process it.
Java onlyWhich BlockingQueue call waits up to 50 ms for space and then reports failure without throwing?
Why. put blocks without limit, add throws IllegalStateException when full, and offer(e) fails immediately. The timed offer waits up to the timeout and returns false if there is still no room.
C++ onlyIn C++, what does cv.wait_for(lock, 50ms, [&] { return !q.empty(); }) return, and when?
Why. The predicate overload loops over spurious wakeups for you and returns a bool: the predicate's final value. The std::cv_status return belongs to the overload without a predicate, which also cannot tell a spurious wakeup from a real one.

Checkpoint

Write your own answer first. Then compare with the model answer, or ask Bar Raiser to mark it like an interviewer would.

Write code
Write a bounded buffer with put, take and close in your language. After close(), put must fail, and take must return the remaining items and then signal that the buffer is finished. Use two conditions.
Model answer

The key lines are the two wait conditions. Producers wait while full and open; consumers wait while empty and open, and report "finished" only when empty and closed, so queued items are drained. close() uses signalAll (notify_all) because every waiter, of both kinds, must re-check under the new rules.

Design
You own the logging library of a service: 64 request threads call log(event), and one background thread writes batches to disk. The interviewer asks: "What happens when the disk is slow and the queue fills up?" Give your answer, with the policy and the numbers you would use.
Model answer

First I would make sure the queue is bounded, because an unbounded one turns a slow disk into memory growth and, eventually, a crash that takes the service down with it. I would size it to ride out a normal disk hiccup: if we log 20,000 events a second and a stall can last half a second, a capacity of about 10,000 to 20,000 absorbs it. By Little's law a full queue of 10,000 drained at 50,000 a second adds about 0.2 seconds of logging lag, which is fine.

When the queue is still full, request threads must not block indefinitely on a side task. For ordinary application logs I would use offer with a short timeout, a few milliseconds, then drop the event and increment a dropped-events counter that we alert on, so the loss is visible. For audit logs that must never lose an event, I would block instead and make that explicit: if the disk is dead, the service should stop accepting work rather than silently break its audit guarantee. The writer drains in batches with drainTo so each disk write and fsync covers many events, and close() stops new events, drains the queue, flushes and then returns.

Explain
An interviewer says: "You used signal() in put and take. A colleague says that is a lost wakeup waiting to happen and you must use signalAll(). Who is right?" Answer as you would out loud.
Model answer

It depends on how many conditions there are, and with my design signal() is safe. I have two conditions: only producers ever wait on not-full, and only consumers ever wait on not-empty. After a put, exactly one more item exists, so waking exactly one consumer is enough, and any consumer can use it. After a take, one slot is free, so waking one producer is enough. Every signal reaches a thread that can make progress.

My colleague would be right for a single-condition design, like synchronized with wait(). There, producers and consumers sleep in the same wait set, so notify() might wake a producer when only a consumer can proceed, and with the wrong interleaving every thread ends up waiting. In that design you must use notifyAll(). The one place I do use signalAll() is close(), because closing changes the condition for every waiter at once.

Say it out loud

Interviews are spoken. Answer each question out loud as if the interviewer just asked it. Tandem transcribes you on this Mac, word for word, measures your pace, fillers and pauses, and Bar Raiser scores what you said and how you said it.

How a senior would say it
I would build it as a monitor. The state is a deque, a capacity, one mutex and two condition variables: not full, where producers wait, and not empty, where consumers wait. The invariant is that the size stays between zero and the capacity, and items leave in arrival order. put takes the lock, waits in a loop while the queue is full, adds the item and signals not empty. take is the mirror image. The loops matter because wakeups can be spurious, and another consumer may get the item first. A single signal is safe because each condition only ever has one kind of waiter, and each put or take frees exactly one of them. With one shared condition I would need notifyAll. Consumers process an item after take returns, never under the lock, or the queue stops being parallel. Now, when it is full. The capacity is really an overload policy, and the answer depends on who the producer is. A background stage, like a file reader feeding a parser, should just block, because running at the consumer's speed is exactly what we want. A request thread should not hang on a side task, so I would use an offer with a short timeout, and on failure return an error or drop the item and bump a counter we alert on. What I would not do is make the queue unbounded. That only turns a slow consumer into memory growth and latency that nobody sees until the process dies.

Out loudabout 90 s
"Here is a symptom: after we added graceful shutdown, the last few hundred items in the queue are sometimes never processed, and nothing crashes. What do you look for?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Lost items with no crash mean consumers left while work was still queued, so check the shutdown ordering
  • The consumer's exit test: returning as soon as closed is set drops the queue; finish only when closed and empty
  • When close happens: only after every producer has finished, never when one producer alone is done
  • With poison pills: exactly one per consumer, put after the last real item
  • Interrupting consumers is a stop now tool that abandons queued work, wrong for a graceful stop
  • Prove the fix with a test that tags every item and checks each is processed exactly once
How a senior would say it
Lost items with no crash usually mean consumers left while work was still queued, so I would check the shutdown ordering. First, the consumer's exit test. If take reports closed as soon as the flag is set, every item still in the buffer is dropped. The rule is drain first: a consumer reports finished only when the queue is closed and empty. Second, when close happens. It must come after every producer has finished, which in practice means after joining them. A common bug is a producer that closes the queue, or puts a poison pill, when it alone is done. A consumer then exits while other producers are still adding, and their last items are stranded or rejected. With pills, it is exactly one per consumer, after the last real item. Third, I would check whether the stop path interrupts the consumers. Interruption abandons whatever is queued. That is right for a stop now, and wrong for a graceful stop. To prove the fix, I would write a test that tags every item, runs several producers and consumers, and checks that each item is processed exactly once.

Out loudabout 90 s
"Walk me through exactly what each thread does, and where the context switches are, when a consumer calls take on an empty queue and a producer then calls put."
What a strong answer covers
  • The consumer takes the lock, sees the queue empty, and calls await, which releases the lock and sleeps as one step
  • The producer takes the free lock, adds the item and signals not empty; the signal makes the consumer eligible but does not hand it the lock
  • The consumer must reacquire the lock inside await before it returns, so it may wake only to block until the producer unlocks
  • Back in its loop, the consumer re-checks the condition, because another consumer may have taken the item or the wakeup may be spurious
  • It removes the item, signals not full, releases the lock, and processes the item outside the lock
  • Context switches: one when the consumer blocks, one when it is woken, and possibly one more if it wakes into a held lock
How a senior would say it
The consumer goes first. It acquires the lock, sees the queue is empty and calls await on not empty. That call releases the lock and puts the consumer to sleep as one step. That is the first context switch, off the CPU. Now the producer calls put. The lock is free, so it gets it at once, sees there is room, adds the item and signals not empty. The signal does not hand over the lock. It only makes the consumer eligible to run, and the producer carries on until it unlocks. The consumer is scheduled again, the second switch, and it must reacquire the lock inside await before it returns. If the producer still holds it, the consumer may wake only to block again, depending on the implementation. Once it has the lock, the consumer re-checks the condition in its loop, because another consumer could have taken the item first, or the wakeup could be spurious. The queue is not empty, so it removes the item, signals not full in case a producer is waiting, releases the lock, and only then processes the item.

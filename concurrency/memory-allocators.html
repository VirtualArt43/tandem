Memory allocators: free lists, buddy allocation and thread safety

Build and reason about free lists, buddy allocation, fragmentation and thread-safe allocator caches.

Asked atRubrikNvidia
Before this:Queues inside a fixed bufferLock granularity, contention and lock striping

A Rubrik phone screen asked for alloc(size) and free(index) over 16 GB, with a follow up steering toward a buddy allocator. Allocators combine resource management, ownership and synchronization on a hot path, making them a useful system coding exercise.

In plain words

Picture a big banquet hall with folding partition walls. A wall can split the hall in half, and each half can be split in half again, down to small rooms. A party of 30 gets the smallest room that seats at least 30: a 32-seat room, made by unfolding walls until a room that size exists. When the party leaves, the manager checks the room next to it, the other half of the same split. If that neighbour is empty too, the wall between them folds away and the bigger room is back. Keep folding while the neighbour at each size is empty, and the hall becomes one hall again.

That is the buddy allocator. The price is visible: a party of 33 gets a 64-seat room and 31 seats stand empty. The prize is that finding a room and rejoining rooms takes a handful of steps, because a room's partner is always found by a simple rule, not by searching.

Why allocators show up in system coding rounds

  • Rubrik, 2025 phone screen (senior): alloc(size) and free(index) over 16 GB, steered toward a buddy allocator, in C++.
  • Rubrik, 2026 redo round: "thread-safe parking in a 1-D array, a car takes 1 unit, a truck takes 2 contiguous units", framed explicitly as a memory allocation problem.
  • Rubrik, US onsite 2025: a multithreading round that was mostly memory management and systems discussion.
  • Nvidia, senior system software (2026): implement malloc and printf, alongside spinlocks and process synchronization.

A common interview shape is: a fixed region (an arena) addressed by offsets, an alloc that must find space fast, a free that must make space reusable, metadata that must stay small, and many threads calling both. The same shape hides in disk block allocation, GPU memory, connection IDs and parking lots.

The vocabulary

TermMeaning
ArenaThe region the allocator manages, here addressed by unit offsets from 0
BlockA piece of the arena handed out by alloc, identified by its offset
Internal fragmentationWaste inside a block: you asked for 33 units and got 64
External fragmentationEnough free space in total, but no single free piece big enough
MetadataWhat the allocator remembers: which blocks are free, how big each allocated block is
CoalescingMerging adjacent free pieces back into a larger one on free

Every design trades these against speed. Interviewers want to hear the trade named, not avoided: fragmentation depends on the request sizes and the allocation policy; a fixed-size pool can avoid external fragmentation for requests of that one size.

Fixed-size pools and free lists

When every block has the same size, allocation is trivial and fast. Keep the free blocks in a free list: alloc pops one, free pushes it back, both O(1), with zero external fragmentation. The classic trick is the intrusive free list: a free block's own memory holds the link to the next free block, so links reuse idle payload space, although pool headers and allocation-state metadata still cost memory; in C that is a pointer stored in the first bytes of each free block, and in an index-based arena the next index is stored there. Each free block must be large and aligned enough for its link, and only free blocks may have their payload reused this way.

Real allocators build variable sizes out of fixed pools. Requests are rounded up to one of a few size classes (for example 16, 32, 48, 64 bytes and so on), each class has its own pool of same-size blocks, and large requests go elsewhere. That bounds rounding waste within each size class; unused space stranded in other classes and partly occupied slabs can still waste memory. Two safety points interviewers like: a double free pushes the same block on the free list twice, so two later alloc calls hand it to two owners; and freeing a block that was never allocated corrupts the list. Keep a per-block "in use" bit and check it.

Variable sizes without size classes

The general-purpose approach keeps free ranges (offset and length) and picks one with first fit (the first range big enough) or best fit (the smallest range big enough), splits it, and on free merges the block with free neighbours on either side. Finding the neighbours quickly needs either an address-ordered free structure or boundary tags (each block's size stored at both ends). Search cost depends on the data structure: a plain list can require O(number of free ranges) for either policy. Best fit minimises the immediate leftover, but can create tiny unusable holes; neither policy guarantees less long-term fragmentation. Merging needs care. The buddy system makes neighbours and merging trivial by restricting sizes to powers of two.

The buddy allocator

The arena has 2 to the power K units. A block of order k is 2 to the power k units, and it always starts at a multiple of its own size. Keep one set of free block offsets per order.

  • alloc(size): round size up to the next power of two, order k. Find the smallest order j at or above k that has a free block. While j is larger than k, split: the block's upper half (offset plus 2^(j - 1)) becomes a free block of order j minus 1, and you keep the lower half. Record the allocated block's order.
  • free(offset): look up the block's order k. Its buddy, the other half of the block it was split from, starts at offset XOR size: for an aligned block, flipping that single bit gives the partner. If the buddy is free at the same order, remove it from its free set, continue with the merged block at the lower of the two offsets and order k plus 1, and repeat. Stop when the buddy is busy or split, or the whole arena is reached. Put the final block in its free set.

Each call visits at most K split or merge levels, plus an O(K) search for a non-empty order on allocation. Interpreting the reported 16 GB as 16 GiB and using 4 KiB units gives 2^22 units, so at most 22 splits or merges. Here GiB and KiB mean powers of 1024; decimal GB and KB would give different counts. A buddy is in the free set of its order only when that entire block is free and unsplit. An allocated buddy is absent; a split buddy has any free pieces recorded at lower orders. That one fact makes the merge check a single set lookup.

Demo contract: this is an offset allocator, not a replacement for malloc or Java object allocation. Orders 0 to 62 are supported; invalid sizes fail, and free rejects offsets that are not currently allocated. Callers must stop using a block before freeing it and safely transfer ownership between threads. The ordered free sets make output deterministic, but each set lookup or insertion is O(log F) for F free blocks at that order, rather than O(1); the allocation map has expected O(1) operations.

The sample assumes host-heap metadata allocations succeed. If a set or map allocation fails partway through splitting or merging, it does not roll back: do not catch that failure and continue using this instance. A fixed-memory allocator preallocates its metadata, or stages updates with rollback, so even freeing memory cannot require another successful allocation.

Why the lock is enough: split blocks remain disjoint and cover their parent, and merging only equal-order buddies preserves that partition. Successful allocation linearizes when its live record is installed; free linearizes when the coalesced block is returned to a free set. Both happen under the same mutex, so no caller observes an intermediate split or merge. This protects allocator metadata, not application accesses after a caller frees its block.

The stress test is the one you should write in the round: each thread stamps its id on every unit of every block it owns and checks the stamps before freeing. Overlapping allocations can corrupt these stamps, but a finite stress run can miss a bad schedule. Add deterministic exhaustion, invalid-free and full-coalescing tests too. In C++ the stamps are also a ThreadSanitizer check: different threads write the same owner cells over time, and that is race-free only because every hand-over passes through the allocator's mutex.

Sizing it for 16 GiB

The Rubrik prompt was alloc(size) and free(index) over 16 GB. Say the choices out loud:

  • Pick a minimum block. Byte granularity gives 35 orders (0 to 34); one metadata entry per minimum block would be enormous. A 4 KiB minimum (a chosen unit, not a portable OS page-size assumption) gives 2 to the power 22, about 4.2 million minimum blocks, and 23 orders (0 to 22).
  • Metadata. The order of each allocated block fits in a byte: an array of 4,194,304 bytes (4 MiB) indexed by block number replaces the hash map. Add a state value distinguishing allocated heads, free heads and interior units. Free sets can be doubly linked lists threaded through index arrays: direct buddy indexing plus the free state and order make membership checks and removal O(1). A bitmap alone also needs an efficient way to find a free block. The Linux kernel keeps a free list per order and marks which pages head a free block, and of what order, in its per-page metadata, which is the same idea.
  • Cost per call. At most 22 splits or merges, each O(1) with directly indexed state and doubly linked free lists. The demo uses trees, so its set work is O(K log F), with K orders and at most F free blocks per order, excluding lock waiting and the allocation map.
  • Fragmentation. For requests at least one minimum unit, power-of-two rounding wastes less than half the returned block. A one-byte request in a 4 KiB minimum block wastes 4095 bytes, so that bound does not cover minimum-size rounding; external fragmentation remains, because two free neighbours that are not buddies can never merge.

For context, the Linux page allocator is a buddy allocator; its page size and maximum order depend on the architecture and kernel configuration, and /proc/buddyinfo shows how many free blocks of each order every memory zone has.

Ownership, alignment and stale frees

A live-offset check catches an immediate double free, but not a stale one after reuse: A frees offset 0, B allocates offset 0, then A frees 0 again. The map now describes B's allocation. A checked handle needs both offset and generation; compare both on free and define generation wraparound. A raw-pointer allocator instead relies on callers obeying ownership and lifetime rules.

For real byte storage, alignment is a separate contract. Align the arena base, choose a block size at least as large as both the request and its required power-of-two alignment, and include any header or padding before rounding, with overflow checks. C++ object construction and destruction must respect lifetime and alignment, typically through RAII; an offset alone is not a live object. In Java this exercise manages indices into storage, not the placement or reclamation of ordinary Java objects.

Making an allocator thread-safe

Allocation is on the hot path of every thread, so the locking design matters more than for most structures:

DesignHow it worksTrade-off
One lockEvery alloc and free takes the allocator's mutex, as in the demoSimple and obviously correct; with many threads, they queue on one contended lock
A lock per size classSmall requests go to fixed-size pools, each with its own lockThreads allocating different sizes stop colliding; same-size traffic still does
Several arenasThreads are spread over independent arenas, each with its own locksLess contention and less false sharing between threads' objects; free capacity can remain stranded in one arena unless the allocator transfers or releases it
Per-thread cachesEach thread keeps a small stash of free blocks per size class; refill and drain in batches from the shared poolMost calls take no lock at all; memory sits idle in stashes, and a thread that frees what another allocated grows its stash unless it gives blocks back

Production allocators combine these. glibc malloc and jemalloc use arenas and thread caches; TCMalloc supports per-thread or per-CPU front ends backed by shared transfer and central free lists. Exact defaults depend on the implementation and configuration. This family is the thread-caching allocator, and the core move fits in a few lines:

The following fragments assume non-negative pool capacities, valid block ids returned exactly once to their originating pool, successful metadata allocations, and all worker threads joined before the shared pool is destroyed. They illustrate batching, not validation. A return value of -1 means this thread and the shared pool have no blocks: other threads may still hold idle cached blocks. A strict allocator must reclaim those caches or retry before claiming global exhaustion.

Notice the two leaks the design has to handle. A thread that only frees (a consumer receiving objects allocated by a producer) would grow its stash forever, so the stash gives a batch back when it passes a high-water mark. And a thread that exits would strand its stash: the C++ version returns it from the thread_local destructor, which runs at thread exit; Java's ThreadLocal has no exit hook, so a dying thread's stash is simply garbage and those block numbers are lost from the pool unless you return them explicitly, as flushCurrentThread() does when called in a worker's finally block. Thread pools may also need task-boundary flushing, since their threads remain alive. Production policies combine cache limits, draining and thread-exit handling; no one scavenging policy is universal.

Common mistakes

  • Finding the buddy with addition. offset + size is the buddy only for a lower half; an upper half's buddy is below it. offset XOR size is right for both.
  • Merging with a buddy that is split. Checking "is the buddy's first unit free" is not enough; the whole buddy block must be free at the same order, which is exactly "is it in the free set of this order".
  • Forgetting the block's size on free. free(offset) has no size, so the allocator must remember each allocated block's order.
  • Trusting the caller. A double free or a bad offset corrupts the free sets silently. Check that the offset is a live allocation.
  • Locking per free set. One alloc touches several orders while splitting, and one free several while merging, so per-order locks need careful ordering for little gain. One lock per allocator (or per arena) is the right interview answer.
  • Doing slow work under the allocator lock. Zeroing a 1 GB block, logging or calling the operating system for more memory while holding the lock stalls every thread's allocation.

In the interview

The Rubrik phone version starts open-ended: "implement alloc(size) and free(index) over a 16 GB region". A strong opening names the options and picks one with its trade-off before coding.

I could keep free ranges with first fit and coalescing, but merging neighbours and external fragmentation get messy, so I will use a buddy allocator: sizes are powers of two, every block's buddy is at offset XOR size, so splitting and merging each take at most one step per order. I will interpret 16 GB as 16 GiB and use a 4 KiB minimum block: 23 orders, a free list per order, indexed state and order bytes, and next and previous indices for free blocks. Larger requests waste less than half their rounded block, but tiny requests can waste almost the whole minimum block. For threads, one mutex around alloc and free is correct and simple; if it becomes hot, per-thread caches of small blocks in front of it, the way tcmalloc and jemalloc do it.

Follow ups: "what happens on a double free?" (detect it with the per-block state), "the arena is fragmented and alloc(1 GB) fails though half is free: what now?" (that is external fragmentation; buddies cannot merge across non-buddy neighbours; compaction needs moving blocks, which needs indirection), and "the parking lot version: a truck needs two adjacent spots" (scan for two adjacent free spots under the lot's lock; with striped locks, a truck spanning a stripe boundary takes both stripes in ascending order).

Senior depth

  • Lock-free free lists. A free list pushed and popped with compare-and-swap is a Treiber stack, and it has the ABA problem: a block popped, freed and pushed back between another thread's read and its CAS. A versioned head can detect ABA until its tag wraps, but does not by itself make reading a reused link safe. Hazard pointers delay reuse only if every path honours the protection and retirement protocol; neither is a drop-in fix for an arbitrary intrusive free list; see the lock-free stack and ABA. Per-thread caches avoid the question for the common path.
  • Java allocation fast paths. HotSpot commonly uses thread-local allocation buffers (TLABs): eligible allocations that fit bump a private pointer. Refills, large objects and garbage collection take other paths; generation layout depends on the collector, so this is not a Java language guarantee. It is the per-thread cache idea applied to bump allocation, and it is why allocation in Java is cheap.
  • False sharing between allocations. Two small objects that different cores frequently write on the same cache line can contend even though they are logically separate. Per-thread caches and per-thread arenas keep a thread's objects together.
  • Returning memory. Freeing a block usually makes it reusable by the allocator without immediately releasing its physical pages. Depending on the OS, allocators can advise discarding page contents with an appropriate madvise mode, or remove a mapping with munmap. Those are different operations; later reuse may need page faults or a new mapping.

Key takeaways

  • Allocators are the canonical system coding resource problem: an arena, fast alloc and free, small metadata, many threads.
  • Fixed-size pools with a free list are O(1) with no external fragmentation; size classes build variable sizes from them.
  • A buddy allocator splits power-of-two blocks on alloc and merges a block with its buddy, at offset XOR size, on free.
  • Name the fragmentation trade: buddy allocators accept internal waste to make coalescing trivial.
  • One lock is correct; per-size-class locks, arenas and per-thread caches are how production allocators scale.

Quick check

Answer each question, then read why. Your score is saved for your module test.

In a buddy allocator, a free block of size 4 (order 2) starts at offset 12. At which offset does its buddy start?
Why. The buddy of an aligned block of size s at offset x is at x XOR s: 12 XOR 4 is 8. Blocks 8 to 11 and 12 to 15 are the two halves of the size 8 block at offset 8. Adding the size (16) would only be right for a lower half, and 16 is not even in the parent block.
A buddy allocator with a minimum unit of 1 receives alloc(5). What block does it hand out, and how much internal fragmentation results?
Why. Requests round up to the next power of two, so 5 becomes 8 and 3 units inside the block are wasted. In the worst case, a request one unit above a power of two wastes almost half its block, which is the price for trivial splitting and merging.
An arena has 600 MiB free in total, but no suitable contiguous 512 MiB block, so alloc(512 MiB) fails. Which term describes this?
Why. External fragmentation is free memory scattered into pieces too small for the request. Internal fragmentation is waste inside blocks that are already handed out. In a buddy allocator, two free neighbours that are not buddies can never merge, so external fragmentation remains even with coalescing.
An initially free 8-unit arena uses the locked allocator above. Threads A and B each call alloc(5) once, neither frees, and main prints their results after joining both. What can it print, using "none" for failure?
Why. Each request rounds up to all 8 units. The lock serialises the calls, so the first returns offset 0 and the second fails. Scheduling chooses the winner. Two zeros would overlap, offset 4 cannot start an 8-unit block, and both cannot fail in an initially free arena with successful metadata allocation.
Many threads call your allocator and it has become a bottleneck. Which designs can reduce lock contention? Select all that apply.Select all that apply
Why. Per-thread caches take no lock on most calls, arenas split one hot lock into several, and per-size-class pools stop different sizes from colliding: glibc malloc, jemalloc and tcmalloc combine these. Holding the lock longer makes contention worse, and spinning only burns CPU while waiting for the same single lock.
Java onlyIn HotSpot with TLABs enabled, why is a small object allocation that fits its current TLAB usually cheap?
Why. For this fast path, the TLAB is the per-thread cache idea applied to bump allocation: the common path is a pointer increment in memory the thread owns. Escape analysis can sometimes avoid heap allocation entirely, but in general Java objects live on the heap.
C++ onlyIn the C++ BlockAllocator, the per-thread stash is a thread_local Stash whose destructor returns its blocks to the shared pool. When does that destructor run for a thread that has initialised it and terminates normally?
Why. Objects with thread storage duration are destroyed when their thread exits (for the main thread, before static objects are destroyed). That gives the stash a natural place to hand its blocks back; Java's ThreadLocal offers no such hook.

Checkpoint

Write your own answer first. Then compare with the model answer, or ask Bar Raiser to mark it like an interviewer would.

Pseudo code
A buddy allocator manages 32 units and starts fully free. Trace these calls, giving each returned offset and the free blocks after each step: alloc(6), alloc(2), alloc(9), free(8), free(0).
Model answer
  1. alloc(6): round up to 8 (order 3). Split 32 at 0 into 16 at 0 and 16 at 16 (free); split 16 at 0 into 8 at 0 and 8 at 8 (free). Return 0. Free: 16 at 16, 8 at 8.
  2. alloc(2): order 1. Orders 1 and 2 are empty; take 8 at 8; split into 4 at 8 and 4 at 12 (free); split 4 at 8 into 2 at 8 and 2 at 10 (free). Return 8. Free: 16 at 16, 4 at 12, 2 at 10.
  3. alloc(9): round up to 16 (order 4). Take 16 at 16. Return 16. Free: 4 at 12, 2 at 10.
  4. free(8): order 1; buddy 8 XOR 2 is 10, free, merge into 4 at 8. Order 2: buddy 8 XOR 4 is 12, free, merge into 8 at 8. Order 3: buddy 8 XOR 8 is 0, allocated. Stop. Free: 8 at 8.
  5. free(0): order 3; buddy 0 XOR 8 is 8, free, merge into 16 at 0. Order 4: buddy 0 XOR 16 is 16, allocated. Stop. Free: 16 at 0.

Internal waste while all three were allocated: 2 units in the first block and 7 in the third.

Debug
This free passes simple tests but corrupts the allocator after a while. Find the bug.
Model answer

The buddy is computed with addition. For a block that is the upper half of its parent, the buddy lies below it, at offset - size. Here, freeing an upper half looks for a "buddy" at offset plus size, which is a block in a different parent. If that block happens to be free at the same order, the code merges two blocks that are not buddies, records a merged block that is not aligned to its size, and later splits and merges go wrong. Use offset ^ (1L << k) in Java or offset ^ (uint64_t{1} << k) in C++; a plain 1 has the wrong width for large orders.

There is a second bug: after a merge the merged block must start at the lower of the two offsets (offset = min(offset, buddy)); this code keeps the original offset. And neither version checks that the offset was actually allocated: the Java version throws a NullPointerException on unboxing, and the C++ version's allocated_[offset] inserts a 0 and frees a block that was never handed out. Check for a live allocation first.

Design
"Implement alloc(size) and free(index) over a 16 GB region, thread-safe. Talk me through your choices before coding."
Model answer

I would use a buddy allocator with a 4 KiB minimum block, interpreting 16 GB as 16 GiB: this is 2 to the power 22 such blocks, so orders 0 to 22. Sizes round up to a power-of-two number of 4 KiB blocks. Metadata: for each order a free set that supports removing a specific buddy in O(1), for example a doubly linked list threaded through an index array of 4.2 million entries, plus one byte per minimum block holding its head order and whether it is allocated, free or interior to catch double frees. That is a few tens of MiB of metadata for 16 GiB, depending on index widths and layout.

alloc finds the smallest non-empty order at or above the request and splits down; free merges with the buddy at offset XOR size while it is free at the same order. There are at most 22 splits or merges, plus the scan for a non-empty order; those per-level operations are constant time with this indexed metadata. I would state the trade: power-of-two rounding waste below half a block for requests at least 4 KiB, with potentially much larger percentage waste for tiny requests; external fragmentation reduced by merging but not eliminated. Thread safety: one mutex around alloc and free, because each call touches several orders and per-order locks add ordering risk for little gain. If many threads allocate small blocks, I would add per-thread caches of free minimum-size blocks, refilled and drained in batches of, say, 32 under the lock, with a high-water mark so a thread that only frees gives blocks back, and a flush when the thread exits.

"You have a 16 GB region. Walk me through a thread-safe allocator, including what your complexity claim depends on."
What a strong answer covers
  • Clarify binary capacity and minimum unit: 16 GiB, 4 KiB, 23 orders
  • Allocate by rounding and splitting; free by XOR buddy lookup and repeated coalescing
  • State disjoint ownership and cover all metadata transitions with one mutex
  • Distinguish constant-time indexed metadata from logarithmic tree operations
  • Explain minimum-block waste, external fragmentation and preallocated metadata
How a senior would say it
I would start with a buddy allocator protected by one mutex. I would clarify that the region is sixteen gibibytes and choose a four kibibyte minimum unit. That gives two to the power twenty-two units and twenty-three orders, including order zero. I keep free blocks by order and record the order and state of every block head. Allocation rounds the request up, finds a non-empty order and splits a larger block. Free checks that the offset is live, finds its buddy by XOR with the block size, and merges while that exact buddy is free at the same order. Every live allocation must be disjoint, so the mutex covers the whole transition across all affected orders. There are at most twenty-two split or merge levels. Constant time per level needs indexed state and removable free-list links; the tree-based demo pays logarithmic set costs. Power-of-two rounding wastes less than half a block for requests above the minimum, but a tiny request can waste nearly a whole minimum block. External fragmentation still exists when neighbours are not buddies. I would preallocate metadata so freeing cannot fail while allocating bookkeeping, then test exhaustion, invalid frees, concurrent ownership and complete coalescing before optimising contention.

Likely follow up: What if a caller frees an old offset after another thread has already reallocated that offset?

Out loudabout 75 s
"Adding thread caches improved throughput, but memory use grew and allocation sometimes fails while blocks are idle. What do you check?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Distinguish live allocations from free blocks stranded in thread caches
  • Explain batch refill and draining as the contention versus retention trade-off
  • Handle producer-consumer imbalance, cache bounds and thread or task exit
  • Clarify local exhaustion versus a globally exhausted pool
  • Test cross-thread ownership transfer and shutdown lifetime
How a senior would say it
I would first separate live allocations from free blocks retained in caches. The improvement is plausible because most calls now touch only one thread's stash, and one shared lock acquisition transfers a whole batch. But those idle blocks are unavailable to other threads until they return to the shared pool. A producer may allocate continuously while a consumer frees its objects, so the consumer's cache grows unless it drains above a high-water mark. I would bound each cache, batch its returns and provide a pressure-driven drain or retry path. An empty local cache and empty shared list do not prove that the entire pool is exhausted. I would also check thread exit. The C++ stash destructor returns cached blocks, provided the shared pool is still alive. Java needs an explicit flush in the worker's finally block, and long-lived thread pools may need task-boundary cleanup too. My tests would transfer ownership through a synchronised queue, return blocks from a different thread, end workers with populated caches and verify that all capacity becomes reusable. I would measure both contention and retained memory before choosing batch sizes.

Likely follow up: How would your design reclaim idle caches without adding a lock to every fast-path allocation?

std::chrono: durations, clocks and deadlines

Choose clocks, convert durations, and share one deadline across timed waits without restarting the budget after wakeups.

Asked atRubrikLinkedInNetflixConfluent
Before this:Templates, auto and type deduction

Every timeout, retry, rate limit, lease and scheduled task is arithmetic on time. Use the wrong clock, or restart a timer at the wrong moment, and a wall-clock correction or a burst of wakeups makes a 100 ms timeout fire at once, or never.

In plain words

A cricket umpire's wristwatch and the stopwatch used to time an over are different tools. The wristwatch tells the time of day, and someone can change it: a reset or a wrong setting. The stopwatch does not know the time of day at all, but it only ever moves forward, at a steady rate. To ask "have five minutes passed?" you use the stopwatch.

std::chrono gives you both. A duration is an amount of time, like "five minutes". A time point is a position on one particular clock, like "3:05 pm on the wristwatch" or "300 seconds on the stopwatch". Timeouts are stopwatch questions.

Why this matters for concurrency

  • Every timed wait takes chrono types. condition_variable::wait_for and wait_until, timed_mutex::try_lock_for, future::wait_for, counting_semaphore::try_acquire_for, and std::this_thread::sleep_for and sleep_until.
  • A wait loop needs a fixed deadline. Spurious and stolen wakeups make a thread wait several times. If each wait restarts a 100 ms timer, the total wait has no bound.
  • Schedulers store absolute times. A delayed task runner keeps due times in a heap and sleeps with wait_until until the earliest one, waking early when an even earlier task arrives.
  • It is asked. The research notes report delayed scheduler questions at LinkedIn and Confluent, scheduled thread pool executors at Netflix, and a per-task timeout follow up to a hand-built thread pool at Rubrik. All of them are built on this lesson.

Durations: an amount of time, with the unit in the type

std::chrono::duration<Rep, Period> is a count of ticks (Rep, usually an integer) plus the length of a tick (Period, a compile-time fraction of a second). You will use the aliases: nanoseconds, microseconds, milliseconds, seconds, minutes, hours. With using namespace std::chrono_literals; you can write them as literals: 100ms, 2s, 500us, 1min.

For integral representations, conversion is implicit when the source tick is an exact multiple of the destination tick, as seconds to milliseconds is. A conversion that may discard fractional ticks needs an explicit operation. Floating-point destinations also allow implicit conversions, but the representation can round; the type system does not check numeric range or prevent overflow.

Three habits follow from this. Keep values as durations until the last moment, and call .count() only to print or to hand a number to a C API. Make APIs take std::chrono::milliseconds (or any duration) instead of int timeoutMs, so a caller cannot pass seconds by mistake. And when you round a timeout, round up with ceil: duration_cast truncates toward zero, and a positive timeout that truncates to 0 becomes a poll; retrying that poll in a loop can burn CPU.

Printing a duration with <<, as in the last line, is C++20; in C++17 print .count() and the unit yourself. The literals are C++14; floor, ceil and round are C++17. With negative durations, duration_cast<seconds>(-1500ms) gives -1 s, floor gives -2 s, and ceil gives -1 s; round chooses the nearest value with ties to even.

Time points: a position on one particular clock

A std::chrono::time_point<Clock, Duration> is a duration measured from that clock's epoch. Clock::now() returns one. The arithmetic is what you would expect from positions and distances: time point plus duration gives a time point, and time point minus time point gives a duration. Adding two time points does not compile, and neither does subtracting time points from different clocks, because a position on the wristwatch and a position on the stopwatch have nothing to do with each other.

Three clocks, and which one to use

ClockWhat it measuresCan it jump?Use it for
std::chrono::system_clockWall-clock time: since C++20, Unix time from 1 January 1970 UTC, excluding leap secondsYes. The last row surprises people. high_resolution_clock sounds like the right tool for timing, but its steadiness is implementation-defined. On this Mac libc++ aliases it to steady_clock; other libraries may alias it to system_clock, which can jump. Run this to see what your machine says:

The steady clock is a monotonic clock, and it promises only two things: it never goes backward, and its ticks are steady. It says nothing about the time of day, so its epoch has no portable calendar meaning. Its time points are useful for ordering local events and setting deadlines, but must not be persisted or compared across machines as timestamps. Whether it keeps counting while a laptop is suspended depends on the platform.

Use steady_clock for elapsed-time budgets

A deadline expressed on system_clock is a wall-clock time: "wake me when the clock on the wall reads 10:00:05". An implementation that follows that clock can stretch or shorten the wait when the clock changes. This is not a portable elapsed-time budget. The standard allows implementation latitude in responding to clock adjustments; do not promise that every library reacts immediately or identically.

Relative waits are meant to be safe: the standard says implementations should measure wait_for, sleep_for and try_lock_for with a steady clock, and condition_variable::wait_for(lock, d, pred) is defined as wait_until(lock, std::chrono::steady_clock::now() + d, pred). Some older library and OS combinations implement timed waits using wall-clock primitives, so verify timing behaviour when supporting old deployments. The danger is in your own code: deadlines you compute and store on system_clock, elapsed times you measure with it, and high_resolution_clock used on the assumption that it is steady.

sleep_for and sleep_until

std::this_thread::sleep_for(d) blocks the thread for at least d; it may oversleep, because the thread must be scheduled again. sleep_until(tp) blocks until a time point. The difference matters for anything that runs at a fixed rate: a loop that does some work and then sleeps for the period drifts by the work time on every tick, while a loop that advances a deadline by the period and sleeps until it stays on schedule.

Ignoring overhead, the first loop needs at least about 125 ms and the fixed-rate schedule targets 100 ms. These are illustrative timings, not upper bounds: scheduler delays, timer resolution and instrumentation can make either much slower. This is the difference between "fixed delay" and "fixed rate" in a scheduled executor. And one rule from the house style: never sleep to wait for another thread. A sleep guesses how long the other thread needs; a condition variable waits for protected state and rechecks it after every wakeup.

Waiting with a deadline

Timed waits come in pairs, one taking a duration and one taking a time point, and each reports a timeout in its own way:

For a condition variable, use the predicate overloads. They loop over spurious wakeups for you, and they compute the deadline once. Here is a queue whose popFor gives up after a timeout and reports it through std::optional:

The deadline is computed before taking the lock, so lock contention consumes the budget. However, acquiring this ordinary mutex and reacquiring it after the wait are not timed, and the scheduler may run the thread late: this is not a hard upper bound on return time. The predicate can also be true when finally checked after the deadline, in which case this API succeeds. A strict acceptance cutoff requires an explicit clock check under the lock, in addition to the state check.

Zero or negative budgets perform an immediate predicate check once the lock is acquired; an available item may still be returned. The examples assume all deadlines are representable and item moves do not throw. Validate externally supplied durations before conversion or addition: multiplying ticks, adding to a time point, or casting an out-of-range floating value can overflow or have undefined behaviour.

For several operations, pass one popUntil(deadline) style deadline through the whole request. If only relative APIs are available, compute the remaining duration before each step and stop starting work once it is nonpositive. A timed mutex applies the budget to the lock-acquisition attempt, but cannot turn ordinary OS scheduling into a hard real-time guarantee.

The fix is to compute the deadline once and check the condition one last time. The predicate overload does exactly that, and it is a single line:

The standard defines this as while (!pred()) { if (wait_until(lock, deadline) == std::cv_status::timeout) return pred(); } return true; with deadline = steady_clock::now() + 100ms computed once. Note the final return pred(): on a timeout it still checks the condition, because the state may have become true just as the time ran out.

Worked example: a delayed task runner

The core of every delayed scheduler question: schedule(task, delay) may be called from any thread, and one worker thread runs each task when it is due, earliest first. Three rules make it correct. Store absolute due times on steady_clock. Sleep with wait_until the earliest due time. And when a new task arrives that is due earlier than the one the worker is sleeping for, wake the worker so it can recompute its deadline.

C is submitted first. If the worker starts waiting before A is submitted, it initially targets C's deadline. When the submissions happen close together, A arrives with an earlier due time. schedule notifies, the worker's predicate sees a task earlier than the one it was waiting for, and it starts over with A's deadline. If all three submissions finish before the worker runs, it simply finds A first in the heap. This demo illustrates the mechanism; it does not force the worker to be asleep before A arrives.

Other decisions to say out loud: the task runs outside the lock, so a slow task cannot block schedule; the sequence number keeps equal deadlines first in, first out; and top() returns a const reference, so the task is copied out (with move-only tasks, keep a std::vector heap and use std::pop_heap). The heap and its ordering come from the containers lesson and the std::tie comparison from vocabulary types. A full scheduled executor adds periodic tasks, cancellation and several workers; you will build one in scheduled executors and timers.

This compact runner assumes nonempty tasks that finish and do not throw, representable deadlines, and a sequence counter that does not overflow. Destruction must happen outside its worker, after callers have stopped submitting; it drops queued work and joins any running task. Task copies are also assumed to succeed here; an exception escaping the worker calls std::terminate. A production executor must catch task failures and report them, define cancellation, and bound admission. A single slow task delays later tasks even though submissions can still acquire the mutex. std::latch is C++20; in C++17 use a mutex-protected completion count and a predicate wait.

Common mistakes

  • Timing with system_clock or high_resolution_clock. The first can jump; the second may be the first. Use steady_clock for deadlines and elapsed time.
  • Restarting wait_for in a loop. Each wakeup restarts the timer. Compute the deadline once, or use the predicate overload.
  • Treating no_timeout as "my condition is true". It only means the thread was woken. Check the condition, which the predicate overload does for you.
  • Truncating timeouts. duration_cast<milliseconds>(900us) is 0 ms, which becomes a poll and can cause a spin if repeatedly retried. Round up with ceil.
  • Raw integers for time. int timeout in an API invites seconds where milliseconds were meant. Take a duration type.
  • time_point::max() as "no deadline", plus arithmetic. Adding a positive duration to it can overflow the representation. For no deadline, call the plain wait.
  • Sleeping instead of waiting. sleep_for to "give the other thread time" is a race with a guess. Wait on a condition.

In the interview

Time shows up as a follow up ("add poll(timeout)", "add a per-task timeout", "what clock do you use?") or as the whole problem ("implement a delayed scheduler", "a rate limiter", "a TTL cache"). The interviewer listens for absolute deadlines, a monotonic clock, and a budget that wakeups cannot restart.

I keep deadlines as steady_clock time points, because the wall clock can jump during a wait. I compute the deadline once and wait with wait_until and a predicate, so spurious wakeups and notifications for other waiters do not restart the budget. Mutex contention and scheduling can still delay the return. When it returns false, the deadline passed with the condition still false, and I return an empty optional.
The scheduler's worker sleeps until the earliest due time. schedule notifies after pushing, and the worker's wait predicate checks whether the earliest task changed, so a task that is due sooner wakes it up.

Follow ups to expect: "How do you test time-dependent code without sleeping?" (inject the clock; see below), "Fixed rate or fixed delay?" (advance the next deadline by the period, or schedule the next run after this one finishes), "What happens if a task takes longer than its period?" (fixed rate falls behind and must skip or catch up, fixed delay simply shifts), and "Why not one thread per delayed task?" (threads consume resources, and sleep_for has no built-in cancellation; a shared queue makes cancellation and reordering manageable).

Senior depth: make the clock injectable

Hard-coded calls to steady_clock::now() make boundary tests depend on real elapsed time, which can make them slow and flaky. Make it an injectable clock: a template parameter (or a std::function returning a time point), with steady_clock in production and, in tests, a fake clock that you advance by hand. Its fake clock only advances, and is shared mutable state: multithreaded tests must synchronize both advancement and reads, for example with a mutex or atomic tick count. Injecting now() alone does not wake an OS condition-variable wait; scheduler tests must also control the wait mechanism and notify it when fake time advances.

Timeout, cancellation and clocks outside one process

A timeout stops waiting; it does not stop the operation. future::wait_for returning timeout neither cancels its task nor makes get() safe to call without blocking. A deferred future returns deferred immediately from a timed wait; a non-timed wait() or get() executes that deferred work. Releasing the last future from std::async(std::launch::async, ...) may itself wait for task completion, so returning from a timeout branch is not necessarily a fast escape.

Cancellation needs a separate cooperative protocol, such as std::stop_token in C++20, plus a way to wake blocking operations; neither an ordinary sleep nor condition_variable::wait automatically observes a stop request. timed_mutex::try_lock_for and counting_semaphore::try_acquire_for can fail spuriously, so false does not prove that the resource stayed unavailable for the entire interval.

A calendar appointment or a shared expiry timestamp belongs to wall time. A local remaining budget can be translated once into a steady deadline, with range checks, but that chooses not to follow later wall-clock corrections. Across machines, send a remaining budget or a wall expiry with a stated clock-skew policy; never serialize a steady-clock epoch as universal time. Clock tick size is also not measurement accuracy: nanosecond storage does not promise nanosecond hardware resolution or wakeup precision.

Key takeaways

  • Durations carry their unit in the type; integral conversions that discard fractional ticks require an explicit cast or rounding operation. Range still needs checking.
  • system_clock is wall time and can jump; steady_clock never goes backward; high_resolution_clock may be an alias and need not be steady.
  • Elapsed-time budgets use steady_clock. Wall-clock deadlines can stretch or shrink when the wall clock is adjusted; no ordinary timed wait guarantees a punctual return.
  • Compute a deadline once and wait with wait_until (or wait_for) and a predicate; a restarted wait_for has no bound.
  • A scheduler stores absolute due times, sleeps until the earliest, and is notified when an earlier task arrives.

A loop must run a 5 ms job every 20 ms. Version A calls job(); sleep_for(20ms);. Version B keeps next += 20ms; job(); sleep_until(next);. Assume next starts at steady_clock::now(), the job takes exactly 5 ms, and scheduling overhead is negligible. Over 10 iterations, what happens?
Why. sleep_for measures from when it is called, after the job, so every iteration is about 25 ms and the error accumulates. sleep_until targets points on a fixed schedule, so the job's time is absorbed into the period. Checkpoint

Write your own answer first. Then compare with the model answer, or ask Bar Raiser to mark it like an interviewer would.

Write code
Add bool tryAcquireFor(std::chrono::milliseconds timeout) to a counting semaphore built from a mutex, a condition variable and an int permits_. Wakeups must not restart the timeout budget. Explain why a hard upper bound on return time is still impossible with this mutex and ordinary OS scheduling. Assume a nonnegative initial count, no permit-counter overflow, and representable deadlines.
Model answer

The deadline is computed once on steady_clock, before taking the lock, so the time spent waiting for the mutex and every spurious or stolen wakeup all count against the same budget. The predicate is re-checked after each wakeup, and on timeout wait_until checks it one last time, so it can succeed if a permit is available at that final check, even after the deadline. Initial mutex acquisition, reacquisition, and scheduling can delay return beyond the budget; the deadline prevents restarts, not those delays.

Explain
An interviewer asks: "Why does it matter which clock you use for a timeout? Wall time is more accurate, isn't it?" Answer as you would out loud.
Model answer

Accuracy is not the issue; monotonicity is. system_clock is wall time, and it can be adjusted while my thread waits: NTP corrections, an administrator, a VM resuming. A deadline on system_clock means "wake me when the wall clock reads this time", so clock adjustments can stretch or shorten an elapsed wait; the precise response depends on the implementation. steady_clock never goes backward and ticks at a steady rate, which is exactly the property a timeout needs, so I store deadlines and measure elapsed time on it. I also avoid high_resolution_clock, because it may alias system_clock and is not guaranteed steady. The standard says relative waits like wait_for should be measured on a steady clock, so the danger is mainly in deadlines and elapsed times I compute myself. I use system_clock for civil-time events and timestamps that humans or other machines read; those are different contracts from an elapsed-time budget.

Design
Describe how the worker thread of a delayed scheduler waits, and what happens in each case: a spurious wakeup, a new task due earlier than the current earliest, a new task due later, and shutdown.
Model answer

The worker holds the scheduler's mutex and keeps tasks in a min-heap by (due time on steady_clock, sequence number). If the heap is empty, it waits on the condition variable with the predicate "stopping or not empty". Otherwise it reads the earliest due time and waits with wait_until(lock, due, pred), where the predicate is "stopping, or the earliest due time is now earlier than due".

  • Spurious wakeup: the predicate is false and the deadline has not passed, so wait_until simply goes back to sleep until the same deadline.
  • Earlier task: schedule pushes under the lock and notifies. The predicate sees a smaller earliest due time and returns true, so the worker starts over and sleeps until the new deadline.
  • Later task: the notify wakes the worker, the predicate is false (the earliest due time did not change), so it keeps sleeping until the original deadline. An extra wakeup costs a little CPU and nothing else.
  • Shutdown: the destructor sets stopping_ under the lock and calls notify_all; the predicate becomes true, the worker exits, and the destructor joins it. I would state whether pending tasks are dropped, run immediately, or run at their due time, because interviewers ask.

When the wait returns false, the deadline has passed and the earliest task is due: the worker pops it, leaves the lock scope, and runs it outside the lock. A slow task delays other tasks on this worker, but does not hold the scheduler mutex while doing so.

How a senior would say it
I would check whether we restart the budget after each wakeup. I would compute one steady-clock deadline before acquiring the mutex and pass that deadline to the predicate form of wait_until. That prevents notifications and spurious wakeups from buying another hundred milliseconds. For example, if unrelated notifications arrive every ninety milliseconds, a loop that repeatedly calls wait_for with the full duration may never time out. The predicate overload also checks the queue again after reacquiring the mutex, including when the timeout expires. Its result describes that final state, so true does not prove the operation finished before the deadline. Then I would measure lock contention and scheduling delay. An ordinary mutex acquisition is not timed, and a condition-variable wait must regain the mutex before returning. A fixed deadline cannot guarantee that the function returns within a hundred milliseconds. If the contract forbids accepting an item after expiry, I would check the clock under the lock before removing it. I would state that acceptance rule separately from the return-time limitation.

"Why would you choose steady_clock instead of high_resolution_clock, and when would system_clock be the right choice?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Choose steady_clock for elapsed-time budgets because it is guaranteed steady
  • High_resolution_clock promises a short tick period, not steadiness or precise wakeups
  • System_clock expresses wall time and can change with clock corrections
  • Use wall time for calendar events and shared timestamps; do not serialize steady epochs

How a senior would say it
I choose steady_clock for an elapsed-time budget because its readings do not go backward and its tick rate is steady. A name like high_resolution_clock does not give that guarantee. It may alias the wall clock, and a fine tick period says nothing about how promptly the operating system schedules my thread. I use system_clock for a calendar appointment or a timestamp another machine must interpret. That clock can be corrected, so a deadline on it can change its elapsed meaning. For a local request, I store a steady deadline and propagate the remaining budget. I would never send its raw epoch value to another machine as universal time. If suspension matters, I also check whether the platform's steady clock counts suspended time. Those are separate choices: clock meaning, numeric resolution, and wakeup latency.

Out loudabout 60 s
"Your scheduler is waiting for a task ten seconds away. I submit one due in a second. What happens, and how would you test it?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Store tasks in a mutex-protected min-heap of steady deadlines

How a senior would say it
The new task must wake the worker and replace its current waiting deadline. I keep a min-heap of steady-clock due times under a mutex. Submission inserts the task, releases the lock and notifies the condition variable. The worker's timed-wait predicate checks for shutdown or a head earlier than the deadline it captured. If that predicate becomes true, it loops and recomputes the deadline. Later tasks and spurious wakeups do not restart the existing budget. Once a task is due, I remove it under the mutex and invoke it outside the lock. For a deterministic test, I control both the clock and the waiting mechanism, then advance fake time and wake the worker. Injecting the clock alone is insufficient. This single worker can still run late behind a slow callback, so I would separately define task failure, cancellation and shutdown policies.

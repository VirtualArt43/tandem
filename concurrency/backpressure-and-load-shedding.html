Backpressure, bounded queues and load shedding

When work outruns capacity: bound every queue, push back on producers, shed early with 429 or 503, and drop work nobody waits for.

Asked atDatabricks
Before this:Queueing math for design roundsBounded queues, rejection and the ways pools fail

When work arrives faster than you can do it, it has to go somewhere. A system that survives overload decides where in advance: it slows the producers, rejects early, or drops the least important work. A system that never decides puts everything in an unbounded queue, and dies slowly.

In plain words

A popular restaurant on a Saturday night. A good host keeps a waiting list of fixed length, and when it is full she tells new arrivals at the door: "about an hour, the place next door has tables". A bad host seats everyone in the lobby. The kitchen cannot cook any faster, so the lobby fills, people wait two hours, many give up and leave before their name is called, and the kitchen still cooks their orders for empty tables.

The good host is doing load shedding. Telling the regulars' WhatsApp group "we are full, come later" is back-pressure. Cooking for people who already left is zombie work.

Overload is arithmetic

If requests arrive at λ per second and you can finish μ per second, then whenever λ is bigger than μ the backlog grows by λ - μ every second, for as long as it lasts. At 1,200 arrivals per second against a capacity of 1,000, the queue grows by 200 per second: starting empty, after one minute the backlog is about 12,000. With FIFO service and a constant drain rate, a new request waits about 12 seconds.

This is a fluid backlog estimate, not steady-state Little's law; assume no rejection or abandonment. Callers with shorter deadlines will give up before service.

No buffer size fixes a rate problem. A buffer absorbs a burst (a short spell where λ is above μ); it cannot absorb a sustained excess. Under sustained overload you must reduce admitted work or increase service capacity:

  1. Push back: make producers slow down to your pace (block them, or signal them to wait).
  2. Shed: refuse some work, early and cheaply, with a clear signal.
  3. Degrade: do less per request (serve a cached answer, skip the recommendations).
  4. Scale: add effective capacity at the bottleneck, if it can be expanded.

The dishonest answer, "accumulate silently", is what an unbounded queue does.

Bound every queue

An unbounded queue turns overload into two slow failures: memory grows until the process is killed, and latency grows with the queue until every request times out while still waiting. The classic trap in Java is Executors.newFixedThreadPool(n), which uses an effectively unbounded queue: the default LinkedBlockingQueue capacity is Integer.MAX_VALUE. It usually exhausts memory before queue saturation; it still rejects submissions after shutdown. The C++ equivalent is a std::deque behind a mutex with no cap.

A bounded queue needs a policy for "full":

The first four rows are ThreadPoolExecutor handlers. Timed offer is a separate queue API, not an executor rejection policy: inserting through getQueue() bypasses worker creation and lifecycle checks.

Do not block an event loop, or hold a lock consumers need, while waiting for space or running a rejected task. Many caller threads executing inline can also exceed the worker pool's downstream concurrency budget. Silent discard with submit can leave a Future incomplete forever; explicitly fail or cancel work whose result matters.

In C++ you write the same choices into your own queue (these are application-defined methods, not standard-library APIs): try_push that returns false, push_for that waits on a condition variable with a deadline, and a blocking push for producers that should simply slow down.

Size the bound from the queue-wait budget and a byte budget. First subtract service time and other stages from the end-to-end deadline.

If the workers drain 50 requests per second and a request may wait at most 100 ms before it is useless, 50 × 0.1 = 5 is a starting estimate for the queue cap. A FIFO backlog of 10,000 at that unchanged drain rate takes about 200 seconds to reach the tail. These are fluid estimates, not hard delay bounds: service variability, stalls and scheduling require margin and deadline checks.

A cap on queued items does not cap blocked producers, running work or bytes retained by variable-sized requests. Bound admission before allocating large payloads, include active work in concurrency limits, and cap or reject waiting producers. Otherwise pressure merely moves to another queue.

Combine bounded admission with deadline checks, including checks during long operations.

The C++ pool assumes tasks do not throw; a general executor must capture task failures, for example in a std::packaged_task. Its C++20 std::jthread members join before the mutex and queue are destroyed.

In C++17, use std::thread and explicitly join every worker after setting the stop flag and notifying, including on partial construction failure. Callers must stop using the pool before destruction begins.

Reject early, and say so

The cheapest place to refuse a request is the front door, before you parse a large body, take a lock or call a dependency. Rejection should be much cheaper than accepted work; its cost depends on the implementation. A timeout can retain a thread, buffer or connection for the entire wait, and retries may amplify the overload. Say clearly what happened, so clients can react:

  • HTTP 429 Too Many Requests: this client is over its quota or rate. The client should slow down.
  • HTTP 503 Service Unavailable: temporary service inability, such as overload or maintenance; it does not mean every other request fails.
  • Retry-After on either: how many seconds to wait (or a date), as a delay hint. Identical hints can synchronize clients: combine them with jitter, a retry budget and the remaining deadline.
  • In gRPC, quota or resource exhaustion commonly uses RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED; temporary service unavailability uses UNAVAILABLE. Neither code makes retrying a non-idempotent operation safe.

Shed in a deliberate order. The SRE chapter Handling Overload recommends making request criticality explicit. For example, shed batch jobs and prefetches before checkout requests. Degrade where semantics permit: a page without recommendations may be acceptable, while a stale checkout price may not be. Keep liveness checks cheap and distinguish overload from a dead process, so health handling does not turn a load spike into cascading removals.

HTTP 503 and HTTP 429 describe the status semantics.

Backpressure: make the producer slow down

Shedding protects you from callers you do not control. Inside a system where you control both ends, backpressure can be useful: the consumer's slowness travels upstream and slows the producer, provided each intervening buffer and the number of waiting producers are bounded. It happens at every level:

LevelMechanismWhat the producer experiences
Inside a processA bounded BlockingQueue or bounded bufferput blocks until there is room
Async streamsReactive Streams: the subscriber calls request(n) to grant credit for n itemsThe publisher may not send more than it was granted
NetworkTCP flow control: the receive windowOnce send buffers fill, a blocking write waits; a POSIX non-blocking write can return EAGAIN or EWOULDBLOCK
Between services, asyncA log such as Kafka: consumers pull at their own paceProducers are not slowed; consumer lag grows instead
Between services, sync429 or 503 with Retry-After, plus client backoff and jitterThe client waits before retrying

TCP may still send zero-window probes, and writes can transfer only part of a buffer. POSIX would-block errors differ from Java NIO: a non-blocking SocketChannel.write may return zero. TCP flow control bounds transport buffering; application buffers must preserve that pressure.

Netty exposes a related application-buffer signal: when a channel's outbound buffer passes its high water mark, channel.isWritable() turns false, and a well-behaved handler stops reading from its source until the buffered bytes fall below the low water mark and channel.isWritable() becomes true. This reports local buffer pressure, not the peer's receive window directly.

The credit idea behind Reactive Streams is worth seeing on its own: the consumer hands out permission, and the producer may only run ahead by that much.

The shown output is representative of the C++ version, with scheduler-dependent times. Its four permits bound queued plus currently processed items; each completion returns a permit. Java's request(1) instead bounds delivery to onNext, while SubmissionPublisher also buffers items before delivery. Its capacity is per subscriber and may be rounded; demand credit and buffer capacity are separate limits, so the initial burst need not match C++.

Both examples eventually slow the producer to roughly the consumer's pace. SubmissionPublisher.submit can block, so do not call it from the same single-thread executor needed for subscriber progress. C++20 provides std::counting_semaphore; the C++17 fallback is a permit counter protected by a mutex and a condition variable with a predicate. Neither sample implements a general cancellation protocol for blocked producers.

Kafka: when the buffer is the log

A log such as Kafka decouples producers from consumers on purpose: producers append at their own rate, consumers pull at theirs, and the log absorbs the difference on disk. So the pressure shows up as consumer lag: one measure is the log end offset (the next offset to be assigned) minus the group's committed next-to-read offset. That measures distance in offsets, including processed work not yet committed; it need not equal pending record count with compaction or transactions. Under a simple one-record-per-offset model, lag behaves like a backlog.

For one FIFO partition with 120,000 pending records, consumption at 2,000 per second and production at 1,500, an item joining the FIFO backlog waits roughly 120,000 / 2,000 = 60 seconds, while the entire backlog shrinks by only 500 per second and clears in 240 seconds; if producers add 2,000 or more, it never clears.

The levers: add consumers (partition-level parallelism in a conventional consumer group is capped by the partition count), make each message cheaper, or skip work that has gone stale.

Two consumer behaviours matter: exceeding max.poll.interval.ms between polls can cause reassignment; static membership can defer reassignment until the session timeout. Uncommitted work may then be replayed, not necessarily the entire batch exactly twice. pause(partitions) and resume(partitions) control consumption from assigned partitions; keep polling while paused for group progress, and manage commits according to completed processing.

On the producer side, send() can wait for metadata or buffer space within max.block.ms and then fail. Broker quotas and network pressure can also throttle production; slow consumer groups do not automatically slow producers.

Zombie work

Zombie work is work done for a caller who has already given up. For dispensable response computation it is waste, and it can dominate overloaded systems. An accepted durable write or a commit already in progress may still need to finish even if its caller disconnects; the operation's contract decides.

Expired work comes from two places: requests sit in a queue past their deadline and are served anyway, or the caller times out without propagating cancellation.

  • Carry a deadline with every request and check it before starting work and between expensive steps. Inside a process use System.nanoTime() elapsed-time differences or std::chrono::steady_clock. Across machines propagate a remaining budget with transit-time accounting, or a wall-clock deadline with clock-skew handling; never transmit a process-local monotonic timestamp as a shared clock.
  • Bound queue time, not just queue length. A queue whose oldest item is older than the callers' timeout is serving the dead.
  • Propagate cancellation when a client disconnects or a deadline passes (see timeouts and cancellation).
  • Consider newest first under overload. For independent, dispensable requests with similar relative deadlines, last-in, first-out can favour callers still waiting. It can starve old requests and break ordering; cap queue age and use it only when those semantics are acceptable. FIFO with expiry checks is often sufficient.

Worked example: a log writer whose disk stalls

Research reports describe this Databricks logger setting: many threads call log(level, message); a single writer thread batches messages and writes them to disk. Use this practice variation on the reported backpressure follow up: "the disk stalls for two seconds. What happens, and what should happen?"

With an unbounded queue, nothing visible happens for a while: producers keep appending, memory climbs, and if sustained intake exceeds average drain rate, memory can be exhausted and queued messages lost on failure.

The design. Bound both message count and bytes, with illustrative caps such as 100,000 messages and 64 MB; choose actual limits from ingest rate and allowed stall duration. When it is full, choose by level: ERROR and audit messages block the producer with a timeout (offer(msg, 50, MILLISECONDS)), then explicitly fail the call or use a separately bounded durable fallback if admission times out; DEBUG and INFO are dropped at once, counted, and the count is itself logged when the disk recovers ("dropped 18,342 debug messages during a stall"). Expose queue depth, oldest message age and drop counts as metrics.

Reserve capacity or separate queues for critical messages so debug traffic cannot fill their entire budget. On shutdown, stop accepting, then drain with a deadline and report any undelivered work. Never claim a memory enqueue or a timed offer guarantees audit durability.

The senior move is naming the trade-off out loud: you cannot have unbounded intake, bounded memory and zero loss at once when the disk is slower than the producers. Pick which to give up, per message class.

Common mistakes

  • Executors.newFixedThreadPool in a server: an unbounded queue hidden behind a friendly name.
  • A queue bound picked from memory alone: budget both retained bytes and useful waiting time, including active work and blocked producers.
  • Rejecting late, after parsing and after taking locks or connections, so even a rejection is expensive.
  • Retrying a 503 immediately and in sync, ignoring Retry-After, which turns a dip into a storm.
  • Ignoring expiry for dispensable work: check deadlines and propagate cancellation; preserve committed or durably accepted effects.
  • Buffering without limit in an event loop when a socket is not writable, throwing away TCP's backpressure.
  • Treating Kafka as infinite: lag is a queue too, and it has a time limit called retention.

In the interview

Backpressure is a standard follow up whenever you put a queue in a design: "what happens when the queue is full?" and "what if the consumer is slower than the producers?". In Databricks' concurrent log writer questions, back-pressure when the queue fills is one of the reported follow ups, after batching and flushing.

Every queue in the design is bounded, and I size each bound from how long an item can usefully wait: drain rate times that wait. Inside the process, a full queue pushes back on producers, blocking briefly with a timeout. At the edge, when we are full we reject immediately with a 503 and Retry-After, with client retry budgets and jitter, or 429 for a client over its quota, before doing any expensive work. Every request carries a deadline, so workers can skip expired work that the operation contract allows us to abandon, and I would shed by priority, batch and prefetch traffic first. The metrics I would watch are queue depth, age of the oldest item, rejection rate and, for Kafka, consumer lag converted into seconds.

Follow ups: "Block or reject?" (block when the producer can wait without holding resources needed for progress; reject at a boundary with callers you do not control). "What is backpressure in TCP?" (the receive window). "Why not just a bigger queue?" (a buffer absorbs bursts, not a rate gap; bigger only means slower failure).

Senior depth

Metastable failure. Some systems stay overloaded after the trigger is gone. A brief spike causes timeouts, clients retry, the retries keep the queue long, the long queue causes more timeouts, and the loop sustains itself at normal traffic. The 2021 paper "Metastable Failures in Distributed Systems" studies these self-sustaining failure states.

The escape is to break the feedback: shed hard, cap retries with budgets, drop expired work, and recover capacity before letting load back in.

Which queue discipline? First in, first out is fair when the system is healthy. Under overload it serves the oldest requests, the ones most likely to be abandoned. Expiry checks remove useless work regardless of queue order.

LIFO can favour fresher calls but risks starvation; earliest-deadline-first can help when deadline slack matters. Preserve per-key ordering and reserve capacity per tenant or priority before changing discipline. Validate goodput, meaning useful completions before deadlines, together with rejection rate and queue age; successful-request p99 alone hides rejected traffic.

Key takeaways

  • If admitted arrivals exceed capacity without drops, backlog grows; buffers absorb bursts, not rate gaps.
  • Bound queued bytes and items, active work and waiting producers; estimate queue size from drain rate times the queue-wait budget.
  • Push back only where the producer can safely wait; otherwise reject early. Retry-After needs client jitter and retry budgets.
  • Queues, request(n) credits and TCP windows can propagate pressure. Consumer lag records backlog; it does not itself throttle producers.
  • Carry deadlines and cancel dispensable expired work; durably accepted effects may need to finish despite caller timeout.

Quick check

Answer each question, then read why. Your score is saved for your module test.

Requests arrive at 1,200 per second and the service can finish 1,000 per second. The FIFO queue starts empty and rates stay constant. Ignoring rejection and abandonment, how long will a request that arrives after 60 seconds of this wait?
Why. The backlog grows by 1,200 - 1,000 = 200 per second, so after 60 s about 12,000 requests are waiting. Drained at 1,000 per second, the newest waits about 12 s. A sustained rate gap makes waiting grow without limit; no buffer fixes it.
A worker pool drains 50 requests per second, and the queue-wait budget is 200 ms after reserving service and network time. What fluid estimate is a starting queue cap?
Why. The estimate is 50 × 0.2 = 10 queued items. It is not a hard deadline guarantee: leave margin for bursts and variable service, and enforce expiry. One item is a possible stricter policy, not the requested estimate. A 10,000-item backlog takes about 200 s to drain at this rate; memory alone cannot set the latency budget.
Java onlyA running Java executor rejects a task because its bounded queue and workers are full. Which built-in policy directly runs that task on the submitting thread, slowing that producer?
Why. With CallerRunsPolicy, a full pool makes the submitting thread run the task itself, so that producer cannot submit more until it finishes. Multiple producers can still run tasks outside the pool concurrently, and after shutdown this policy discards instead. AbortPolicy sheds by throwing; the two discard policies drop work silently.
A client is over its per-minute quota. A different server is simply overloaded for everyone. Which status codes fit?
Why. 429 Too Many Requests says "you, slow down"; 503 Service Unavailable signals temporary inability to serve this request, often overload or maintenance; other requests may still succeed. Retry-After suggests a delay but does not desynchronize clients; add jitter. 500 reports an unexpected server condition, and 408 means the client was too slow to send its request.
A slow reader stops calling read on its socket, while the writer on another machine keeps writing. Assuming a live TCP connection with no socket timeout, errors or signals, and plain blocking sockets, what eventually happens to the writer?
Why. TCP flow control: the receiver advertises how much buffer space it has; at zero ordinary data transmission pauses, its send buffer fills, and blocking write waits for space. POSIX non-blocking writes instead report EAGAIN or EWOULDBLOCK if they cannot transfer any bytes. Partial writes are also possible; a full buffer does not itself reset the connection or silently drop accepted data. That is backpressure with no application code.
A Kafka consumer group has a backlog of 90,000 records, treating lag as record count with no offset gaps. Producers add 3,000 messages per second and the group consumes 3,600 per second. How long until the lag is gone?
Why. The lag shrinks at the difference of the rates: 3,600 - 3,000 = 600 per second, and 90,000 / 600 = 150 s. Dividing by the consume rate alone (25 s) forgets that producers keep adding. The 30 s option divides by production rather than net recovery. Because recovery here is positive, the backlog clears; if producers matched or beat consumers, it would not.
In the runnable overload demo, which statements remain true if worker scheduling becomes slower? Select all that apply.Select all that apply
Why. A capacity cap limits waiting tasks, not their duration. A task can start before expiry and finish late. Each submission takes exactly one counted outcome, checked after drain and join. Two active tasks plus four queued tasks can leave six outstanding; the active tasks do not occupy queue slots. The deadline check is therefore useful but insufficient for a completion guarantee.

Checkpoint

Write your own answer first. Then compare with the model answer, or ask Bar Raiser to mark it like an interviewer would.

Design
A notification service accepts events over HTTP from many upstream services and sends emails through a provider that allows 500 sends per second. On some days upstream bursts reach 5,000 events per second for ten minutes. Design the overload behavior.
Model answer

The provider caps us at 500 per second, so a ten minute burst at 5,000 per second is 4,500 per second more than we can send, 2.7 million events in ten minutes. This is a long but finite burst, so buffering can absorb it only if storage and allowed delivery delay cover the accumulated work.

If delayed delivery is acceptable, accept at the edge into a durable log (a Kafka topic or queue table), acknowledging only after the required durability level is met. Bound disk use and backlog age too; reject before accepting more work than retention and delivery deadlines permit. That converts the burst into consumer lag on disk instead of memory. Before that, apply per-upstream rate limits and return 429 with Retry-After to any single upstream that exceeds its share, so one noisy service cannot starve the rest; if the log itself is unhealthy, return 503.

Senders consume from the log and call the provider through a rate limiter set just under 500 per second, with a concurrency limit as well, so a slow provider cannot pile up in-flight calls. Lag in seconds is the key metric: 2.7 million events at 500 per second takes 90 minutes to drain only if new arrivals stop. If the baseline continues at b events per second below 500, catch-up takes 2.7 million / (500 - b) seconds; at or above 500 it never clears.

Because emails go stale, every event carries a deadline or a priority: use separate bounded queues or topics and a priority scheduler if password resets and receipts must bypass bulk work; marketing and digest emails are delayed or dropped when the lag passes a threshold, and dropped counts are recorded.

Retries of failed sends use backoff with jitter and a retry budget, never a tight loop. All attempts consume provider rate capacity. Deduplicate event intake and use provider idempotency keys when available: a timeout may hide a successful email send. And if the backlog is predictable, we ask the provider for a higher rate or a second provider, while retaining overload protection during failures.

Write code
Write the submit side of a bounded work queue that waits up to a timeout for space and otherwise rejects, so callers get a fast, explicit answer. Java: use BlockingQueue. C++: use a mutex and condition variable.
Model answer

The bounded wait is the compromise between pure backpressure (block forever) and pure shedding (reject instantly): it can smooth a short burst, but is not a hard end-to-end timeout. Lock acquisition, scheduling and the service itself can add delay. Choose the queue-wait budget from the caller's remaining deadline, then check expiry again after admission.

In C++, wait_for with a predicate handles spurious wakeups and returns false if the predicate is still false at its final timeout check; notification under the mutex is also correct, although a woken thread may have to wait briefly for the mutex. The C++ type requires non-throwing moves to keep removal safe.

These fragments show admission, not shutdown: a full implementation needs a closed flag, rejection after close and wakeups for blocked producers and consumers, and must outlive its callers.

  • Measure useful completions, rejection rate and queue age
How a senior would say it
I would bound admission and shed excess work before it consumes expensive resources. With no rejection or abandonment, the backlog grows by two hundred requests a second. After a minute that is twelve thousand requests. At an unchanged thousand completions a second, a new FIFO arrival waits roughly twelve seconds. A larger queue cannot fix that sustained rate gap. I would budget queued items and bytes from the useful waiting time, after reserving time for service, and limit active work as well. At an external boundary I would reject cheaply with an overload response. Inside the system I could make producers wait briefly, provided they are not event-loop threads or holding locks the consumers need. Every request should carry a deadline, and workers should skip dispensable expired work. Accepted durable effects need their own completion contract. Clients need retry budgets, backoff and jitter, because synchronized retries can keep the service overloaded. I would measure useful completions before deadlines, rejection rate and queue age while testing recovery.

Out loudabout 60 s
"A Kafka consumer is falling behind. Is that backpressure, and how do you work out recovery time?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Consumer lag records backlog; it does not automatically slow producers
  • Distinguish offset lag from record count and delayed commits
  • For 120,000 pending records, consumption 2,000/s and production 1,500/s, catch-up takes 240 seconds
  • For one FIFO partition, an item joining its backlog waits roughly 60 seconds at that consumption rate
  • Bound storage and age, manage consumer progress and use safe replay semantics
How a senior would say it
Consumer lag is backlog, not automatic backpressure on producers. Producers can keep appending while a slow consumer falls further behind. I would first check what the lag metric measures: committed offsets can trail completed processing, and offset distance is not always a record count. Suppose one FIFO partition has a hundred and twenty thousand pending records. If consumers handle two thousand a second while producers add fifteen hundred, we recover at only five hundred a second. Catch-up takes two hundred and forty seconds. That differs from the roughly sixty seconds an item joining the current FIFO backlog waits. I would bound retained bytes and message age, then improve processing or add consumers where partition ownership allows it. Pausing consumption can protect a local worker queue, but the consumer still needs to poll for group progress. Finally, replay and timeout handling must not duplicate unsafe effects.

The sleeping barber and the unisex bathroom

Bounded admission, lost wakeups, graceful shutdown and fairness policies for a sleeping barber and a bathroom shared by incompatible groups.

Asked atRubrik
Before this:Condition variables and monitorsProducer and consumer with a bounded bufferReaders and writers, three waysLivelock, starvation and fairness

Rubrik interview reports describe a bathroom that two groups may not share, with a capacity of three and a fairness follow up, and it has handed candidates a broken barber shop to debug. Together the two puzzles cover resource admission: a server that sleeps when idle, and a room with rules about who may share it.

In plain words

A neighborhood barber shop has one barber, one barber's chair and three chairs for waiting. When nobody is waiting, the barber naps in his own chair. A customer who finds him asleep wakes him; a customer who finds all three waiting chairs taken walks away. The famous bug: the barber glances at the empty waiting room and decides to nap, and in the second before he sits down a customer walks in, sees the barber awake, and sits quietly to wait. Now the barber sleeps and the customer waits, forever.

The office bathroom has three stalls and is shared by two teams who must never be inside together. Rules are easy until people keep coming: if red team members keep arriving while red is inside, blue may wait all day. Both puzzles are about one decision made atomically, "may I go in now, or do I wait", and about never letting a waiter be forgotten.

The sleeping barber

The sleeping barber problem has these rules: one barber, N waiting chairs; when no customer is waiting, the barber sleeps; an arriving customer who finds every waiting chair taken leaves; otherwise the customer sits down, wakes the barber if he is asleep, and waits for a haircut. Haircuts happen one at a time, and a customer leaves only after their own haircut.

The naive version and its lost wakeup

The bug in the story is a check then act on "is anyone waiting?" followed by "go to sleep", done as two separate steps:

The fix is the rule from the lost wakeup lesson: check the condition and go to sleep under one lock, in a loop, so a customer cannot change the predicate between the check and the atomic release-and-wait operation. In C++ that is the predicate form of wait. The deliberately broken fragment also mishandles spurious wakeups: it could subtract from an empty waiting count.

The barber as a monitor, with closing time

A complete answer adds two things interviewers ask for next. Each customer must leave only after their own haircut, so the customer waits for a "done" signal tied to them; here every seated customer holds a ticket, haircuts finish in ticket order, and a customer waits until the finished ticket reaches theirs. And the shop must close cleanly: nobody new sits down, seated customers are still served, and a sleeping barber is woken so he can go home.

For these samples, chair counts and room capacities are positive, visits finish, and ticket counters do not wrap. Admission is measured when a caller acquires the monitor lock. The barber sample queues every accepted customer before service; supporting zero waiting chairs would require a separate direct handoff to an idle barber. One barber serves tickets in order, and accepted customers wait uninterruptibly for completion; Java interruption may cancel lock acquisition before admission.

The barber cuts hair outside the lock, so customers can sit down meanwhile. Two conditions keep the signals targeted: the barber sleeps on customerWaiting, customers wait on haircutDone, and since several customers wait there for different tickets, finishing a haircut uses signalAll (notify_all in C++). Printed counts depend on scheduling; sleeps make overlap visible and are not correctness requirements. This demo uses close() to stop the barber, not interruption.

A real haircut failure needs a terminal failure result for the affected customer, rather than leaving their wait unsatisfied.

The insight: it is producer and consumer with a "leave if full"

Step back and the shop is a bounded queue. Customers are producers who try to add themselves without blocking and walk away if it is full; the barber is a consumer who sleeps on an empty queue. That suggests the second technique: give each customer their own "done" signal and put it in a bounded queue. The barber takes the next signal, cuts, and completes it. It also explains why the classic three semaphore solution (a count of seated customers that the barber sleeps on, a semaphore with which the barber calls the next customer, and a mutex for the count) needs care: a semaphore permit is not addressed to anyone, so "your haircut is done" can be picked up by the wrong customer unless you add a per customer rendezvous.

This fragment isolates per-customer completion and omits admission shutdown. Stop new arrivals and drain accepted work before stopping its worker. offer does not wait for a free queue slot, but it can wait briefly for the queue's internal lock; it is not a lock-free operation. C++ uses get() so a broken promise or an explicitly stored failure is reported. With multiple barbers, a single highest-finished ticket is unsafe because haircuts can finish out of order; per-customer completion still works.

Seen this way, a shop with several barbers is a thread pool: barbers are workers, the waiting chairs are the task queue's capacity, and a customer walking away is the pool's rejection policy. The same failure modes apply, which is why bounded queues and rejection is the natural next read.

The barber shop Rubrik hands you to debug

A reported 2026 Rubrik debugging round gave a candidate about 180 lines of a multithreaded barber shop with shared chairs, brushes, scissors and combs, and asked them to fix deadlocks and an incorrect shutdown. Expect two families of bug. A barber who needs two tools is a philosopher who needs two forks: if one barber takes scissors then comb and another takes comb then scissors, they can deadlock, and the fix is one global order for the tools (see the dining philosophers). And shutdown bugs: a closed flag that sleeping threads never see because nobody signals them, a wait predicate without || closed, or customers left waiting forever because the barber went home without serving or dismissing them. The closing rule above handles those shutdown cases with one barber and successful service. With several idle barbers, wake all of them on close, and track completion separately for each customer.

The unisex bathroom

The rules of the unisex bathroom: one bathroom with C stalls (Rubrik uses 3), two groups of people (men and women in the textbook, Democrats and Republicans in Rubrik's version), and never both groups inside at once. That is group mutual exclusion: like a lock, except that members of the same group may share. It generalizes ideas you already know: if every person were their own group it would be a plain mutex, and readers-writers is the case where all readers form one group and every writer is a group of one.

The obvious monitor, and who it starves

The first correct-looking answer is a monitor whose enter condition is: there is space, and the room is empty or holds my group. In code, wait while !(inside < capacity && (inside == 0 || groupInside == g)). It never mixes groups and never overfills. But it is reader preference with two kinds of reader:

Rubrik's follow ups are about exactly this. The fix is a fairness policy, and there are several, each with a cost. Choosing one and naming its cost is the senior part of the answer.

Fairness policy 1: first come, first served

Hand out tickets under the lock and let people in strictly in ticket order. The person at the head of the line enters as soon as there is space and the room holds their group or is empty; when they enter, the next ticket is served, so a run of same group people at the head enters together. After ticket assignment nobody can be overtaken. Starvation freedom assumes every visit ends and every eligible thread eventually runs and acquires the lock; the mutex itself does not promise scheduler fairness or a wall-clock waiting bound. The cost is head of line blocking: if the head of the line is blue and red is inside, the reds behind that blue person wait too, even though there is space.

The checker sits outside the bathroom's lock on purpose. Each person counts themselves in after enter returns and out before calling leave, and looks at the other group's count while inside. A nonzero other-group count proves a violation, but a bad admission can be missed if a thread pauses before updating its external counter. These are stress checks, not proof; deterministic tests should also gate arrivals, record admission order under the monitor lock, and check the invariant at each admission. The Java version waits uninterruptibly and preserves interrupt status because a ticket that leaves the line would block everyone behind it. Supporting cancellation means removing or recording abandoned tickets under the same lock, skipping them whenever they reach the head, and waking waiters. Pair every successful enter with leave in finally in Java or an RAII guard in C++, including when the visit throws.

Fairness policy 2: batches that switch

To keep more stalls busy, let groups take turns in batches: once the other group is waiting, stop admitting new members of the group inside; when the room empties, reserve as many admissions for the other group as it has waiters at that moment. That finite quota forms the next batch and may be used even though the first group is waiting again. This version counts slots rather than identifying people, so newcomers from the chosen group can take a slot before older waiters.

The first instinct, "never enter while the other group is waiting", hides a deadlock. With an empty room and both groups waiting, each side politely yields to the other, and nobody ever goes in. Every turn taking design needs a tie breaker for the empty room; here it is the group whose batch it currently is.

Groups are numbered 0 and 1 in this implementation. Cancellation is excluded: abandoning a promised slot without updating the waiting count and batch quota could prevent a switch.

This is fair between the groups: once both sides are waiting, neither group can admit an unbounded number of people before the other gets a turn, assuming finite visits and eventual scheduling. It does not guarantee fairness between individuals: a red who arrives during red's batch may take a batch place ahead of a red who waited longer, who might lose again in every later red batch. If the interviewer wants both, combine it with tickets: a batch is "every ticket of that group issued before the switch".

Fairness policy 3, and the policies in one table

A third family limits how long a group may hold the room while the other waits: at most K more admissions, or stop admitting after a T millisecond deadline, then switch when empty. Existing occupants are not evicted, so T does not bound total occupancy time. A timer is needed if the deadline itself must trigger a state transition while nobody calls the monitor. It is simple to bolt onto the obvious monitor and gives a tunable trade-off.

Worked example: one morning, three policies

Capacity 3. During a short arrival burst, six people acquire the monitor lock in this order: red 1, red 2, blue 1, red 3, red 4, blue 2. No visit completes until all six have requested entry. Treat admission and departure overhead as negligible, and every visit as one time unit. Who is inside in each unit?

The obvious monitor keeps every stall it can busy, but blue 1, who arrived third, goes in last, and with a suitable steady stream of overlapping reds could wait forever. First come, first served is perfectly fair and needs four units, because each blue at the head of the line blocks the reds behind it. Batches that switch finish in three units: when blue 1 arrived, red 3 and red 4 were held back, and when the room emptied, both waiting blues went in together.

Rubrik's variants: "the most eligible person" and the team playground

One Rubrik version asks you to admit "the most eligible waiting person" whenever there is room, with each person's visit taking a time that depends on their name. Clarify what "eligible" means: oldest request globally, or oldest request compatible with current occupants. These are different policies. Choosing only the oldest currently compatible request is the obvious monitor with a queue, so it can starve the other group; add the batch switch (stop admitting your group once the other has waited) and you have a defensible answer.

Because visit times are known, you may be asked about shortest job first: shortest job first minimises average waiting time for a single server with a fixed set of available jobs and known durations. That theorem does not directly apply to three stalls with group constraints; favouring short eligible visits is a heuristic here and can starve long visits. Aging raises priority with time, but still needs a policy that eventually makes the group eligible.

The team playground (reported at Rubrik in 2025) has many teams, at most 10 players inside, one team at a time, and asks for several ways to prevent starvation. Everything above generalizes: tickets work unchanged with a team number instead of a group; batch switching hands the playground to the team of the longest waiting player; a batch or admission deadline limits new entries before a handoff, but does not bound how long existing players stay. Have three ready, with their costs.

Common mistakes

  • The barber checks, then sleeps. Any gap between "nobody is waiting" and "wait" loses a wakeup. Check and wait under one lock, in a loop.
  • A customer who leaves before the haircut. "Seated" is not "served". Each customer must wait for a signal that is about their own haircut.
  • Shutdown that nobody hears. Setting closed = true without signalling leaves a sleeping barber asleep; a wait predicate without || closed ignores it even when signalled.
  • Capacity and group checked in two steps. "If the room is empty or mine, then take a stall from the capacity semaphore" lets a red and a blue both see an empty room and both enter. The group check and the admission must be one atomic decision under one lock.
  • Yielding with no tie breaker. "Never enter while the other group waits", applied by both groups to an empty room, deadlocks. Someone must have the turn.
  • Signalling one waiter with mixed groups waiting. notify can wake a person from the wrong group, who waits again while the right one sleeps. Use notifyAll, or targeted conditions with enough wakeups for eligible waiters; strict ticket order needs the head ticket specifically.

In the interview

This is Rubrik's territory. The political bathroom (capacity 3, never both parties, others queue, admit the most eligible waiting person) was reported in a 2024 G5 multithreading round, where the candidate was first asked to explain operating system resource allocation policies with pros and cons and then implement one (they chose first come, first served), and again in a May 2025 concurrency round. A 2025 L4 round asked the team playground (one team at a time, at most 10 players) with a follow up on several ways to prevent starvation. A 2026 Rubrik debugging round handed over a barber shop simulation with deadlocks and an incorrect shutdown.

What the interviewer listens for: exclusion and capacity decided atomically, a named fairness policy with its cost, and no lost wakeups.

I will write it as a monitor: one lock, the count inside, the group inside, and the waiters. A person enters when there is space and the room is empty or holds their group. That alone lets one group starve the other, so I will add a policy: once the other group is waiting, stop admitting my group, and when the room empties hand it to everyone the other group has waiting, with a tie breaker so two waiting groups cannot deadlock. If strict fairness per person matters more than throughput, I would switch to tickets, at the cost of head of line blocking.

Follow ups to expect: "Who can starve now?" "What if there are 10 teams instead of 2?" "Each person's visit time is known; does that change your policy?" "How does the barber go home at closing time?" "How would you test that groups never mix?" (an independent counter per group checked from inside, many threads, random arrival order, and ThreadSanitizer for C++).

Senior depth

Name the scheduling policy you are choosing. Rubrik's prompt about operating system resource allocation is a hint that this is a scheduling problem. First come, first served is fair with head of line blocking; batching trades fairness per person for throughput; rotating bounded admission batches resembles round robin, but occupants are not preempted; shortest job first has an average-wait optimum in the fixed, single-server model; with ongoing arrivals it can starve long jobs, and group constraints require additional admission rules. Saying which one you picked, and what it costs, is the senior signal.

The same shape appears in real systems. Database lock managers decide admission with a compatibility table (shared locks share, exclusive locks do not) and must avoid starving exclusive requests behind a stream of shared ones. A device or connection that can serve only one tenant's workload at a time, with batches per tenant, is the bathroom with tenants as groups. And the barber is every bounded worker pool that rejects when full.

Waking everyone has a cost. These monitors use one condition and notifyAll because waiters have different predicates. With hundreds of waiters, each change wakes all of them, and most go back to sleep. Per-group conditions reduce unrelated wakeups, but waiters must still check capacity. Strict ticket order needs a targeted signal to the head ticket or a broadcast: signalling an arbitrary same-group waiter can still strand the eligible ticket.

Key takeaways

  • The sleeping barber's famous bug is a lost wakeup: checking "anyone waiting?" and sleeping must be one step under one lock.
  • The barber is producer and consumer with "leave if full"; each customer needs their own done signal, and closing means refuse new, serve seated, wake the sleeper.
  • The bathroom is group mutual exclusion: decide "space, and empty or my group" atomically.
  • Without a policy one group can starve the other. Tickets are fairest, batches that switch keep stalls busy, limits are tunable; every turn taking rule needs a tie breaker for the empty room.
  • With many teams the same three policies apply; name the one you pick and its cost.

Quick check

Answer each question, then read why. Your score is saved for your module test.

The barber locks, sees waiting == 0, unlocks, and then locks again to call wait(). A customer arrives between the two locks, increments waiting and calls notify(). Assume no later arrival or interruption. What can happen?
Why. Condition variables do not remember notifications. Because the check and the wait were two separate critical sections, the customer's signal fell in the gap. Checking and waiting in one loop under one lock closes the gap. A spurious wakeup may happen but cannot be relied on. The customer has already been accepted, and the barber owns the monitor when calling wait, so neither departure nor a monitor-ownership exception follows.
You model the barber shop with Java's ArrayBlockingQueue (or your own bounded queue). Which enqueue operation reports a full room with false, without waiting for capacity?
Why. A customer who finds no free chair must leave, not wait, so the enqueue must not wait for capacity. put would turn the waiting room into an unbounded line on the pavement. add throws when full rather than returning false; take removes an item and is not an enqueue. None of this promises lock-free access to the queue.
A bathroom monitor admits a person when inside < 3 && (inside == 0 || groupInside == g), with no other rule. Red members arrive continuously and overlap. A blue member is waiting. What happens to them?
Why. The rule is reader preference with two groups: same group arrivals keep joining while the room is theirs. It is safe (never mixed, never over 3) but not live for the other group. A fairness rule must eventually stop new red admissions while blue waits. One departure need not empty the room, and capacity never permits mixed groups.
Both groups adopt the rule "never enter while someone from the other group is waiting". The room is empty, and one red and one blue are waiting. What happens?
Why. Symmetric politeness with no tie breaker is a deadlock. A turn taking design needs a deciding value for the empty room, such as whose batch it is. Arrival order is not consulted by this rule, both predicates are false, and no automatic alternation exists.
A first come, first served bathroom with capacity 3 is empty. The line is red 1, red 2, blue 1, red 3, with tickets assigned in that order. Assume nobody leaves and eligible entrants run. Who enters before the first departure?
Why. Ticket order lets red 1 and red 2 in together. Blue 1 is next and must wait for an empty room, and red 3 may not overtake blue 1 even though a stall is free. That is head of line blocking, the price of ticket-order fairness. Red 3 cannot bypass blue 1, blue 1 cannot mix with red, and red 2 need not wait for red 1 to leave.
Spot the bug: if (groupInside == -1 || groupInside == g) { capacity.acquire(); synchronized (this) { inside += 1; groupInside = g; } }, where capacity is a semaphore with 3 permits.
Why. It is a check-then-act across two steps. Both people read groupInside as empty before either writes it. Deciding "space, and empty or my group" must be one atomic step under one lock, together with updating the state. Changing the permit count does not repair that decision; inside += 1 is already protected by the monitor, and making only the counter atomic would not repair the group check.
Rubrik's team playground has many teams, a limit of 10 players inside, and one team at a time. Assume finite visits, eventual lock acquisition and no cancellation. Which policy guarantees no overtaking after admission tickets are assigned?
Why. Tickets bound the number of admissions ahead of each ticket, and therefore prevent starvation under the stated progress assumptions; they do not give a wall-clock bound. Largest team first can starve small teams; random choice provides no worst-case progress bound; the obvious monitor starves anyone not inside. The cost of tickets is lower throughput from head of line blocking.

Checkpoint

Write your own answer first. Then compare with the model answer, or ask Bar Raiser to mark it like an interviewer would.

Write code
Implement Rubrik's team playground: players of many teams call enter(team) and leave(); at most capacity players are inside; only one team is inside at a time; no team and no player may starve.
Model answer

Admission is strictly in ticket order. With positive capacity, finite visits, no abandoned tickets or counter wrap, and eventual lock acquisition, every issued ticket progresses after finitely many earlier tickets. This is an admission-order guarantee, not a maximum duration for waiting. Consecutive players of the team inside enter together up to the capacity. The trade-off to state: a player of another team at the head of the line blocks everyone behind them until the playground empties. If throughput matters more, switch to batches: when the playground empties, give it to the team of the oldest waiting player and admit that team's waiting players as one batch.

Design
Before you code the political bathroom, the interviewer asks: "Explain some resource allocation policies, with pros and cons, and pick one." Answer as you would out loud.
Model answer

First come, first served: hand out tickets and admit in order. Pro: nobody starves and it is easy to reason about. Con: head of line blocking, because a person of the other party at the front leaves stalls empty while others wait.

Batching or turn taking: let one party in until the other party is waiting, then switch when the room empties and admit the other party's waiting people as a batch. Pro: can improve use of the three stalls by grouping compatible visits. Con: fair between parties but not strictly per person, and it needs a tie breaker for the empty room or it can deadlock.

Admission windows or a batch limit: stop new admissions after K entries or a T second deadline while the other party waits, then switch once the occupants leave. Pro: one tunable knob. Con: K trades batching against waiting; a T second admission deadline does not evict existing occupants, so it is not a wall-clock waiting guarantee.

Shortest visit first: since each visit time is known from the name, admit short visits first. Pro: can reduce average wait, though the single-server optimality proof does not cover group constraints and multiple stalls. Con: long visits can starve; aging and a fair group-selection rule are needed.

I would pick batching with a tie breaker if group fairness and utilisation are the goals. I would clarify whether fairness must apply to each individual; a slot-count batch alone does not guarantee that. If the interviewer says strict fairness per person matters most, I would switch to first come, first served.

Debug
A barber shop's close() sets closed = true under the lock and returns. After it is called, the program never exits: the barber thread is stuck. Find two possible causes and fix them.
Model answer

First cause: nobody wakes the barber. He is asleep on the "customer waiting" condition, and setting a flag does not wake anyone. Fix: close() must signal that condition (signal, or notify_one in C++) after setting the flag, under the lock.

Second cause: the barber's wait predicate ignores the flag. If he waits while (waitingRoom.isEmpty()), he goes back to sleep even when woken. Fix: wait while the room is empty and the shop is not closed, and after waking return "go home" when the room is empty and closed. That keeps the promise to customers already seated (they are still served), refuses new ones (the customer side checks closed), and lets the barber thread end so join returns.

What a strong answer covers
  • A notification is not stored: checking empty, unlocking and then waiting loses the customer's signal
  • Check the queue and wait in a loop under one lock; waiting atomically releases the lock
  • Remove a customer under the lock, perform the haircut outside it, then publish that customer's completion
  • Closing rejects new arrivals, wakes the barber and drains accepted customers before exit
  • Completion by highest ticket assumes one barber; multiple barbers need per-customer completion
How a senior would say it
The barber has lost a wakeup. He checks an empty queue, releases the lock, and decides to sleep. In that gap a customer queues and signals. Nobody is waiting yet, so the signal disappears. The barber then sleeps without checking again. I would put the queue predicate and the wait in one loop under one lock. Waiting atomically releases that lock, so a customer cannot slip between the check and registration of the wait. A wakeup only means check again; it does not reserve a customer. The barber removes one customer under the lock, cuts hair outside it, then records completion under the lock. Each customer waits for their own completion, because being accepted is not the same as being served. To close, I set a closed flag under the same lock, reject new arrivals and wake the barber. He drains accepted customers and exits only when closed and empty. The owner joins him before destroying the shop. With one barber, tickets finish in order. With several barbers, I need a separate completion result for each customer because later tickets may finish first. That keeps admission, service and shutdown promises separate and testable.

Likely follow up: What if the haircut throws, or an accepted customer wants to cancel?

Out loudabout 90 s
"Design a bathroom with three stalls, two groups that cannot mix, and no starvation. Why does your fairness policy work?"
What a strong answer covers
  • One monitor protects occupancy, the current group and ticket state
  • Admit only the next ticket when there is capacity and the room is empty or contains that group
  • Advance the ticket on entry and wake waiters; consecutive compatible tickets may share
  • Progress assumes finite visits, eventual lock acquisition and no abandoned tickets
  • Explain head of line blocking and distinguish group batching from individual fairness
  • Cancellation removes or skips abandoned tickets; successful admission always requires leave
How a senior would say it
I would use one monitor and admission tickets. The monitor protects the number inside, the current group and the next ticket to serve. A caller takes a ticket under the lock and waits until it is their turn, a stall is available, and the room is empty or contains their group. On entry I advance the ticket and wake waiters, so consecutive people from the same group can share the room. On departure I reduce occupancy, clear the group when empty and wake waiters again. Nobody can overtake an issued ticket. If visits finish and eligible threads eventually acquire the lock, each ticket has only finitely many people ahead and eventually enters. That is not a wall-clock bound or a promise about operating system scheduling. The cost is head of line blocking. A blue person waiting behind two reds prevents later reds from using the third stall. Batching can improve utilisation, but counting batch slots alone does not guarantee fairness to each individual. I would also define cancellation explicitly: an abandoned head ticket must be removed or skipped under the lock. Every successful entry must have a matching departure, even when the caller's work throws.

Likely follow up: How would you support cancellation without leaving a hole in the ticket sequence?

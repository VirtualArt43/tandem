Your study plan

A ten week plan for two people studying together: the daily and weekly rhythm, when to start problems, tests, mocks, revision and the last week.

Before this:Welcome to TandemHow top companies test concurrency

A plan you can keep beats a perfect plan you abandon in week three. This one is built for two working people studying the same course in two languages, about 90 minutes on weekdays and a few hours at weekends, for ten weeks.

In plain words

Think of two friends training for a half marathon. They run short and steady on weekdays, do one long run at the weekend, track every run in a notebook, and ease off in the last week so they arrive fresh. Neither one sprints for a week and then stops for two.

Studying for interviews works the same way. Small daily sessions, one longer weekend block, steady revision, and a calm last week beat heroic weekends every time, because what decides an interview is what you can recall under pressure, and recall is built by repetition spread over time.

The shape of the plan

Tandem's Concurrency section has 120 course lessons, a 26 lesson refresher for each of you, more than eighty practice problems, module tests, timed drills, spoken questions, the theory bank and mock interviews. Not all of it is needed on a first pass: each week below lists must do problems (about forty in total) and if time problems, so you can match the plan to your target companies.

Budget about 12 to 15 hours a week each:

  • Weekdays, 90 to 120 minutes: revision, two or three lessons, some code, and ten minutes together.
  • Saturday, about 3 hours: the week's problems and module tests.
  • Sunday, 2 to 3 hours: a pair session, mocks from week 4, and planning.

If you have fewer hours, stretch the plan to twelve weeks by splitting weeks 5 and 9, the heaviest. If you have only eight weeks, see the compression at the end of the table.

The daily rhythm

  1. 10 minutes: revise. Open Logbook's "Revise today" list. Notes you marked revise later: come back after 1, 3, 7 and 21 days. Press "Quiz me on these" and answer Bar Raiser's questions on them.
  2. 45 to 60 minutes: two or three lessons. Predict before you press Run. Answer the quick check without scrolling back. Write your checkpoint answer before opening the model answer, and ask Bar Raiser to mark the ones you are unsure of. Then record one of the lesson's "Say it out loud" questions on the Speak page; Bar Raiser scores what you said and how you said it.
  3. 30 minutes: code. The checkpoint's code question, the lesson's key sample rewritten from memory, or one of the week's problems. Always in your own language, always run.
  4. 10 minutes together: teach back. Each of you explains one idea from today in two minutes; the other asks one follow up from that lesson's "In the interview" box. Save any sentence that worked as an interview line: note.

The weekly rhythm

  • Saturday, about 3 hours. The week's must do problems: work for 20 minutes before opening the first hint, open hints one at a time, and only then the solution. Take the module test for every module you finished; the target is 90 percent. From week 3, take one mixed test. From week 4, add one timed drill from the Practice page (15 to 25 minutes, online assessment style, answers hidden until the end), and one run of the phone screen simulator on the Speak page.
  • Sunday, 2 to 3 hours. A pair session: one of you interviews the other on a problem for 45 minutes, using the follow ups on its page, then swap. From week 4, each of you also runs one Bar Raiser mock in the Interview room. End by writing Logbook's weekly digest and agreeing next week's targets.

When to start problems

  • Every problem names the lesson that teaches it. Do the lesson first; a problem attempted without its lesson turns into guesswork. One deliberate exception: the ordering puzzles in weeks 3 and 4 (print in order, foobar, zero even odd) name lesson 12-01, but a lock, a condition variable and a turn variable from modules 04 and 05 are enough, so solve them that way and redo one with semaphores after reading 12-01 in week 7. The executor with wait for all is taught by 17-02, so the table moves that lesson into week 5.
  • Bug hunts first. The first two, in week 2, need only module 03.
  • The bounded blocking queue after module 05. It is one of the most widely reported concurrency coding problems (LinkedIn, Rubrik, Bloomberg, Microsoft and others) and the heart of every thread pool. Do it in week 3, then again from memory in week 9 and in the final week.
  • After you pass a problem, redo it from scratch two days later. The first pass teaches you the solution; the second proves you can produce it. Ask Bar Raiser to review your passing code: it often finds the follow up you were not ready for.
  • Time yourself from week 5. A medium problem in about 30 minutes and a hard one in about 45 to 60 is the interview pace.

Module tests, mixed tests and revision

Take each module test when you finish the module. Below 90 percent, read the "why" of every question you missed, turn each one into a mistake: note, and take the test again two days later. The mixed test draws 20 questions from everything you have finished, so it is the honest check that early modules have not faded; do one every week from week 3.

Revision is the part people skip first and regret most. Protect the ten daily minutes even on a bad day. Spaced revision is what turns a lesson read once in week 2 into an answer you can give without thinking in week 10.

Mock interviews

Bar Raiser runs mocks from the Interview room: pick a company style, a length and a focus, and you get a timed round in the side panel with a scorecard at the end. Schedule them like this:

  • Week 4: the first one, 30 minutes, coding focus, "Any top company". It will feel rough; that is the point.
  • Weeks 5 to 8: one a week each, in your target company's style, rotating the focus: coding, rapid fire theory, concurrency in system design, debugging.
  • Week 9: two each, full length. Week 10: three each.

After every mock, copy each item from the scorecard's "what was missing" into Logbook as a mistake: or revise later: note, and fix the biggest one before the next mock. Pair mocks on Sundays add what Bar Raiser cannot: a real person interrupting you.

Using Bar Raiser and Logbook well

  • Bar Raiser: "Test me on this lesson" after each lesson you found hard; "What can this print?" and "Spot the bug" for ten minutes when you have them; "Drill my weak spots" every Saturday; checkpoint marking whenever you are not sure your answer would satisfy an interviewer.
  • Logbook: one mistake: note for every wrong quick check answer, saying why the right answer is right; revise later: for anything shaky; doubt: for anything to ask Navigator or each other; interview line: for sentences worth saying again. Speak notes on a walk if typing is a chore; Hinglish is fine.

The last week

  • No new lessons. New material in the last week mostly adds anxiety.
  • Every day: one timed problem from memory (the bounded queue, the pool with shutdown, the scheduled executor, the job runner, the log writer, the crawler) or one full mock; one mixed test; the Logbook revision list.
  • Read the module 18 pages: the final checklist, the phrasing bank, the company playbooks, and your cheat sheet (C++ or Java).
  • Prepare one deep project story about concurrency or performance you have handled at work: the problem, the shared state, what broke, what you measured, what you changed.
  • The day before: a light review of your interview lines, and rest. Sleep is worth more than one more problem.

If you fall behind: keep the daily revision and the must do problems, skip lessons marked Stretch on the first pass, and slide the plan by a week rather than skimming. Never skip modules 05, 09 and 17; they carry the most asked coding problems.

Worked example: a Rubrik loop in six weeks

Suppose Rubrik books Shubhi's loop six weeks from the day you start. Candidate reports describe its System Coding rounds clearly: usually two per loop, 60 minutes each, code that must compile and run, and often no ExecutorService or BlockingQueue, so you build the queue, the pool and the "wait for all" yourself from locks and condition variables. There is often a separate debugging round on someone else's threaded code. So the plan keeps its daily rhythm and changes its order.

  • Weeks 1 to 3: unchanged. The refresher, foundations, races, locks and the bounded queue are the base of every Rubrik problem.
  • Week 4: pools without libraries. Module 08, then 09-01, 09-02, 09-05, 09-07, 09-08 and 17-02 (11 lessons). Problems: semaphore, latch, thread pool, executor with wait for all, scheduled executor.
  • Week 5: the rest of a Rubrik round. 06-01 to 06-04, 07-01, 10-02, 10-04, all of module 11, then 17-03 and 17-04 (15 lessons). Problems: LRU cache, fix the transfer deadlock, dependency job runner, debug the cinema.
  • Week 6: no new lessons. The bounded queue, the pool with shutdown, the job runner and the scheduled executor from memory, 45 minutes each, and two Bar Raiser mocks in Rubrik style, one coding and one debugging.

Modules 12 to 16 and the rest of 07, 09, 10 and 17 wait until after the loop; if a week runs light, pull forward the Rubrik problems from weeks 7 and 9, each with its lesson. Notice that every moved lesson still comes after the lessons it builds on. That is the one rule reordering never breaks: change the order to fit the round you face, but never attempt a problem before you have learned what it stands on.

Common mistakes

  • Binge reading at weekends instead of daily sessions. Five short sessions beat one long one for memory, and they keep the two of you in step.
  • Skipping revision when busy. Ten minutes a day is cheap; relearning a forgotten module is not.
  • Solving problems with the solution tab open. It feels productive and teaches recognition, not recall. Twenty honest minutes first, then the hints, one at a time.
  • Postponing mocks until the end. Mocks reveal what you cannot yet say under time pressure. Start in week 4, while there is time to fix it.
  • Studying alone in parallel. The teach-back and the pair mock are where most of the benefit of studying together comes from.
  • Never timing yourself. A correct solution in 90 minutes still fails a 60 minute round.

In the interview

Everything in this plan rehearses three things an interview tests: recalling an idea without notes, saying it clearly under time pressure, and writing code that runs. The quick checks and spaced revision train recall, the teach-backs and mocks train speaking, and the timed problems train working code. When a mock goes badly, do not just redo the problem; find which of the three failed and practise that one.

Let me restate the problem and the constraints, name the shared state and the invariant I need to keep, pick the simplest primitive that keeps it, write it, and then walk through the interleaving that would break a naive version.

Say that opening in every mock until it is automatic. It buys you thinking time and shows the interviewer a structure from the first minute.

Key takeaways

  • Ten weeks at 12 to 15 hours each: weekdays of 90 to 120 minutes, a problem block on Saturday, a pair session and mocks on Sunday.
  • Every day: revise, two or three lessons, some code, ten minutes of teaching each other.
  • Problems follow their lessons: bug hunts after module 03, the bounded queue after module 05, about forty must do problems in all.
  • Module tests to 90 percent, a weekly mixed test from week 3, mocks from week 4.
  • The last week: no new lessons, timed redos, mocks, checklists and rest.

Quick check

Answer each question, then read why. Your score is saved for your module test.

When should you make your first attempt at the bounded blocking queue problem?
Why. The problem needs locks (module 04) and condition variables with the wait loop (module 05). Attempting it earlier turns it into guesswork; attempting it only at the end leaves no time to redo it from memory. It is reported at LinkedIn, Rubrik, Bloomberg and Microsoft among others, so it is worth doing three times.
You score 72 percent on a module test. What should you do next?
Why. Retaking at once mostly measures short term memory of the answers. Understanding each miss, logging it, and testing again after a gap shows whether the idea has actually stuck. Rereading everything wastes time on what you already know.
You are stuck on a practice problem. What is the plan's approach?
Why. The struggle before the hint is where the learning happens, but endless struggle wastes time; graded hints keep you working on the problem yourself. The redo two days later is what turns a solution you have seen into one you can produce in an interview.
What does the plan recommend for the final week before interviews?
Why. The last week converts what you know into what you can produce under pressure. New material adds anxiety and little recall; timed practice, mocks and revision sharpen exactly what the interview will test.
You are a week behind the plan. Which response fits the plan best?
Why. Skimming produces lessons you have "read" but cannot use, and dropping revision lets earlier modules fade. Stretch lessons are designed to be optional on a first pass, and modules 05, 09 and 17 carry the most asked problems, so they are the last things to cut.

Checkpoint

Write your own answer first. Then compare with the model answer, or ask Bar Raiser to mark it like an interviewer would.

Numbers
You have nine weeks and about 12 hours a week each. Budget the hours: lessons, problems, revision and mocks. Does the plan fit, and what would you cut first if it did not?
Model answer

Total: 9 × 12 = 108 hours each.

  • Lessons: 120 course lessons at about 30 minutes each including the quick check and checkpoint is about 60 hours; the 26 refresher lessons at about 25 minutes each add about 11 hours. Around 71 hours.
  • Must do problems: about 40 problems at 40 to 50 minutes on average, including the redo, is about 30 hours.
  • Revision: 10 minutes a day for 63 days is about 10 hours.
  • Mocks and pair sessions: about 10 Bar Raiser mocks at about 45 minutes with the review is about 8 hours, and a 90 minute pair session on each of 9 Sundays is about 13 hours. Around 21 hours.

That is about 130 hours against 108 available: over by about 22 hours, nearly two weeks of this budget. The plan as written needs about 14 to 15 hours a week. I would cut in this order: the refresher lessons whose quick check I pass before reading (a few hours); one 45 minute pair interview each Sunday instead of two, swapping roles week to week (about 7 hours); lessons marked Stretch, if my path has any (most lessons are Core or Senior, so this saves little); and the "if time" problems, which are already excluded above. That still leaves me 10 to 15 hours short. I would not cut revision, the must do problems in modules 05, 09 and 17, or the mocks, because those are what the interview actually measures. So the honest fix is about 13 hours a week or a tenth week, not skimming.

Explain
Your partner says: "I'll just reread every lesson the weekend before the interview; that's more efficient than all this daily revision." Explain why the plan disagrees.
Model answer

Rereading makes material feel familiar, but an interview does not ask whether something looks familiar; it asks you to produce it from nothing, under time pressure, out loud. That needs recall, and recall gets stronger each time you successfully pull something out of memory, especially when the attempts are spread out and some forgetting has happened in between. A single reread of everything just before the interview gives one weak exposure per topic, at the moment you have the least time to fix what is missing.

The plan's pieces each target recall: quick checks before scrolling back, checkpoint answers written before the model answer, revise later notes returning after 1, 3, 7 and 21 days, problems redone from scratch two days later, and teach-backs where you explain an idea without notes. Writing code from memory and running it adds the second thing interviews test, code that compiles. So daily retrieval is not extra work on top of learning; it is the learning.

Say it out loud

Interviews are spoken. Answer each question out loud as if the interviewer just asked it. Tandem transcribes you on this Mac, word for word, measures your pace, fillers and pauses, and Bar Raiser scores what you said and how you said it.

Out loudabout 90 s
"Here is the problem: a bounded blocking queue, where put blocks when it is full and take blocks when it is empty. Before you write any code, talk me through how you will approach it."
What a strong answer covers
  • Restate the contract and ask the clarifying questions: capacity, block forever or time out, shutdown, which libraries are allowed
  • Name the shared state: the buffer and its count
  • State the invariant: size between zero and capacity, first in first out, nothing lost or duplicated
  • Pick the simplest primitive and say why: one lock with two conditions, not full and not empty
  • Every wait sits in a while loop, shown with the interleaving that breaks an if
  • Say how you will test it: several producers and consumers, every item out exactly once
How a senior would say it
First I would restate it and pin down the contract. What is the capacity? Should put and take block forever or take a timeout? Do we need shutdown? And may I use the library queue, or should I build it from a lock and conditions? Then I name the shared state, which is just the buffer and its count, and the invariant: the size stays between zero and the capacity, items come out in the order they went in, and nothing is lost or duplicated. The simplest primitive that keeps that is one lock with two conditions, not full and not empty. put takes the lock, waits while the queue is full, adds the item and signals not empty. take mirrors it. Every wait sits in a while loop. To show why, I walk the naive version with an if: two consumers wait on an empty queue, one item arrives, both are woken by a notify all or a spurious wakeup, the first takes it, and the second takes from an empty buffer. Then I write it, and test it with several producers and consumers, checking that every item comes out exactly once.

Likely follow up: How would you add a shutdown that releases every blocked thread?

Out loudabout 90 s
"Tell me about a concurrency or performance problem you handled at work. What was shared, what broke, and how did you fix it?"
What a strong answer covers
  • The context in two sentences: the system, its scale, and why the problem mattered
  • The shared state and the rule it had to keep
  • The symptom, and the evidence that found the cause: a thread dump, a profile or a metric
  • The fix, the trade off chosen, and the option rejected
  • The result in numbers, and what now stops it coming back
How a senior would say it
The clearest one was in a metrics ingestion service that handled about twenty thousand requests a second on two hundred threads. Every request read a tenant's settings from an in memory cache, and the cache sat behind one lock. The symptom was that p99 latency jumped from about forty milliseconds to two seconds every few minutes, with no rise in traffic. I took a few thread dumps during a spike. Most request threads were blocked on that one monitor, and the thread holding it was refreshing a tenant's settings over the network while still holding the lock. So one slow call stalled the whole service. The fix was to keep the network call out of the lock. The refresher builds a new immutable settings map without the lock, then swaps one volatile reference, and readers take no lock at all. I considered a read write lock, but it only helps once the writer stops calling out while holding it, and after that change a single reference swap is simpler and lets readers skip the lock entirely. p99 went back to about forty five milliseconds. I also added a metric for lock hold time and a code review rule: no input or output while holding a lock.

Likely follow up: What would you design differently if you built that service again?

Out loudabout 90 s
"You are 35 minutes into a 60 minute round. Your queue hangs in your own test and you cannot see why. Talk me through what you do."
What a strong answer covers
  • Keep talking: say what you are checking so the interviewer can help
  • A hang means a thread waits for a signal that never comes, or threads wait on each other's locks
  • Get evidence: a thread dump with jstack in Java, every thread's stack in the debugger in C++
  • Check the usual causes: a signal on the wrong condition, a lock not released on an error path, two locks in different orders
  • After about five minutes, fall back to the simplest correct version and get it working
How a senior would say it
First, I keep talking, because silence wastes the interviewer's chance to help me. A hang means some thread is waiting for something that will never happen, so I look for who is waiting and on what. In Java I take a thread dump with jstack and look for threads waiting on a condition, and threads blocked on a lock that another thread holds. In C++ I pause it in the debugger and print every thread's stack. Then I check the usual causes in order. A signal sent on the wrong condition, for example put signalling not full instead of not empty. A lock taken by hand and not released when something throws, which is why I use try and finally, or a guard. Two locks taken in different orders. If I have not found it in about five minutes, I fall back to the simplest version I trust: one lock, notify all after every change, and every wait in a while loop. A correct simple queue at minute 45 beats a clever one that hangs, and I tell the interviewer which optimization I would add back with more time.

Likely follow up: Your fixed queue now uses notify all everywhere. What does that cost, and when is a single notify safe?

Building H2O

Ordering puzzlesmedium30 minLeetCode 1117LinkedIn

Threads arrive one at a time and must leave in groups of exactly two hydrogens and one oxygen. LinkedIn has used it in its concurrency round (a 2024 senior candidate in Bangalore reported getting no hints and getting stuck), and it is the template for every "form a group, then let it go" problem (group formation): a barrier with a quota per kind.

The task

Many threads share one H2O object. Each hydrogen thread calls hydrogen() once and each oxygen thread calls oxygen() once. Think of the method as a barrier: a thread may not pass until it can form a water molecule with two others, two hydrogens and one oxygen. Passing means calling your release callback (releaseHydrogen.run() or releaseOxygen.run() in Java, releaseHydrogen() or releaseOxygen() in C++) and returning.

  • An oxygen that arrives when no hydrogen is waiting must wait for two hydrogens.
  • A hydrogen that arrives alone must wait for another hydrogen and an oxygen.
  • A third hydrogen may not join a molecule that already has two; it waits for the next molecule.
  • All three callbacks of one molecule must return before any callback of the next molecule begins. So the output, read in groups of three, is always two H and one O in some order: HHO, HOH or OHH.

For example, with 4 hydrogen and 2 oxygen threads arriving as O H H H O H, a valid output is HOH HHO (spaces added for reading). HHH OHO is wrong: its first group has no oxygen.

Callback contract: each invocation calls its own callback exactly once, on the calling thread, and returns only after that callback returns. A callback may pause before or after producing its output. The three callbacks in a complete molecule must be able to run concurrently, including waiting for their partners to enter their callbacks. Do not hold a lock across a callback or serialize callbacks. Separate H2O objects coordinate independently.

Rules: threads arrive in any order and any number at once. A waiting thread must block, not spin. No sleep to form the groups. The tests always supply exactly two hydrogens per oxygen in total per object, but at any moment the waiting atoms may be lopsided. Callbacks do not throw or reenter the object, and threads are not interrupted or cancelled. FIFO admission is not required. In C++, ThreadSanitizer must stay quiet.

What the tests check

  • Incomplete molecules block: a lone oxygen releases nothing during an observation window, and adding one hydrogen is still insufficient. Two hydrogens also wait for oxygen. Java samples thread states; C++ samples process CPU use and context switches to detect sustained spinning.
  • Quotas and reuse: three hydrogens and one oxygen release exactly one molecule, leaving one hydrogen waiting. A fourth hydrogen and another oxygen then complete a second molecule.
  • Every invocation owns one callback: count each supplied callback separately and check the executing thread. A duplicate callback cannot compensate for a missing partner callback.
  • Slow callbacks keep the next molecule out: pause a hydrogen or oxygen before output and after output. All three callbacks of the first molecule must enter, while no callback of the next molecule may begin until the paused callback returns. A separate object must complete a molecule during the pause.
  • Stress: 1, 2, 5, 20 and 50 molecules, up to 150 workers, with seeded shuffled starts, hydrogen starts first and oxygen starts first. These are launch orders, not promises about the OS scheduling order. Java also uses a common start gate in alternating rounds. C++ reuses workers across rounds. Every group of three releases is two H and one O, and every invocation makes exactly one callback.
  • Bounded completion, no unexpected exceptions and, in C++, no ThreadSanitizer report. Timed joins or completion waits and a process watchdog turn a hang into a failing test.

Taught in: H2O and group formation problems

Open them one at a time. Each one gives away a little more.

Picture a table with three seats: two marked H and one marked O. An atom needs a free seat of its own kind, then must wait until the table is full. What should happen to atoms that arrive while the table is full?
When the table fills, its three atoms release. Nobody new may sit down until all three have released, or the next molecule's output could slip in between. Keep a count of atoms that still have to release (leaving); the last one to release clears the table and wakes the waiters.
With library helpers: a semaphore with 2 permits for the H seats, one with 1 permit for the O seat, and a barrier of 3 so the seated atoms wait for each other. Release your seat only after your callback returns. In Java, Semaphore and CyclicBarrier; in C++20, std::counting_semaphore and std::barrier.

What the interviewer asks after your first version works. Try to answer each one out loud before you open it.

Why is a semaphore of 2 for hydrogen and 1 for oxygen not enough on its own?
The semaphores only cap how many atoms of each kind are in flight. Nothing makes an atom wait for its partners: two hydrogens with no oxygen anywhere would release at once, and a new hydrogen could take a freed seat and release before the oxygen of the first molecule did. You need a barrier, a point where the three seated atoms wait for each other, and the seats must be given back only after the callback returns, so the next molecule cannot overtake.
Why must the next molecule wait until every atom of this one has released?
Because the output is checked in consecutive groups of three. If a new hydrogen could sit down the moment the table filled, its release could land between this molecule's releases. The reference solution closes the gap with a leaving count; the semaphore version closes it by releasing each seat only after that atom's callback returns.
Generalize it to H2SO4: two hydrogens, one sulfur and four oxygens per molecule.
Only the quotas change. Keep a required count and a seated count per kind (H 2, S 1, O 4); an atom waits for a free seat of its kind in a molecule that is still forming; the molecule is complete when every kind is full (7 atoms), and then leaving starts at 7 and the last atom out clears all the seats. The semaphore version needs one semaphore per kind with its quota as permits and a barrier of 7. An Amagi interviewer asked for exactly this generalization, with working code, in 2024. The bug to avoid is a completion check that forgets one of the kinds.
Can an atom starve?
Yes, in principle: starvation is possible. With non-fair locks and conditions, a hydrogen that has waited a long time can lose the race for a free seat to newer hydrogens again and again. To prevent newer waiters overtaking older ones of the same kind, use a fair semaphore (new Semaphore(2, true) in Java), or a ticket counter per kind. This still assumes matching atoms eventually arrive, callbacks return and threads get scheduled; fairness alone gives no wall clock bound. Fairness costs throughput, so say when you would pay for it.
What if a seated atom's thread is interrupted while it waits for its partners?
Cancellation is outside this task's tested contract; the reference does not recover from it. Before the molecule is complete, roll back the seat under the same lock, signal waiters and propagate the interruption. Once the molecule is complete, simply dropping that atom would strand its partners and leave the departure count stuck. Choose an explicit policy: finish the committed molecule and restore the interrupt flag, or break the entire generation and notify every affected waiter. A CyclicBarrier uses a broken state: an interruption while waiting breaks the barrier and other waiters receive BrokenBarrierException. It remains broken until reset; a reset or replacement needs coordination with all participants.

Try for at least twenty minutes first. Reading a solution feels like learning; writing one is learning.

The idea

A molecule is a table with seats per kind. An atom waits for a free seat of its kind in a table that is still forming, sits, and then waits until the table is full. The atom that fills the last seat sets leaving = 3 and wakes everyone; the three atoms release outside the lock; the last one to release clears the seats for the next molecule. The invariants: at most 2 hydrogens and 1 oxygen are seated, nobody sits while leaving > 0, and the seats are cleared only after all three have released. Together they make every group of three consecutive releases a whole molecule.

Why each line is there

  • One lock, one condition, signalAll. Different kinds of waiter (atoms waiting for a seat, seated atoms waiting for the molecule) share the condition, so waking one could wake the wrong one.
  • The seat wait includes leaving > 0. While a complete molecule is still releasing, the table is closed. This is what keeps molecules from interleaving.
  • leaving records the committed molecule. A seated atom waits for leaving > 0. That count stays positive until this atom itself has finished its callback, so even a slow waiter cannot miss completion. The seat counts also stay full throughout departure; the separate leaving count makes the admission and departure phases explicit. This avoids needing a separate generation counter in this implementation.
  • Release outside the lock. A paused callback cannot keep its partners from entering their callbacks. No callback runs while holding the lock. The departure count is decremented after the callback returns, including any work after it prints.

Why this cannot interleave: the next molecule needs both hydrogen seats and the oxygen seat, and each of them is handed back only after a callback of this molecule has returned. So the next barrier cannot trip until this molecule is completely out.

Pitfalls

  • Semaphores without a barrier. Caps the kinds in flight but never makes atoms wait for partners; the lone-oxygen test fails at once.
  • Letting hydrogens release before the oxygen arrives (for example, each hydrogen releases and then hands the oxygen a permit). The output groups can even look right, yet two hydrogens pass the barrier with no oxygen in sight. The "two hydrogens wait" test catches it.
  • Matching totals while losing callback identity. A leader cannot invoke its own hydrogen callback twice in place of its partner's callback. Two visible H characters do not prove that both hydrogen invocations released once.
  • Clearing the seats as soon as the table is full. The next molecule then sits down while this one is still releasing, and groups interleave.
  • notify with one shared condition, and waiting with if: both lose or misuse wakeups among the different kinds of waiter.

Cost and limits

The reference keeps O(1) counters and synchronization objects, excluding the waiting threads themselves. Each arrival and departure changes O(1) state, but a broadcast can wake O(W) waiters to recheck their predicates when W threads are waiting. Admission is not FIFO, and individual starvation is not ruled out under an unbounded stream of new arrivals.

How to say it in the interview

This is a barrier with quotas. I'll keep seat counts per kind and a leaving count under one lock. An atom waits for a free seat of its kind in a molecule that is still forming, sits, and waits until the molecule is complete; whoever fills the last seat sets leaving to three and signals all. The three release outside the lock, and the last one out clears the seats, so the next molecule can only form after this one has fully left. With library helpers it is two semaphores for the seats and a cyclic barrier of three, giving each seat back only after the callback returns. The same shape handles any formula, H2SO4 included: only the quotas change.

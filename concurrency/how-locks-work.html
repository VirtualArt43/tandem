How locks are built: CAS, spinning, parking and futexes

Every lock is an atomic word plus a way to sleep. How spinning, parking and futexes combine, what AQS is, and what a lock really costs.

Asked atMicrosoftDatabricksRubrik
Before this:Mutexes and RAII guardstryLock, timeouts, interruptible locking and fairness

"How does a lock actually work?" separates engineers who use locks from engineers who can reason about them. The answer is short: an atomic instruction on one word of memory, plus a way to put losing threads to sleep without missing the moment they should wake. Everything else, including what a lock costs, follows from those two parts.

In plain words

Think of a single-occupancy washroom on a train, with an "occupied" indicator that flips when you slide the bolt. Sliding the bolt and seeing whether it was already slid happen in one motion: two people can never both think they got in. That one motion is the atomic instruction.

If it is taken, you can stand at the door and keep rattling the handle (spinning): you get in the instant it frees up, but you can do nothing else, and you block the corridor. Or you can sit down in your seat and ask the person inside to tap your shoulder when they leave (parking): restful, but it takes you a few seconds to get up and walk back. Good locks do both: rattle a few times, because most visits are short, then sit down.

Why a plain flag cannot be a lock

The obvious attempt is a boolean: wait until it is false, then set it to true. It fails for the same reason as every check then act race: the check and the set are two steps, and another thread can run between them.

Making the flag volatile or atomic does not help on its own, because the problem is the gap between the read and the write, not visibility. A practical lock needs one indivisible step that both looks and changes, and hardware provides exactly that.

Strictly, mutual exclusion from reads and writes alone is possible: Peterson's algorithm does it for two threads, and Lamport's bakery algorithm for any number. But both rely on "I write my flag, then I read yours" never being reordered, which real CPUs do not promise for plain loads and stores (see reordering), and the bakery needs a slot per thread. So they need sequentially consistent atomics or full fences anyway, and every real lock uses the hardware's atomic read-modify-write instead. If an interviewer asks "can you build a lock without CAS?", that is the answer.

The atomic step: test-and-set and compare-and-swap

  • test-and-set (an atomic exchange): write "taken" and get back the old value, in one step. If the old value was "free", you own the lock. Java: AtomicBoolean.getAndSet(true). C++: std::atomic<bool>::exchange(true) or std::atomic_flag::test_and_set().
  • compare-and-swap (CAS): "if the word still holds the value I expect, replace it with my new value, and tell me whether it worked". More general: it can move a word between any states, such as free, locked, and locked with waiters. Java: compareAndSet. C++: compare_exchange_strong and compare_exchange_weak.

On x86 these compile to XCHG and LOCK CMPXCHG; on ARM64 to load-exclusive and store-exclusive pairs, or to the single SWP and CAS instructions added in ARMv8.1. On x86 the core holds the cache line exclusively for the whole step; with an exclusive pair, the store fails if another core wrote the line in between, and the code loops to try again. Either way, no other core can act between the look and the change. A lock must also order memory: taking it is an acquire (nothing in the critical section moves above it) and releasing it is a release (nothing moves below it). That is how a lock makes the previous holder's writes visible to the next one; the details are in C++ memory orders.

A spinlock, done properly

The simplest real lock: loop on test-and-set until it succeeds. The naive loop has a performance bug. Every failed test-and-set is a write, and a write needs the cache line in exclusive mode, so spinning threads drag the line back and forth between their cores, slowing everyone, including the holder trying to release. The fix is test-and-test-and-set: spin on an ordinary load (still an atomic or volatile read, just not a read-modify-write), which hits each core's own shared copy of the line and costs no traffic while the lock stays held, and only attempt the atomic write when the lock looks free.

The C++ class has lock() and unlock(), so it satisfies the standard's BasicLockable requirements and works with std::lock_guard. The acquire and release orders are what make the plain count safe: without them this would be a data race. Randomized backoff after a lost race, and exactly when spinning beats blocking, are covered in spinlocks.

Why spinning alone is not enough

A spinlock is great when the lock is held for less time than it takes to put a thread to sleep and wake it up. It is terrible otherwise:

  • A spinning thread burns a whole core doing nothing, and with more runnable threads than cores it steals CPU from the very thread holding the lock.
  • If the holder is descheduled (its time slice ends, or it waits for a page to be read from disk), every spinner wastes its entire time slice, possibly milliseconds, waiting for a thread that is not even running.
  • Under strict real-time priorities, a high-priority spinner on the same core as a low-priority holder can keep the holder off the CPU forever.

So real locks need a second mechanism: when waiting will take a while, take the thread off the CPU entirely and wake it when the lock is released. That is parking, and the hard part is doing it without a race between "I am about to sleep" and "the lock was just released".

Parking without losing the wakeup: futexes

Linux solved this with the futex ("fast userspace mutex"). The lock is still one word in ordinary user memory, manipulated with atomics. The kernel is involved only when a thread must sleep or be woken, through two operations:

  • wait(address, expected): put me to sleep, but only if the word at this address still equals expected. The kernel performs that check and the sleep as one step, so if the holder released the lock a nanosecond earlier and changed the word, the call returns at once instead of sleeping through the release.
  • wake(address, n): wake up to n threads sleeping on this address.

The classic futex mutex (from Ulrich Drepper's paper "Futexes Are Tricky") uses three states: 0 free, 1 locked, 2 locked with possible sleepers. An uncontended lock and unlock never enter the kernel; a waiter announces itself by writing 2 so the releaser knows a wake-up call is needed. C++20's std::atomic::wait and notify_one give portable access to the same mechanism (a futex on Linux, WaitOnAddress on Windows, the __ulock calls on macOS), so the C++ side below is Drepper's futex mutex, written portably. The Java side builds the same thing on AbstractQueuedSynchronizer, which supplies the queue and the parking.

Spin, then park

Production locks combine the two mechanisms. A thread that finds the lock taken spins for a short, bounded time, because many critical sections finish in less time than a sleep and wake round trip, and only then parks. The spin budget is tuned to roughly the cost of parking: spin longer than that and you waste CPU, park sooner and you pay a context switch for a lock that was about to be free. Some locks adapt the budget at runtime, spinning longer when spinning recently succeeded: adaptive spinning. HotSpot's synchronized does this, AQS spins a little when a woken waiter at the head of its queue loses the race, glibc offers it as the opt-in PTHREAD_MUTEX_ADAPTIVE_NP mutex type, and common implementations of std::atomic::wait poll briefly before calling into the kernel.

Not every platform mutex spins, though. On Linux, std::mutex is a thin wrapper over glibc's default pthread_mutex_t, which uses the same three states as the futex mutex above: when its CAS fails it marks the word 2 and goes straight to the futex wait. Say "may spin briefly, then parks", not "always spins first".

Java's AbstractQueuedSynchronizer

Most of java.util.concurrent is built on one class, AbstractQueuedSynchronizer. Its design is exactly the two parts of this lesson, packaged for reuse:

  • One int state, read and changed with CAS. Each synchronizer decides what the number means.
  • A FIFO queue of waiting threads (a variant of the CLH queue lock), where a thread that cannot acquire enqueues itself and parks with LockSupport.park(). A release unparks the next thread in the queue. On Linux, HotSpot implements park with a pthread mutex and condition variable, so it too ends in a futex wait.
  • Hooks you override: tryAcquire and tryRelease for exclusive mode, tryAcquireShared and tryReleaseShared for shared mode. AQS handles the queueing, parking, timeouts, interruption and condition queues.
  • Condition queues: each newCondition() gets its own queue. await() saves the state, releases the lock completely and parks; signal() moves the first waiter from the condition queue to the lock's queue, so the woken thread must win the lock again before await() returns. You will lean on this in condition variables.

Under the hood: what about synchronized?

synchronized does not use AQS; the JVM implements it directly, with the same two ingredients. Every object can act as a monitor with an entry set (threads waiting to acquire it, shown as BLOCKED in a thread dump) and a wait set (threads that called wait()). For the common uncontended case HotSpot avoids building a full monitor at all: it records ownership in the object's header with a single CAS, and only "inflates" to a full monitor structure when threads actually contend or someone calls wait(). You may also read about biased locking, an older trick that skipped even that CAS for a single repeat owner: it was disabled by default in JDK 15 and later removed, and Java 21 does not have it. Treat these as names to recognise; interviews care about the guarantees, the entry and wait sets, and the costs below.

What a lock costs

These are orders of magnitude for modern hardware, not benchmarks; your numbers will vary, but the gaps between rows will not.

SituationWhat happensOrder of magnitude
Uncontended lock and unlockOne or two atomic instructions on a cache line this core already ownsTens of nanoseconds or less
Contended, resolved by spinningThe lock's cache line moves between cores, perhaps several timesHundreds of nanoseconds
Contended, waiter parksA system call to sleep, a context switch, a wake-up, and cold caches on returnMicroseconds per hand off
Holder descheduled while others spinSpinners burn their time slices until the holder runs againUp to milliseconds

You measured two of these rows already. In the fairness demo, the unfair lock did about 36 million acquisitions a second across four threads, under 30 ns each, because it rarely had to wake anyone. The fair lock, which must wake a parked thread on every hand off, managed about 400,000 a second: roughly 2.5 µs per hand off. The cost of a lock is almost entirely the cost of contention. An uncontended lock is nearly free; do not avoid locks out of fear, avoid contended ones.

Worked example: what happens inside lock() under contention

An interviewer asks: "Three threads call lock() on the same ReentrantLock (or a Linux std::mutex) at once. Walk me through it." A strong answer, step by step:

  1. All three try a CAS on the lock's state word, from free to held. Exactly one succeeds, say A; it returns immediately, having done no system call.
  2. B and C fail the CAS. They may spin briefly, re-reading the word in their own caches, in case A finishes quickly. (HotSpot and AQS do a little of this; glibc's default mutex under a Linux std::mutex skips it.)
  3. A is still inside, so B and C record that waiters exist (the futex mutex sets the word to 2; AQS links them into its queue) and park. For the futex mutex, the kernel takes them off the CPU only after confirming the lock word still says "held". AQS gets the same guarantee from LockSupport's permit: an unpark that arrives before the park makes the park return at once. Either way, a release in between cannot be missed.
  4. A unlocks: a release store to the word, and because waiters exist, one wake-up call. A returns at once; it does not wait for B to run.
  5. B wakes after a few microseconds and retries the CAS. With an unfair lock, a running thread (even A again) may have taken the lock in the meantime, and B parks again. With a fair lock, newcomers may not take a free lock while B is queued ahead of them, so the lock waits for B.
  6. Memory: A's writes inside the critical section happen before A's release store, and B's successful CAS is an acquire, so B sees everything A wrote.

Common mistakes

  • A lock made of a plain flag. Check-then-set is two steps. Use an atomic exchange or CAS for the acquire, even in "simple" code.
  • Spinning on an atomic write. A loop of failed test-and-set or CAS operations keeps the cache line bouncing between cores. Spin on a read, and attempt the write only when the lock looks free.
  • Spinlocks in ordinary application code. With more threads than cores, or a holder that can block or be preempted, a spinlock wastes CPU and can be far slower than a mutex. Use the platform mutex unless you have measured otherwise.
  • Forgetting memory ordering in a hand-made lock. A lock must acquire on lock and release on unlock. In C++, relaxed operations here make the protected data a data race; in Java, the atomic classes give you the right ordering.
  • "Locks are slow, so I will avoid them." Uncontended locks cost tens of nanoseconds. The expensive part is contention, and the cure is shorter critical sections or less sharing, not clever lock-free code.

In the interview

The internals come up as theory follow ups and as build-it tasks. Microsoft reports include questions on how synchronized and volatile work down to the CPU caches, and a request to write a lock library without built-ins; a Databricks-tagged commenter says CAS questions are now common there; Rubrik interviewers have asked how CAS works on the way to a lock-free stack. "Implement a semaphore, a latch or a lock from scratch" is the build-it form.

A lock is an atomic word plus a way to sleep. Acquiring is a CAS from free to held, which is an acquire for memory ordering; releasing is a release store. When the CAS fails, the thread may spin briefly, because many critical sections are shorter than a context switch, and then parks. On Linux parking is a futex wait, which sleeps only if the word still says "held", so a release cannot slip in between the check and the sleep; the unlocker issues a futex wake only if it knows there are waiters. That is why an uncontended lock costs a couple of atomic instructions and a contended hand off costs microseconds.
In Java, ReentrantLock, Semaphore and CountDownLatch all sit on AbstractQueuedSynchronizer: one int state changed by CAS, a FIFO queue of parked threads, and per-synchronizer rules for what the state means.

Follow ups to expect: "Why test-and-test-and-set?", "When is a spinlock the right choice?", "How does the futex avoid a lost wakeup?", "What is the state in a CountDownLatch?", "Can you build a lock without CAS?" (Peterson or bakery, and only with sequentially consistent atomics), and "What does a contended lock cost compared with an uncontended one?"

Senior depth

  • The three-state trick has a small price. A thread that wakes up re-acquires by writing 2, not 1, because it cannot know whether other sleepers remain. Its own unlock may then make one unnecessary wake-up call. The design accepts that to keep the uncontended paths at one atomic instruction each.
  • Queue locks spread the spinning. In a test-and-set lock every waiter watches the same word. Queue locks such as MCS and CLH give each waiter a flag that no other waiter watches (its own queue node in an MCS lock, its predecessor's node in CLH), so a release touches one waiter's cache line instead of all of them. AQS's queue descends from CLH, adapted for parking rather than spinning.
  • Wake-up policy is a throughput decision. Waking one waiter and letting running threads barge in keeps the lock busy; handing the lock directly to the woken thread is fair but leaves the lock idle during every wake-up. That is the fairness trade-off from the tryLock lesson, seen from the inside.
  • Priority inheritance. Real-time systems use mutexes that temporarily raise the holder's priority to that of its highest-priority waiter, so a medium-priority thread cannot keep the holder off the CPU. You will meet the failure it prevents in priority inversion and convoys.

Key takeaways

  • A lock is an atomic read-modify-write on one word (test-and-set or CAS) plus a way to put losers to sleep and wake them.
  • Spin on reads, not writes (test-and-test-and-set), and spin only briefly; then park.
  • Futex-style waiting sleeps only if the word still holds the expected value, so no release is missed; the kernel is involved only under contention.
  • Java's AQS is the same design: an int state changed by CAS, a FIFO queue of parked threads, and per-synchronizer meanings for the state.
  • Uncontended locks cost tens of nanoseconds; contended hand offs cost microseconds. Fight contention, not locks.

Quick check

Answer each question, then read why. Your score is saved for your module test.

A lock is written as while (locked) {} locked = true; with locked declared atomic (or volatile). What is wrong?
Why. Atomicity of each read and each write does not make the pair atomic: it is check-then-act. The acquire must be one indivisible read-modify-write, such as exchange(true) (test-and-set) or a CAS from false to true.
On Linux, roughly what does locking and then unlocking an uncontended futex-based mutex involve?
Why. The fast path is a CAS from free to locked and an atomic release; the kernel is called only when a thread has to sleep (futex wait) or when the unlocker knows sleepers exist (futex wake). That is the whole point of the design.
Why does a test-and-test-and-set spinlock perform better under contention than one that loops on test-and-set?
Why. Each test-and-set is a write that needs the line exclusively, so a crowd of spinners steals the line from each other and from the holder. Reads can be served from each core's shared copy. Ordering, fairness and sleeping are separate concerns.
A futex wait takes the address and the value the caller expects to find there. Why the expected value?
Why. Without it, a thread could see "locked", get preempted, the holder unlocks and wakes nobody (nobody is asleep yet), and the thread then sleeps forever: a lost wakeup. Checking the value and sleeping as one kernel step closes that window. C++20 atomic::wait(old) has the same contract.
As an order of magnitude, what does it cost to hand a contended lock to a thread that is parked?
Why. Waking a parked thread involves the kernel and a context switch, typically a few microseconds, which is why fair locks that always hand off to a sleeping thread are so much slower. An uncontended lock is tens of nanoseconds or less. Milliseconds is the spinning worst case when a holder is descheduled.
Java onlyCountDownLatch is built on AbstractQueuedSynchronizer. What does its state hold, and when does await() succeed?
Why. countDown() CASes the state down by one, and the release that brings it to 0 unparks the queued waiters, all of them, because the acquire is in shared mode. Permits describe Semaphore; the hold count describes ReentrantLock.
C++ onlyIn C++20, what does state.wait(2) do on a std::atomic<int> state?
Why. wait(old) compares the current value with old and blocks only if they are equal, and it returns only once the value has changed, even across spurious wake-ups. Paired with notify_one or notify_all, it is the portable form of futex wait and wake.

Checkpoint

Write your own answer first. Then compare with the model answer, or ask Bar Raiser to mark it like an interviewer would.

Explain
"How would you implement a mutex if the language gave you only atomics and a way to sleep and wake a thread?" Answer as you would in the interview.
Model answer

I would use one atomic integer with three states: 0 free, 1 locked, 2 locked with possible waiters. Lock tries a CAS from 0 to 1; that succeeds without any system call when the lock is free. If it fails, the thread swaps in 2 to announce that a waiter exists; if the old value was 0 it now owns the lock, otherwise it sleeps with a futex-style wait that only sleeps if the word is still 2, and after waking it swaps in 2 again and checks the old value. Unlock does an atomic decrement: if the old value was 1 there were no waiters and it is done; if it was 2, it stores 0 and wakes one sleeper.

The acquire operations use acquire ordering and the release uses release ordering, so the next owner sees everything the previous one wrote. In production I would add a short bounded spin before sleeping, since many critical sections are shorter than a sleep and wake. In Java I would not hand-roll this: I would subclass AbstractQueuedSynchronizer, implement tryAcquire as a CAS on the state and tryRelease as resetting it, and let AQS manage the queue and the parking.

Write code
Write a spinlock with test-and-test-and-set and a spin-wait hint in your language. Then say in two sentences when you would use it instead of the standard mutex.
Model answer

An atomic exchange acquires with acquire semantics, the inner loop only reads while the lock is held, and a release store unlocks.

When to use it: only when critical sections are a few dozen instructions, the number of threads does not exceed the number of cores, and the holder never blocks, for example inside a lock or allocator implementation, or a kernel or real-time system that pins threads to cores. Otherwise the platform mutex is the better choice: it parks waiters instead of burning cores, and many implementations (HotSpot's synchronized, glibc's adaptive type) already spin briefly before parking.

Numbers
A hot lock protects a 100 ns critical section. Handing it to a parked thread costs about 5 µs of wake-up latency. Estimate the maximum throughput if every hand off goes to a sleeping waiter (a fair lock under contention), and compare with a barging lock where a running thread usually takes it.
Model answer

Fair hand off: each operation costs the wake-up plus the critical section, about 5.1 µs, during which the lock is owned but mostly idle. That caps throughput near 1 / 5.1 µs, roughly 200,000 operations per second, no matter how many cores you add.

Barging: a thread that is already running grabs the lock as soon as it is free, so most hand offs cost about the critical section plus a cache line transfer, a few hundred nanoseconds at most, giving several million operations per second, perhaps ten times more or better. The price is fairness: the parked thread may lose the race repeatedly. This is why locks are unfair by default, and why under heavy contention the right fix is to shorten or split the critical section rather than to make the lock fair.

How a senior would say it
A mutex is one integer in ordinary memory plus a way to sleep. Locking starts with a compare and swap from 0, free, to 1, locked. If it succeeds, I own the lock and no system call happened. That CAS is also an acquire, so I see everything the previous owner wrote. If the CAS fails, some implementations spin for a moment, because the holder may be about to leave. Then the waiter swaps in 2, which means locked and someone may be asleep, and calls futex wait on the word, passing 2 as the value it expects. The kernel checks that the word still holds 2 and puts the thread to sleep in one step. That is the key detail: if the holder released the lock a nanosecond earlier, the word is no longer 2 and the wait returns at once, so the wakeup cannot be lost. Unlocking is an atomic decrement with release ordering. If the old value was 1, nobody is waiting and we are done, still with no system call. If it was 2, the unlocker stores 0 and calls futex wake for one thread, which wakes up, swaps in 2 again and retries. So the uncontended path is a couple of atomic instructions, tens of nanoseconds, and the kernel only sees contention, where each hand off to a sleeping thread costs microseconds. That is why I worry about contended locks, not about locks.

Out loudabout 90 s
"Walk me through the lost wakeup a naive sleeping lock would have, and how a futex, or Java's park and unpark, closes the gap."
What a strong answer covers
  • The interleaving: the waiter sees "held" and decides to sleep, but is preempted before it sleeps
  • The holder unlocks, finds nobody asleep and wakes nobody; the waiter then sleeps on a free lock and may never wake
  • The waiter announces itself first (writes 2), so the unlocker knows a wake is needed
  • Futex wait checks the expected value and sleeps as one step, so a changed word makes it return at once
  • Java: the LockSupport permit makes an unpark that arrives before park count; AQS marks the node as waiting before parking
  • C++20 atomic::wait(old) has the same contract as the futex
How a senior would say it
The danger is the gap between deciding to sleep and actually sleeping. Say thread B tries the lock and sees it held. B decides to sleep, but before it gets into the kernel it is preempted. Now A unlocks, looks for sleepers, finds none because B is not asleep yet, and wakes nobody. Then B finally goes to sleep, on a lock that is free, and it may never be woken. That is a lost wakeup. The futex closes the gap by making the check part of the sleep. B first writes 2 into the word to announce that a waiter exists, then calls futex wait saying "sleep only if the word is still 2". The kernel does that comparison and the sleep as one step with respect to wakes, so if A already released, the word is 0 and the call returns at once. Java gets the same guarantee differently. LockSupport gives each thread a permit, so an unpark that arrives before the park makes the park return immediately, and AQS marks the node as waiting before it parks, so the releaser knows whom to unpark. C++20's atomic::wait with an old value has exactly the futex contract.

Out loudabout 90 s
"A teammate replaced the mutex on a hot path with a hand-written spinlock to save system calls. CPU went up and p99 latency got worse. What happened, and what would you do?"
What a strong answer covers
  • An uncontended mutex makes no system call anyway, so there was nothing to save on the fast path
  • Under contention every spinner burns a full core, and with more threads than cores spinners steal CPU from the holder
  • A descheduled holder makes each spinner waste its whole time slice: the millisecond tail in p99
  • Spinning on the exchange itself bounces the cache line and slows even the release
  • Fix: revert to the mutex and cut contention (shorter or split critical section); keep a spinlock only for tiny sections, pinned threads and holders that never block
How a senior would say it
The spinlock solved a problem that did not exist and created new ones. An uncontended mutex never enters the kernel anyway: it is one CAS to lock and one atomic to unlock, about what the spinlock costs. The only time the mutex makes system calls is under contention, and that is exactly when spinning hurts. Every waiter now burns a full core, so CPU goes up. If there are more threads than cores, the spinners steal CPU from the thread holding the lock, and when the holder's time slice ends, every spinner wastes its own slice waiting for a thread that is not running. That is the millisecond tail in p99. And if the loop spins on the exchange itself rather than on a read, the cache line bounces between cores and even the release gets slower. I would revert to the mutex, and treat the system calls as a symptom: the lock is contended, so I would shrink the critical section, move work out of it, or split the lock. I would keep a spinlock only for a few dozen instructions, with threads pinned to cores and a holder that never blocks.

The toolkit: executors, Future and CompletableFuture

Choose execution policies, bound queued work, retrieve and cancel results, schedule repeated tasks, and compose asynchronous Java computations without blocking workers.

Asked atRubrik
Before this:The toolkit: Thread, Runnable, Callable and virtual threadsLambdas, functional interfaces and method referencesExceptions, InterruptedException and try-with-resourcesTime: nanoTime, Duration and TimeUnit

Starting work is easy. The harder questions are where it waits, who sees its failure, how long the caller waits, and who stops the workers. Executors and futures make those decisions explicit.

In plain words

A repair shop takes jobs at a counter, puts waiting jobs on a shelf, and gives each customer a receipt. The workers decide when to pick up each job. A receipt lets you ask for the result later; it does not guarantee that the shelf has room or that cancelling the receipt can undo a repair already made.

Some jobs have instructions attached: after repairing the phone, test it; when both the phone and charger are ready, pack them together. Those instructions describe dependencies without making a worker stand idle beside another worker.

Why this matters for concurrency

An executor separates a task from its execution policy. A future represents its eventual outcome. You need both when designing a crawler, request fan-out, background job runner or an LLD service that owns workers.

The thread toolkit explains Runnable, Callable and virtual threads; the exceptions lesson explains interruption and resource syntax. Here you choose and connect their library APIs. Synchronizers used to control examples are covered in concurrent collections and synchronizers.

API card: execution and results

Executor is the small interface; ExecutorService extends it with results and lifecycle. ThreadPoolExecutor is a configurable implementation, while Executors is a factory utility class. Program against the interface that your component needs.

Execute versus submit: where does failure go?

execute does not mean "new thread": an executor can run inline, queue work or hand it to another thread. In a normal ThreadPoolExecutor, an uncaught exception from a plain Runnable passed to execute reaches the worker's uncaught exception handler and ends that worker; workers are replaced as needed.

submit normally wraps work in a FutureTask, capturing its exception for get(). It does not escape to the worker's uncaught exception handler. Ignoring the future can hide failure. Submission itself can still fail synchronously with RejectedExecutionException under a throwing rejection policy.

FutureTask<T> implements RunnableFuture<T>, combining Runnable and Future<T>. Construct it with a Callable<T> or a Runnable plus a result, then pass it to execute and retain that same object for get or cancellation. Its ordinary run() executes the computation at most once; calling run() yourself runs it on your thread. Calling get() does not start it; see the FutureTask contract.

Submission safely publishes prior caller actions to the task; retrieving its result with get() establishes the return visibility edge. This does not protect concurrent mutations after submission or sibling tasks' concurrent writes. A timeout or cancellation is not a task-exit barrier: the task may still be writing. See happens-before and worker failures.

ThreadPoolExecutor: read the whole constructor

The full constructor takes corePoolSize, maximumPoolSize, keepAliveTime, unit, workQueue, threadFactory, handler. Shorter overloads supply the default thread factory or rejection handler.

Parameter or methodOne-line semantics
corePoolSizeBelow this count, submissions create workers; core workers normally remain alive while idle.
maximumPoolSizeWorker ceiling; expansion beyond core happens only when queueing fails.
keepAliveTime, unitIdle timeout above core, not a task execution deadline.
BlockingQueue<Runnable> workQueueStores waiting tasks; bounded queues expose overload, while SynchronousQueue stores none.
ThreadFactory threadFactoryCreates workers, allowing names and an uncaught exception handler.
RejectedExecutionHandler handlerDecides what happens on saturation or shutdown.

The normal admission sequence is create below core, otherwise queue, otherwise grow toward maximum, otherwise reject. With an unbounded queue, increasing maximum usually changes nothing. Core and keep-alive values must be nonnegative, maximum positive, and corePoolSize <= maximumPoolSize.

AbortPolicy, the default, throws. CallerRunsPolicy executes on the submitting thread while the executor is live, slowing that producer; after shutdown it discards. DiscardPolicy silently drops the new task; DiscardOldestPolicy drops the queue head and retries while live. Dropping submitted tasks without cancelling their futures can strand callers.

Worked example: one core, two maximum, one queue slot

Hold running tasks at a gate. A creates the core worker, B fills the queue, C creates worker two, and D is rejected. B can start after C despite being submitted first.

A bounded queue does not make execute wait for space: it tries offer, not put, then grows or rejects as needed. The call can still take time in worker creation, queue locking or a rejection handler; CallerRunsPolicy runs the whole task before returning. The gate makes the diagnostic snapshot stable in this example only. The ThreadPoolExecutor reference specifies this policy; continue with executor anatomy and rejection and pool failures.

Future: timeout, failure and cancellation differ

get() can return a value, throw ExecutionException wrapping task failure, throw CancellationException, or throw InterruptedException because the waiting caller was interrupted. Timed get adds TimeoutException. Timeout ends this caller's wait; the task continues unless separately cancelled.

For pool task futures, successful cancel(false) prevents a not-yet-started task from running, but does not interrupt one already executing. cancel(true) additionally requests interruption when running. Either may fail if the future already completed. Even successful cancellation does not prove the task stopped or rolled back side effects.

A running FutureTask can accept cancel(false): its future becomes cancelled while its computation continues, and its eventual value is discarded. Both isDone() and isCancelled() can therefore be true before the task exits. Cancellation also need not remove a queued wrapper immediately; ThreadPoolExecutor.purge() removes cancelled task futures from its queue when retention matters.

The zero timeout is intentional: the closed gate proves the task is unfinished, regardless of scheduling speed. The task propagates interruption through Callable; main propagates it too. The Future contract defines these outcomes; cooperative cancellation explains task cooperation.

Invoke all or invoke any?

invokeAll waits for all tasks and returns futures in input order, not completion order. One task failing does not throw that task exception from the batch call: inspect each future. Its timed version cancels unfinished tasks after the batch budget expires and returns; it does not throw TimeoutException.

invokeAny returns one successful result. An early failure need not win; if every task fails, it throws ExecutionException. Its timed overload can throw TimeoutException. On normal or exceptional return it cancels unfinished competitors. Both methods propagate caller interruption and cancel unfinished tasks.

The output reports 7, failure, 9, then a successful 9. To consume completion order, ExecutorCompletionService offers submit, take and timed poll: take() returns a completed future, whose get() reveals success or failure; timed poll returns null on timeout. Do not submit and immediately get inside every loop iteration if you intended parallel work; submit the batch first. See futures and promises.

Lifecycle: ownership includes waiting for shutdown

Use shutdown(), wait with awaitTermination, then escalate to shutdownNow() if the budget expires and wait again. A false result means workers may still be alive. If the owner is interrupted, request immediate shutdown and propagate interruption, or restore interrupt status at a boundary that cannot throw it.

In ThreadPoolExecutor, shutdownNow() does not automatically complete the futures of removed queued tasks. Keep and cancel their handles; returned queued wrappers are also normally Future instances you can cancel. An interrupted running task may fail with InterruptedException inside ExecutionException without its future being marked cancelled.

close() suits locally owned finite work, but has no timeout. If interrupted while waiting, it requests immediate shutdown, continues waiting, and restores interrupt status before returning. Uncooperative tasks can keep it blocked. Never close an executor from one of its own tasks: that task cannot finish while awaiting its executor's termination.

Only the owner shuts down a shared executor. Also await the terminal stage of an asynchronous graph before leaving its executor's resource scope: a task may later try to submit a continuation after close() stops admission. These lifecycle rules follow the Java 21 ExecutorService contract.

ScheduledExecutorService: clock target or rest period

MethodOne-line semantics
schedule(Runnable, delay, unit)Run once after a relative delay; its ScheduledFuture<?> yields null on success.
schedule(Callable<T>, delay, unit)Run once after a delay and return a ScheduledFuture<T> for the value.
scheduleAtFixedRate(task, initial, period, unit)Target starts at initial delay plus successive multiples of a positive period.
scheduleWithFixedDelay(task, initial, delay, unit)Wait a positive delay after execution ends before enabling the next.

Suppose initial delay is zero, the interval 100 ms, and every run takes 150 ms. On an otherwise idle scheduler, idealized fixed-rate starts are 0, 150, 300 ms: it runs late without overlapping executions of that registration. Fixed-delay starts are 0, 250, 500 ms: each completion gets a 100 ms rest. Real scheduling adds delays; neither is a real-time guarantee.

An uncaught exception ends future repetitions of that registration. Its future fails; a healthy periodic task's get() never returns normally. Catch and report recoverable failures inside the task when repetitions should continue. Separate registrations of the same Runnable can still overlap on different workers.

The array belongs to successive, non-overlapping runs of one registration; the caller never reads it. ScheduledThreadPoolExecutor also guarantees that each run happens-before the next, even if different workers execute them. This demo checks repetition and failure, not exact timestamps.

By default ScheduledThreadPoolExecutor.shutdown() stops periodic work but still executes existing delayed one-shot tasks, so a far-future job prolongs termination. Policies are configurable. See the scheduler reference and scheduled executors and timers.

API card: CompletableFuture describes dependencies

CompletableFuture implements Future<T> and CompletionStage<T>. It is an outcome holder you can complete and a place to register a continuation: work triggered when a preceding result is available. It does not itself own a worker thread.

MethodOne-line semantics
supplyAsync(supplier, executor) / runAsync(task, executor)Schedule initial work producing a value or Void; overloads omit the executor.
completedFuture(value) / failedFuture(error)Create an already successful or failed future.
complete(value) / completeExceptionally(error)Attempt to settle an incomplete future; the first successful completion wins.
thenApply(f)On success, transform T into U, returning a new future for U.
thenCompose(f)Invoke T -> CompletionStage<U> on success and flatten the dependent result.
thenCombine(other, f)After both inputs succeed, combine their values into a new result.
thenAccept(action) / thenRun(action)Run a success-only action with the value or without it, producing Void.
exceptionally(recovery)On failure, compute a replacement value; success passes through.
handle((value, error) -> result)Transform success or failure; the error is null on success.
whenComplete(observer)Observe either outcome without replacing it; a throwing observer can fail a successful stage.
allOf(futures...)Return CompletableFuture<Void> that settles after all inputs settle, failing if any failed.
anyOf(futures...)Return CompletableFuture<Object> with an input's first completion, success or failure.
get() / join()Retrieve the outcome; get is interruptible with checked failures, while join uses unchecked failure reporting.
orTimeout(time, unit) / completeOnTimeout(value, time, unit)Complete this future exceptionally or with a fallback if still incomplete at timeout.
minimalCompletionStage()Expose a restricted stage view when callers should compose results without completing your future.

For task failure, get wraps the cause in ExecutionException; join throws unchecked CompletionException. A directly cancelled future throws CancellationException. join has no interruptible-wait contract like get, and no timed overload. Timeout completion and CompletableFuture.cancel(true) do not interrupt the supplier; its cancellation flag argument has no effect on interruption.

Apply, compose and combine: follow the types

Choose thenApply for an ordinary transformation, thenCompose when it returns another asynchronous operation, and thenCombine for independent results. Returning a future from thenApply creates a nested future. Calling get or join inside that callback to flatten it can occupy the worker needed by the child.

The lookups are stand-ins for I/O operations. Only the outer caller waits. lookupPrice borrows an Executor, so cannot manage the owner's lifecycle through that interface. The graph finishes before resource closure. See LLD design for ownership and async composition for deeper graphs.

Which thread runs each stage?

CallExecution context
thenApply, thenCompose, handle and other non-async callbacksMay run on a completing thread; registering against an already completed stage can run the callback immediately on the registering thread.
supplyAsync(supplier) or thenApplyAsync(f) on ordinary CompletableFutureUse ForkJoinPool.commonPool(); if parallelism is below two, the default falls back to a new thread per task.
thenApplyAsync(f, chosenExecutor)Delegate to that executor; an executor can still have an inline policy.

Executor choice is not inherited down the chain. supplyAsync(f, io).thenApplyAsync(g) normally sends g to the common pool, not io. Pass an executor at each async boundary needing isolation. Keep non-async callbacks short and put blocking I/O on a suitable executor with downstream limits, rather than occupying shared common-pool workers. Subclasses can override the default executor; these rules describe ordinary CompletableFuture instances.

This Java 21 example fixes completion and registration order, giving stable observed names. In a racing program, do not depend on one particular thread for a non-async callback. The CompletableFuture execution policies permit completion callers to perform dependent actions.

Recovery and aggregation: failure is also completion

A failed source skips success transformations; their dependent stages fail too. exceptionally supplies a fallback on failure, while handle maps either outcome. Check the error argument, not whether the value is null: success can legitimately return null. If recovery throws, its new stage fails.

allOf waits for every input, does not collect values, and does not fail immediately on the first failure. anyOf can finish exceptionally before another input succeeds, unlike invokeAny. Neither cancels unfinished inputs automatically. Cancelling a dependent stage does not automatically cancel its upstream work.

A success-only dependent that propagates source cancellation fails with CompletionException wrapping CancellationException, rather than necessarily reporting isCancelled(). If a whenComplete observer throws after an upstream failure, that upstream failure takes precedence. Use handle or exceptionally when you intend recovery.

The joins in the final transformation cannot wait: its success callback runs only after both recovered inputs succeed. With no inputs, allOf() is already successful with null; anyOf() stays incomplete. If several inputs fail, do not rely on a particular failure being selected. For a complete report, convert each input into an outcome using handle, then aggregate.

orTimeout and completeOnTimeout mutate and return the same future, affecting other callers sharing it. Apply the timeout to shared.copy() when only one caller's view should expire. Timeout is not underlying I/O cancellation. Choose a cancellation owner and retain necessary task or I/O handles.

The zero timeout needs no sleep: the original future stays incomplete until the caller explicitly completes it. copy() is an independent outcome holder, not a deep copy of the eventual value. For APIs that expose only composition, return minimalCompletionStage(); declaring a mutable future as CompletionStage alone still lets callers cast it back. See the copy and restricted-view APIs and the dependency and failure rules.

Common mistakes

  • Bounded workers, unbounded waiting. A fixed pool can still exhaust memory. Bound admission and define rejection.
  • Get inside every submission iteration. Submit independent work first; otherwise the loop serializes it.
  • Discarding a submitted future. Arrange failure observation instead of assuming the uncaught exception handler sees it.
  • Waiting for a child on the same saturated pool. Every worker can wait while every child is queued. Compose dependencies or separate execution resources.
  • Equating timeout, cancellation and termination. A timed-out caller or cancelled outcome does not prove the task stopped.
  • Assuming Async inherits the previous executor. Supply the executor where execution policy matters.
  • Ignoring periodic failures or lifecycle ownership. Observe scheduled handles, stop owned executors, and never close borrowed ones.

In the interview

Expect "Why is my pool stuck at core size?", "Where did this exception go?" and "Which thread runs this callback?" Start with admission, results and lifecycle. Identify which thread blocks and which thread must make progress.

I separate work from policy. I choose a bounded queue and explicit rejection, observe every outcome, and give shutdown one owner. For dependent asynchronous steps I compose stages so a worker does not block waiting for a queued child.

A 2022 Rubrik report describes improving a crawler with CompletableFuture; a 2024 report bans ExecutorService for dependency scheduling. Establish the allowed toolbox before coding. If libraries are forbidden, rebuild these contracts in a thread pool, an executor without libraries and a completable future.

Key takeaways

  • An executor chooses where work runs; a future carries its outcome. Neither alone bounds admission.
  • ThreadPoolExecutor queues before growing beyond core. Queue capacity and rejection determine overload behavior.
  • Timed get limits waiting, cancel requests cancellation, and termination needs task cooperation.
  • Own shutdown; close waits without a deadline.
  • Fixed rate targets a schedule; fixed delay waits after completion. Uncaught failure ends repetition.
  • Apply transforms values, compose chains dependent stages, and combine merges independent results. Specify execution context and failure handling.

Why. Admission queues after reaching core, so it does not first add six workers. Only failed queue admission triggers growth toward maximum. An unbounded queue accepts the waiting tasks instead of rejecting at eight; submission does not wait for queue space.
A task submitted with submit throws IllegalStateException. No one examines its future. Which statement is correct for a normal ThreadPoolExecutor?
Why. The task wrapper captures failure, so neither the submitter nor the worker's uncaught exception handler receives this task exception directly. Why. Timeout limits one wait and does not settle the future, so a later get() may still succeed. Cancellation is separate: even when it succeeds, execution can continue. Neither timeout nor cancellation rolls back side effects.

Checkpoint

Write your own answer first. Then compare with the model answer, or ask Bar Raiser to mark it like an interviewer would.

Debug
One worker runs a parent task that submits a child to the same single-thread executor and calls child.get(). The queue has room. Explain the hang and repair it without sleeps.
Model answer

The parent occupies the sole worker and waits for the child. The child is queued but needs that same worker to start. No lock cycle is necessary: the dependency and limited execution capacity cause starvation. Express the parent as an asynchronous stage, use thenCompose to return the child stage, and let only an external caller wait. Separate executors can remove this dependency cycle, but increasing one pool's size only postpones it when enough parents occupy all workers.

Design
A component receives an ExecutorService from its caller. A request must stop waiting after 200 ms. Describe results, cancellation and ownership, including what changes with CompletableFuture.
Model answer

The component borrows the executor and does not close it. Use timed get, distinguish timeout from task failure and caller interruption, and retain the task future if policy calls for cancel(true). Tasks must cooperate with interruption; remote side effects need their own semantics. Carry the remaining request budget across multiple waits instead of restarting 200 ms at every step.

CompletableFuture.orTimeout completes that future exceptionally but does not interrupt its supplier. It also changes the shared future itself, unlike a timed get's local wait; use copy().orTimeout(...) for a separate caller's view. Define who owns cancellation and retain any task or I/O handle needed to stop the operation. Observe terminal failure and leave shutdown to the executor's actual owner.

Write code
Given independent CompletableFuture<Integer> values price and shipping, produce a future of their sum, recover failure to -1, and report the final outcome. Where could callbacks run?
Model answer

All callbacks are non-async and may run during completion or registration. Recovery converts upstream failure into normal completion with -1, so status normally reports a value. If a sentinel hides a useful distinction, use an explicit outcome type. Use async overloads with an explicit executor when callback execution needs isolation.

Say it out loud

Interviews are spoken. Answer each question out loud as if the interviewer just asked it. Tandem transcribes you on this Mac, word for word, measures your pace, fillers and pauses, and Bar Raiser scores what you said and how you said it.

Out loudabout 75 s
"Our pool has a maximum of 100 threads, but it stays at 10 while latency and memory grow. What would you inspect and change?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Inspect core size and queue type; an unbounded queue explains lack of growth beyond core
  • Explain admission: create below core, queue, grow only after queue failure, then reject
  • Relate backlog to retained memory and queueing latency
  • Choose queue capacity and rejection based on workload and downstream limits
  • Explain CallerRunsPolicy's caller-thread cost and silent-discard future risk
  • Measure queue age, rejection and dependency latency before tuning workers
How a senior would say it
I would inspect the queue first. If core size is ten and the queue is unbounded, this behavior is expected. ThreadPoolExecutor creates workers below core, then tries to queue. Only when queueing fails does it grow toward maximum. Changing maximum to a hundred does not drain that backlog. Each queued task retains memory and waits longer before starting, explaining both symptoms. I would choose queue capacity and an overload response based on the latency budget and downstream capacity. AbortPolicy makes rejection visible so the caller can shed load or retry under a controlled policy. CallerRunsPolicy slows the producer by executing work on its thread, which can be unacceptable on an event loop. Silent discard is especially dangerous when callers hold futures that never complete. I would measure queue age, rejection counts, worker activity and dependency latency before changing concurrency. More workers can help some blocking workloads, but they can also overwhelm the database. The goal is bounded admitted work with an observable failure policy, not just a larger thread count.

Likely follow up: What happens if every worker submits a child to this pool and waits for it?

Out loudabout 75 s
"Compare thenApply, thenCompose and thenCombine for me. Then tell me which thread runs the callbacks."
What a strong answer covers
  • Apply transforms a value, compose flattens a dependent stage, combine merges independent successes
  • Give dependent and independent lookup examples
  • Explain why joining a child inside apply can starve a bounded pool
  • Non-async callbacks can run during completion or registration
  • Async uses the default executor unless one is supplied; the preceding executor is not inherited
  • Observe the terminal result before closing executors needed by the graph
How a senior would say it
Apply transforms a value, compose chains an asynchronous dependency, and combine joins independent successful results. If I have a user, apply can extract an identifier. If that identifier starts an asynchronous order lookup, compose gives me a future of orders instead of a future containing another future. If an independent discount lookup is running, combine can merge its result with the order total. I would not flatten the nested result by joining inside apply. That occupies a worker, and the child may need the same saturated pool. Non-async callbacks have no dedicated worker guarantee. They can run during completion, or immediately on the registering thread when the source is already complete. Async methods on ordinary CompletableFuture use the common pool by default, subject to its low-parallelism fallback. An explicitly supplied executor controls that boundary only; it is not inherited by later async calls. I would keep inline callbacks short, specify executors where isolation matters, and observe the terminal stage before closing any executor the graph still needs.

Likely follow up: How does anyOf differ from invokeAny when the fastest operation fails?

Out loudabout 60 s
"A request timed out, so I cancelled its future and shut down the pool. Can I tell the caller that the work has stopped?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Timeout ends a wait, not the task
  • Pool Future cancellation can request interruption but cannot stop uncooperative work or roll back effects
  • CompletableFuture cancellation does not interrupt its supplier
  • Shutdown rejects new work; shutdownNow attempts interruption and returns pending tasks
  • Await termination and settle abandoned queued futures; close has no deadline
  • Only the owner shuts down the pool; remote effects need separate confirmation
How a senior would say it
No. Those calls do not prove the work has stopped. Timed get limits my wait. Cancelling a pool task future can request interruption, but the task must cooperate, and cancellation does not roll back effects. CompletableFuture cancellation completes the outcome exceptionally without interrupting its supplier. Shutdown rejects new work while accepted tasks finish. ShutdownNow attempts interruption and returns queued tasks that never started; I must settle their futures so callers are not stranded. I would await termination and check its result before reporting that the executor's tasks finished. Close waits too, but has no deadline and can hang on uncooperative work. Finally, only the owner should shut down the executor. Stopping a borrowed pool affects unrelated requests. Even local termination does not confirm that a remote payment or write was undone, so that requires a separate application-level contract and confirmation.

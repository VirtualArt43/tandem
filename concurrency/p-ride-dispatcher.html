Ride dispatcher

Thread-safe designshard45 minUber cab hailing LLD, Salesforce ride assignment, Flipkart delivery assignment machine codingUberSalesforceFlipkart

Uber's cab hailing LLD, Salesforce's ride assignment round and Flipkart's delivery assignment machine coding all come down to this class: every passenger gets exactly one driver, no driver gets two passengers, the longest waiting passenger goes first, and a passenger who gave up never gets a driver later.

The task

Drivers come online and go offline. Passengers ask for a ride and wait until a driver is assigned. All of it happens on many threads at once.

  • driverOnline(driver): the driver is free to take rides. If someone is waiting, the driver goes to them. Calling it during a ride cancels a pending offline.
  • driverOffline(driver): the driver takes no new rides. An idle driver leaves at once; a driver on a ride finishes it and then leaves. Returns true if this call took the driver offline, false if the driver is unknown, already offline or already leaving.
  • requestRide(passenger, timeoutMs): blocks until a driver is assigned to this passenger and returns the driver, or returns nothing after timeoutMs. With timeoutMs of 0 it only takes a driver who is free right now. A passenger who already waits or rides gets nothing at once (a double tap must not book two cars).
  • completeRide(driver): the ride is over and the driver is free again. Returns false if the driver is not on a ride.
  • waitingPassengers(): how many passengers are waiting right now.

Rules.

  • First in, first out. When a driver frees up (online, or a ride completes) and passengers are waiting, the driver goes to the passenger who has waited longest. A passenger who arrives a moment later must not take that driver, even if their thread happens to run first.
  • A timed out passenger is gone. Once requestRide has returned nothing, no driver is ever assigned to that request. If a driver was handed over at the very moment the timeout fired, return the driver instead: either outcome is fine, but never both.
  • An offline driver is never assigned. Once driverOffline returns, the driver gets no new passenger, at most finishing the ride already assigned.
  • In Java, a thread interrupted while waiting throws InterruptedException and leaves the queue.

Example. Driver d1 is on a ride. Ann calls requestRide("ann", 5000) and waits; then Bob calls requestRide("bob", 5000). d1 completes the ride: Ann gets d1 at once, Bob keeps waiting. Carol's requestRide("carol", 100) times out. Driver d2 comes online: Bob gets d2. Carol does not, and d3 coming online later stays idle.

What the tests check

  • One thread: assign, time out on time, complete, and complete twice (the second returns false).
  • A request really blocks while every driver is busy, and wakes when a ride completes.
  • Five waiting passengers get drivers in arrival order.
  • A passenger who arrives right after a driver comes online never takes it from the passenger who was waiting, 15 rounds.
  • A timed out request is never served later; 25 rounds where a driver comes online right at the deadline never lose the driver.
  • Offline drivers are never assigned, also when they go offline during a ride, and 40 rounds where going offline races a request.
  • A double tap: one passenger requesting from 4 threads at once gets exactly one driver.
  • Java: an interrupted passenger leaves the queue.
  • Stress with 16 passengers taking 25 rides each, 4 drivers and a fifth driver going on and off, started in a seeded random order: no driver on two rides at once, every request served, and afterwards no driver lost or leaked.
  • In C++, ThreadSanitizer must stay quiet.

Write down the states first. A driver is offline, idle or on a ride; a passenger is waiting or riding. Every method reads that state, decides and changes it, so every method is a check then act over shared state: one lock around all of it is the right first version. The real question is who gets a driver when one frees up.
If a freed driver goes into an idle pool and you wake the waiters, the woken passenger still has to win the lock again, and a passenger who just walked in can win it first. That is barging. Instead, hand the driver to the oldest waiting passenger right there, while you hold the lock. Give each waiting passenger a small request object with a driver field and its own condition, kept in a FIFO queue.
Keep one invariant: drivers wait in the idle line only when no passenger is waiting. Then a newcomer may take an idle driver without jumping anyone. On timeout, re-check under the lock: if a driver was handed to you, take it; otherwise remove your request from the queue before returning, so no driver is ever handed to a passenger who left.

What the interviewer asks after your first version works. Try to answer each one out loud before you open it.

A Salesforce candidate used an ExecutorService, blocking queues and concurrent collections, and the code ran out of threads. Your version parks one thread per waiting passenger. What changes at 100,000 waiting passengers?
Make the wait asynchronous. requestRide creates a request, puts it in the FIFO queue and returns a CompletableFuture (a std::future from a std::promise in C++); the hand-off completes it instead of signalling a condition. A timeout becomes a scheduled task or a delay queue entry that, under the same lock, removes the request and completes it with nothing only if no driver was assigned yet. Waiting then costs a small object, not a thread with a stack.
A driver must accept within W ms, otherwise the ride goes to the next driver. How do you add the accept window?
Add an offered state with an offer id and a deadline: the hand-off makes the driver OFFERED instead of ON_RIDE. accept(driver, offerId) is a conditional transition under the lock: it succeeds only if the driver is still OFFERED with that same id. When the deadline passes, the offer expires, the driver goes back (or offline, if they ignore offers), and the request is offered to the next idle driver with a new id, so a late accept sees a different id and fails. The offer is a lease on the driver.
Real dispatch picks the nearest driver, not the longest idle one. How does the locking change?
Keep idle drivers in a geo index (grid cells or geohashes) with a lock per cell, so matches in different areas run in parallel. A match may look at the passenger's cell and its neighbours: lock those cells in a fixed order (by cell id) to avoid deadlock, claim the chosen driver, unlock. Uber style systems go further and give each city or region a single owner thread or service, so matching inside a region needs no locks at all.
The dispatcher now runs on many servers. How do you stop two servers from assigning the same driver?
Make the database or Redis the arbiter with a conditional write: UPDATE drivers SET state = 'ON_RIDE', ride_id = ? WHERE id = ? AND state = 'IDLE', then check that one row changed; the loser picks another driver. Give each ride request an idempotency key so a retried request (the double tap again) finds the existing ride. Or partition by city so one server owns each driver.
Why not a ConcurrentHashMap of drivers with a compare-and-set on each driver's state?
A CAS from IDLE to ON_RIDE does stop double booking of one driver, and it is a fine building block. But the rules here span several objects at once: the waiting queue, the request that may be timing out, and the driver. "Oldest passenger first" and "never to a passenger who left" need those to change together, which one lock (or one dispatcher thread) gives you and a CAS on a single field does not.
Is the solution fair to drivers too?
Yes: idle drivers wait in a FIFO line, so the driver idle longest gets the next passenger who finds the line non-empty. Fairness to passengers comes from the direct hand-off; with a non-fair lock and signalAll neither side is fair.

The idea

This is a monitor with a direct hand-off. One lock guards all dispatcher state: drivers and their states, the line of idle drivers, the FIFO queue of waiting requests, and the set of passengers who are waiting or riding. When a driver frees up, the code that freed it, still holding the lock, gives the driver to the oldest waiting request and signals that request's own condition. The woken passenger finds r.driver already set; nobody can race them for it, because the driver was never free.

The invariants

  • A driver is in exactly one state: offline, idle (and then in the idle line), or on a ride with exactly one passenger.
  • The idle line is empty or the waiting queue is empty. Every place that frees a driver calls the same handOff, so a free driver never sits idle while someone waits. This is what makes the fast path fair: a newcomer who finds an idle driver is not jumping anyone.
  • A passenger is in the busy set while waiting or riding, which is what turns a double tap into a no-op.
  • A request leaves the waiting queue exactly once: by hand-off, by timeout or by interruption, always under the lock.

Why each piece is there

  • One lock. The rules connect the queue, the request, the driver and the passenger set; one lock makes every rule a plain sequential check. Every operation is O(1) apart from removing a timed out request or an offline driver from a deque, which is O(n) and rare.
  • A condition per request (lock.newCondition() in Java, a std::condition_variable in each request in C++): the hand-off wakes exactly the passenger who got the driver, with signal or notify_one, instead of a herd.
  • Waiting in a loop on r.driver handles spurious wakeups. awaitNanos returns the time left; wait_until with a predicate checks once more at the deadline, which is exactly the "handed over at the last moment" case.
  • The request lives on the waiter's stack in C++. That is safe because only the waiter removes it, and only while holding the mutex, before returning.
  • Interruption in Java: on InterruptedException, leave the queue, or, if a driver was assigned in the same instant, complete that ride so the driver goes to the next passenger; then rethrow.

Pitfalls

  • Signalling outside the lock after setting r.driver outside the lock: the field must be written under the same lock the waiter reads it with.
  • driverOffline that only sets a flag while the driver stays in the idle line: the next request takes an offline driver.
  • Forgetting the busy set: a passenger who taps twice gets two cars, and completeRide cannot tell which ride ended.
  • Using notify with one shared condition for different kinds of waiters: the signal can wake the wrong thread and be lost. Per request conditions avoid the question.

How to say it in the interview

All dispatcher state sits behind one lock, because every rule spans the queue, the request and the driver. Waiting passengers are a FIFO queue of request objects, each with its own condition. When a driver frees up, whoever freed it hands the driver straight to the oldest request while still holding the lock, so a newcomer cannot barge in. I keep the invariant that idle drivers exist only when nobody waits, which makes the fast path fair. On timeout the passenger re-checks and removes itself under the same lock, so a driver is never handed to someone who left. At scale I would return futures instead of parking a thread per passenger, and across servers I would claim drivers with a conditional update.

Cached chunk reader over slow storage

Concurrent systemshard45 minDatabricks low level design round (CachedFile over a storage client)Databricks

Databricks asks for a file object that serves reads of any byte range from slow remote storage, makes repeated reads cheap, and makes as few network calls as possible. The concurrency is the hard part: two readers that miss the same chunk at the same moment must share one fetch, and one slow fetch must not stall readers of other chunks.

The task

A remote file is split into fixed size chunks. The harness gives you a ChunkSource whose fetchChunk(i) returns chunk i (bytes i * chunkSize up to (i + 1) * chunkSize, the last chunk shorter). Each fetch takes tens of milliseconds and may fail. Build read(offset, length) on top of it:

  • Return the bytes from offset up to offset + length, cut short at the end of the data. A read that starts at or past the end returns an empty result. Fetch only the chunks the range touches, and keep positions in 64 bits: files can be larger than 4 GiB. In Java the returned array is the caller's own, never the cached one.
  • Cache chunks: each chunk is fetched at most once, even when many threads read it at the same time.
  • A read that needs several missing chunks fetches them in parallel, with at most threads fetches for that read. Do not leak threads: the number of live threads must not grow with the number of reads.
  • Readers of different chunks never wait for each other's fetches, however many slow fetches other reads have in flight.
  • If a fetch fails, every reader waiting for that chunk gets that fetch's exception (in Java it may be wrapped, as long as the original is in the cause chain); do not retry. The failure is not cached: a later read fetches the chunk again. A failure fails only the reads that need that chunk: the other chunks a failing read claimed still reach their readers.

Worked example

With chunks of 1024 bytes, read(1000, 100) needs bytes 1000 to 1099: the last 24 bytes of chunk 0 and the first 76 of chunk 1. If neither is cached, fetch both at the same time, then copy the two pieces into the result. If eight threads call read(5130, 100) at the same instant on a cold cache, exactly one of them fetches chunk 5; the other seven wait for that same fetch.

What the tests check

  • Exact bytes for reads inside a chunk, across chunk boundaries, of whole chunks, of the whole file, of the last byte, running past the end, starting past the end, and of length 0.
  • After reading everything three times, every chunk was fetched exactly once.
  • Only the needed chunks are fetched: a range that ends exactly on a chunk edge does not fetch the next chunk, and no chunk past the end of an 8 chunk file is ever requested.
  • Reads past 3 GiB and 4 GiB of a 5 GiB file with 1 MiB chunks are exact (a chunk start computed in int overflows).
  • Java only: zeroing the array a read returned does not change a later read of the same chunk, and 64 reads in a row leave at most 4 extra threads running.
  • 8 threads doing 150 random reads each: exact bytes, and no chunk fetched twice.
  • A burst: 8 threads miss the same chunk at the same moment, and it is fetched once. Then 40 rounds in which 8 spinning threads read the same 8 cold chunks in the same instant: no chunk is ever fetched twice, so a claim that checks and then inserts in two steps is caught.
  • A read of 8 missing chunks with 4 threads keeps 3 or 4 fetches in flight; a read of 4 missing chunks with 2 threads keeps exactly 2.
  • While 4 readers wait 400 ms each for chunks 0 to 3, and a read of chunks 3 to 6 waits for chunk 3, a read of chunks 4 to 20 finishes in well under 200 ms. A lock held across a fetch, a pool or semaphore of threads shared by all reads, and a read that waits for other reads' chunks before starting its own fetches all fail this.
  • 4 readers wait on one fetch that fails: all 4 get its exception (the message of the original error must reach each of them), and a later read fetches the chunk again and succeeds.
  • With threads = 1, a read of chunks 10 to 13 whose chunk 10 fails throws, a concurrent read of chunk 13 still gets its bytes, and chunks 11 to 13 are fetched once each.
  • In C++, ThreadSanitizer must stay quiet.

Taught in: Parallel copies, chunked reads and caches over slow storage

Open them one at a time. Each one gives away a little more.

Do not cache bytes; cache the promise of bytes. Map each chunk number to a future. A reader that finds a future just waits on it, whether the fetch is finished or still running.
Claiming a chunk must be one atomic step, or two readers both decide to fetch. In Java, putIfAbsent on a ConcurrentHashMap with a fresh CompletableFuture: whoever gets null back owns the fetch. (With computeIfAbsent, the caller whose mapping function ran owns it; never put the fetch itself inside the mapping function.) In C++, do the lookup and the insert of a std::shared_future under one mutex, keep the matching std::promise, and release the mutex before fetching.
Structure read in three steps: claim every needed chunk (remembering which ones you own), fetch the ones you own in parallel (your thread plus up to threads - 1 helpers pulling from an atomic counter, then join them), and only then wait on every needed future and copy the pieces out. Fetch your own chunks before you wait for anyone else's.
On a failed fetch, remove the entry from the map and then complete the future with the exception, so waiting readers see the error and later readers start a fresh fetch. Every future you claimed must be completed on every path, or its waiters hang forever.

What the interviewer asks after your first version works. Try to answer each one out loud before you open it.

The cache is unbounded. How would you bound it?
Keep an LRU order of completed chunks and evict when over capacity. Evict only completed entries (never an in flight future that readers wait on), and accept that an evicted chunk may be fetched again. For a real file, cache chunks on local disk rather than in memory, as the Databricks candidate did, and keep a small in memory index.
How would you pick the chunk size?
Large enough that per request overhead (latency, request cost) is amortized, small enough that a small read does not fetch a lot of unused data and that parallel fetches have enough chunks to spread over. Object stores often work well with chunks of a few MB; it is a measured trade-off, not a constant.
Reads are often sequential. How would you make them faster?
Read ahead: when a reader touches chunk i, start fetching i + 1 and i + 2 in the background so they are cached when needed. The future map makes this safe, because a later reader just finds the in flight future. Cap the read ahead so random readers do not waste bandwidth.
Why not one lock around the whole cache, held during the fetch?
It is correct and it dedups fetches, but every reader then waits for every fetch, even of unrelated chunks, and nothing runs in parallel. The future map holds a lock only for the lookup and insert, so the waiting happens per chunk.
The file can change on the server. What then?
Key the cache by (file id, version or ETag, chunk index), and check the version when the file is opened, so a changed file never mixes old and new chunks. Without a version you cannot know whether cached chunks are stale.
Would you retry failed fetches inside the reader?
Possibly a small number of retries with backoff for transient errors, done by the owning fetch so waiters share the result. Beyond that, surface the error: the caller decides. What you must not do is cache the failure forever, or leave waiters hanging.

Try for at least twenty minutes first. Reading a solution feels like learning; writing one is learning.

The idea

This is single flight applied to chunks. The map holds a future per chunk, and inserting it is the claim. The thread whose insert succeeds fetches; everyone else, now or later, waits on the same future. The lock (or the concurrent map's internal locking) is held only for the claim, never for the network call, so a slow chunk only delays the readers who need that chunk.

Invariants

  • For each chunk, at most one future is in the map at a time, and only the thread that inserted it fetches.
  • Every future a reader inserted is completed, with bytes or with an exception, before that reader returns.
  • A failed future is removed from the map before it is completed, so no reader can start waiting on it after the failure is known, except readers already racing with it, who see the error.

Why each primitive

  • CompletableFuture and putIfAbsent in Java, std::promise, std::shared_future and a mutex in C++: the future both stores the result and lets any number of threads wait for it. shared_future is needed in C++ because many readers call get on it.
  • An atomic counter for the read's own fetches: the caller plus helpers each claim the next owned chunk, which caps the read at threads fetches at once and lets fast threads take more work.
  • Join the helpers before waiting: the read must not return while its own fetches are still running, because other readers depend on those futures being completed.

Pitfalls

  • get, then fetch on a miss, then put: two readers who miss together both fetch. This is the broken version the tests catch. A get (or containsKey) followed by a separate put of a future has the same flaw with a tiny window, which is why the tests also run spinning bursts.
  • Holding one lock across the fetch: no duplicate fetches, but no parallelism either.
  • computeIfAbsent(i, k -> source.fetchChunk(k)): it dedups, but ConcurrentHashMap may block updates of other keys that share a bin while the function runs (its documentation asks for a short computation), and a failure is not delivered to the waiters: each of them runs the fetch again.
  • One pool or semaphore of threads shared by every read: fine under light load, but a few slow fetches fill it and every other reader queues behind them. A pool created per read and never shut down leaks threads with every read.
  • Waiting for chunks other reads are fetching before fetching your own: readers of your chunks now wait for chunks they never asked for, and in Java, where claims are made one chunk at a time, two reads can end up waiting for each other forever.
  • Caching the failed future: the chunk stays broken forever. Retrying the fetch for the waiters instead of failing them hides the error the caller asked to see.
  • Stopping at the first failed fetch: the other chunks the read claimed are never completed, so their waiters hang (Java) or get a broken promise once the std::promise is destroyed (C++). Every claimed future is completed on every path.
  • An exception escaping a helper std::thread calls std::terminate; catch inside the thread and put the error in the promise.
  • Returning the cached array itself in Java: the caller can change your cache.
  • Off by one errors at chunk edges: test the last byte, a read across a boundary, a read that ends exactly on a chunk edge, and a read past the end. Compute (long) i * chunkSize, not i * chunkSize, or offsets past 2 GiB go wrong (in C++ the int overflow is undefined behavior, which the optimizer may hide on one compiler and not another).

Cost

A read of k chunks does O(k) map operations, at most k fetches (only for chunks nobody fetched before), and copies length bytes. Memory is one chunk per distinct chunk ever read, which is why bounding the cache is the first follow up.

How to say it in the interview

I cache futures, not bytes. A read computes which chunks it needs and, for each one, does a putIfAbsent of a fresh future: if mine went in, I own that fetch; otherwise I wait on the existing one. That makes the claim atomic, so a chunk is fetched once even under a burst, and nobody holds a lock during the network call. The chunks I own are fetched in parallel with a bounded number of threads, then I wait on all the futures and stitch the pieces together. A failed fetch is removed from the map before its future fails, so waiters see the error and the next reader retries.

Thread-safe rate limiters

Build thread-safe token buckets and window limiters, choose their guarantees, and handle clocks, per-key eviction, blocking callers and distributed quotas.

Asked atRubrikLinkedInAtlassianMicrosoftSnowflake
Before this:The lost update: read, modify, writeCheck then act: two safe calls, one unsafe sequenceConcurrent hash maps

A rate limiter is a tiny piece of state, a count and a time, that many threads read and change at once. That is why interviewers love it: one small class tests check-then-act, clocks, per key maps and memory, and then grows into a distributed systems question.

In plain words

A temple hands out darshan passes. A helper drops one new pass into a box every 30 seconds, and the box holds at most 10. A visitor who finds a pass in the box takes it and goes in; a visitor who finds the box empty is turned away. Ten people who arrive together after a quiet hour all get in (the burst), but after those saved passes run out, new passes arrive at two per minute (the sustained rate). That box is a token bucket.

The trouble starts with two helpers at two gates sharing one box. Both look, both see the last pass, both wave their visitor in. Shared decisions, elapsed time and retained per-client state all need explicit rules.

Five algorithms in one table

All five answer "may this request go now?" for a limit like "100 per minute". They differ in how they treat bursts, how much they remember and how exact they are.

AlgorithmStateBurstsMemory per keyExact?
Token buckettokens and the time of the last updateAllows a burst up to the capacity, then the steady rateTwo numbersYes, for its own definition
Leaky bucketa bounded queue with paced dispatchAbsorbs bursts in the queue and lets them out evenlyThe queuePaces dispatch; scheduling can delay it
Fixed window counterthe current window and its countUp to twice the limit across a window boundaryTwo numbersExact for aligned windows, not rolling windows
Sliding logthe time of every admitted request in the windowNever more than the limit in any windowUp to the limit, per keyYes
Sliding window countercounts for the current and the previous windowCan over-admit or under-admit in a rolling windowThree numbersApproximately: it assumes the previous window was evenly spread

A useful way to remember the trade: the token bucket and the counters are tiny and fast; the sliding log is exact but remembers every request; the queue form of the leaky bucket delays accepted work. A token bucket with blocking acquisition can also delay work; the policy, not just the algorithm name, determines whether requests wait.

The race every limiter has

Every algorithm ends in the same three steps: bring the state up to date, check whether there is room, and record the request. Without synchronization covering that whole decision, this check then act can over-admit:

LinkedIn interviewers have asked candidates to walk through exactly this "both threads see 99 of 100" interleaving. In C++ it is also a data race: the C++ program has undefined behaviour, and ThreadSanitizer can flag the race even when the count happens to be 100. The displayed output and interleaving illustrate the logical bug, not a bound on C++ behaviour; a clean run is not proof of correctness. The fix is to make the update, the check and the record one atomic step, with a lock or an atomic representation that covers the entire state transition.

A token bucket, done right

Four decisions turn the idea into a correct class:

  • One lock around refill, check and take. The critical section is a few arithmetic operations, so a plain lock is the right first answer.
  • Lazy refill. Do not run a thread that adds tokens on a timer. Store the token count and the time you last updated it; on each request, add elapsed time times rate (capped at the capacity) and move the timestamp. It needs no refill thread and avoids missed timer ticks. Clock resolution, arithmetic precision and memory still matter.
  • A monotonic clock. Measure elapsed time with System.nanoTime() or std::chrono::steady_clock, never with wall clock time (currentTimeMillis, system_clock), which jumps when the machine syncs its clock and can make "elapsed time" negative or huge.
  • An injectable clock. Take the clock as a parameter, so a test can freeze time and step it forward. Testing a limiter with real sleeps is slow and flaky.

Sample contract. Capacities and window limits are positive, floating-point token capacities are finite and between one and 2^53 inclusive, rates are finite and nonnegative, windows are positive nanosecond durations, and idle thresholds are nonnegative. Above 2^53, subtracting one from a double can leave it unchanged, so a larger budget needs another representation. Clocks must be non-null, thread-safe, nondecreasing, fast, and must not call back into the limiter. The fragments below assume validated inputs. For their signed nanosecond arithmetic, use one process-local origin and keep timestamps and future deadlines nonnegative and within the signed 64-bit range.

System.nanoTime() may be negative and may wrap; compare elapsed differences, not calendar timestamps. Its unit is nanoseconds, not a promise of nanosecond resolution. For an origin-relative clock use System.nanoTime() - origin in Java, or a steady_clock duration since a captured origin in C++; keep the origin alive with the limiter. Never compare these timestamps across processes.

The bucket starts full, so a new client can burst immediately. Mathematically, a bucket of capacity B refilled at rate r admits at most B + r * t unit requests over an interval of length t, with consistent endpoint conventions. It does not promise at most r requests in every second. Floating-point credit is convenient but can round at admission boundaries; strict accounting needs bounded integer or fixed-point arithmetic with the fractional remainder retained. Never divide elapsed nanoseconds as an integer before accumulating partial credit.

The leaky bucket: two things with one name

As a meter, a leaky bucket is a level that rises by one per request and drains at the rate; a request that would overflow is rejected. That is the token bucket upside down, with the same code. Rubrik reported a leaky bucket class, without specifying which interpretation in the notes. In the queue interpretation, requests wait in a bounded queue and leave at a steady pace, so a burst in becomes an even stream out, at the cost of added delay. It needs a bounded queue, a drainer, and a rule for a full queue (reject at once).

This serial drainer waits a full period after each callback finishes. It prevents catch-up bursts after a slow callback or scheduling pause, but slow work reduces throughput below the configured ceiling. It is not a real-time scheduler. Shutdown rejects new work and discards queued work; an already dequeued callback may finish.

Java

scheduleAtFixedRate can run delayed invocations close together to catch up. scheduleWithFixedDelay waits after completion instead. An exception escaping a periodic task suppresses later executions; this example reports ordinary callback failures and continues, but an Error can still stop the drainer. shutdown() returns without waiting for an active callback.

C++

This uses C++17-compatible thread and condition-variable APIs. The predicate wait allows shutdown to interrupt the pacing delay, and the destructor joins the worker. Shutdown moves queued callbacks out before destroying them: a captured object's destructor can call user code, so clearing the queue while holding its mutex could deadlock on re-entry. Destroy the limiter only after external callers have stopped, and never from its own callback. Destruction can wait indefinitely if a callback never returns; cooperative cancellation or bounded callbacks are separate requirements.

Windows: fixed, sliding log, and the sliding counter

Window algorithms count requests in a time window instead of tracking tokens. A fixed window counter divides time into windows of length W (each minute, say) and allows K requests per window. It is two numbers per key, but a client can send K at the end of one window and K at the start of the next: 2K in a moment. A sliding log fixes that exactly by remembering when each admitted request happened and counting only those in the last W:

The sliding log uses the interval (now - W, now]: entries exactly W old expire before admission. Trimming is amortized O(1) per admitted timestamp, but one request after a long idle period can remove O(K) entries. The counters below align windows to construction time; use a common origin when calendar-aligned or cross-key boundaries are part of the contract.

Rubrik's 2022 "accepted requests free their slot after an expiry time" question is this structure in disguise: K slots, each held for a fixed time, is a sliding log of at most K timestamps. The sliding window counter uses constant memory by estimating instead: it keeps the counts of the current and previous fixed windows and estimates the requests in the last W as "the part of the previous window still inside, times its count, plus the current count".

Worked example: one minute boundary, three answers

The limit is 100 requests per minute, and fixed boundaries are aligned to the minute. A client sent 90 requests at 12:00:59 and nothing earlier. At 12:01:01 it sends a flood, all evaluated at that frozen timestamp. How many more does each algorithm admit right away?

AlgorithmWhat it sees at 12:01:01Admits
Fixed window (windows start on the minute)A new window with a count of 0100, so 190 in two seconds
Sliding log90 requests in the last 60 seconds10
Sliding window counter1 second into the new window, so 59/60 of the previous window counts: 90 times 59/60 is 88.511: 88.5 + 11 = 99.5, but the twelfth would make 100.5

The counter is close here, but at 12:01:30 its previous-window contribution is only 45, although all 90 old requests are still inside the last minute. New requests contribute separately. If old traffic was instead bunched near the start of the previous window, the estimate can overcount and reject unnecessarily. That is the trade you name in the interview: three numbers per key instead of a log, in exchange for an estimate whose error depends on the actual arrival pattern.

Many keys: a bucket per key, and throwing idle ones away

Real limiters are per user, per IP or per API key. Keep a concurrent map from key to bucket, create each bucket exactly once with an atomic "get or create", and let each bucket have its own lock so unrelated keys can update independently. Map lookup, creation, hash collisions and maintenance can still contend. Two mistakes to avoid: one global lock around the whole map and every bucket (all clients serialize), and a map that only grows (a million IPs that each sent one request a month ago still hold memory).

The eviction insight is short: a token bucket that has refilled to capacity behaves exactly like a brand new bucket, so deleting a full, idle bucket loses nothing. A background sweeper can remove those. The race to handle: a request thread may have looked up a bucket just before the sweeper removed it. Mark the bucket retired under its own lock; a thread that finds it retired simply looks the key up again.

Note the lock order: the sweeper holds the map lock and then a bucket lock, while a request thread releases the map lock before it takes the bucket lock. No thread ever waits for the map while holding a bucket, so the two cannot deadlock. In C++, the local shared_ptr in the sweeper must outlive the bucket lock guard; otherwise erasing the final map reference destroys a locked mutex. This simple sweeper holds the map write lock for the scan, so it pauses lookups; a production implementation can scan shards or small batches. A generic cache eviction policy is insufficient unless it also preserves budget and prevents an old reference from admitting alongside a replacement.

The whole limiter in one atomic number

When the lock really is the bottleneck (measure first), a token bucket can be rewritten as the generic cell rate algorithm, GCRA, which keeps one number: the theoretical arrival time, when the schedule says the next request is due. A request at time now is allowed if that time is not too far ahead of now (the burst tolerance), and allowing it pushes the time one interval further. One number means one compare-and-set loop:

For unit requests, this is mathematically equivalent to a full token bucket with capacity burst and one token every interval nanoseconds. It takes the integer interval directly to avoid silently truncating 1e9 / rate: rounding that division down can over-admit, and a zero interval removes the limit. If converting a requested rate, round the interval up and state the resulting slightly lower rate. Floating-point token accounting need not agree at every rounded boundary.

A successful compare-and-swap commits the admission; rejection leaves the arrival time unchanged. Use a nonnegative origin-relative clock and bounded deadlines as stated above; the code checks tolerance and deadline overflow. A CAS loop can starve a particular caller, and the absence of an explicit mutex does not guarantee a lock-free implementation. In C++ check tat_.is_lock_free() and ensure the clock callback does not block. See the safety ladder.

Across machines, and what a rate limit does not do

Across servers, an authority can atomically check and update each key, for example in a server-side script. An increment followed by a separate expiry call leaves a failure window; a fixed-window design must define both atomically or tolerate abandoned counters safely. Use the authority's time or another explicit clock policy, not unrelated process-local monotonic timestamps. Define fail-closed, fail-open or bounded local fallback during outages; fail-open sacrifices the limit.

A rate limit bounds requests per second, not requests in flight. In a stable system, Little's law gives an average of 5 requests in flight at 100 requests per second and 50 ms latency. At 5 seconds that becomes 500 on average, and the limiter happily lets you drown. That needs a concurrency limit, usually a semaphore, as rate versus concurrency limits explains.

Common mistakes

  • Refill, check and take in separate steps. Two threads see the last token. One lock (or one CAS on one number) around all three.
  • Wall clock time. currentTimeMillis and system_clock jump when the clock is corrected; a jump backwards can make elapsed time negative. Use nanoTime or steady_clock.
  • A refill thread per bucket, or a timer that refills every bucket. A timer per key adds overhead, and counting callbacks loses elapsed time when scheduling is delayed. A shared timer using actual elapsed time can be correct but still does unnecessary scans. Refill lazily from elapsed time.
  • Losing the remainder. Adding whole tokens per elapsed interval and then setting last = now throws away the partial interval, so callers that come faster than one interval apart never earn a token. Keep fractional tokens, or preserve the unconverted time while below capacity. On reaching capacity, discard overflow credit, including its fractional remainder: otherwise a full bucket secretly banks more than its stated burst.
  • One global lock for all keys. Every client waits for every other. Use per-bucket locks for decisions and keep map-lock scope short. The example sweeper still serializes map access during its scan.
  • A map that only grows. Evict idle full buckets with retirement and identity-checked removal. Also define a maximum key count or a policy for floods of new keys; idle expiry is not a hard memory bound.
  • Sleeping inside the lock. A blocking acquire that sleeps while holding the bucket's lock blocks every other caller. Use a timed condition wait that releases the lock and recheck on waking, or make an explicit reservation and wait outside the lock.

In the interview

The research notes contain several concrete versions of this question. At Rubrik in 2022: a rate limiter with a capacity whose accepted requests free their slot after an expiry time, done single threaded, then concurrent, then distributed; a leaky bucket class, followed by a "bad password" lockout limiter with a map from user to bucket; and a token-bucket-style task inferred from one candidate's comments about background refill versus computing tokens from the last request time. LinkedIn (Staff, 2026): a thread-safe sliding window allowRequest(clientId, maxRequests, windowSeconds), a walk through the race where two threads both see 99 of 100, then local counters against a shared store. Atlassian: fixed against sliding windows, why a concurrent map, per user limits and credits for unused requests. Microsoft: a single machine limiter with threads, then without, then distributed. A secondary Snowflake report describes a tiered limiter whose limits change at runtime, tested with a mocked clock, then distributed.

I will use a token bucket per key: it allows bursts up to the capacity and enforces the average rate. Each bucket refills lazily from a monotonic clock, so there is no timer thread, and refill, check and take happen under the bucket's own lock, so two threads can never both take the last token. I create each bucket atomically through the map and retire it under its own lock before evicting it. Only full buckets with unchanged configuration are equivalent to fresh ones. Callers holding a retired reference retry the lookup. The clock is injected so I can test bursts and refill without sleeping.

Follow ups to expect: "Credits for unused requests" (a token bucket already banks unused capacity up to its capacity). "Change the limits at runtime" (serialize reconfiguration with admission; refill at the old rate to the change time, clamp tokens to the new capacity, then install the new rate). "Block instead of rejecting" (wait with a deadline and recheck, or reserve a slot and define cancellation). "Make it distributed." "Why not a fixed window?" "How do you test it?" (a fake clock, a burst at time zero, a step forward, and many threads checking the exact admitted count).

Senior depth

Weighted requests and waiting. For acquire(n), require n > 0; a strict bucket rejects n > capacity, since it can never accumulate enough. Under the lock, refill and take n only when enough credit exists. Otherwise, if the rate is positive, wait roughly (n - tokens) / rate with a timed condition wait that releases the lock, then recheck both budget and deadline. Time alone can create tokens, so waiting only for a notification can stall forever. A zero refill rate cannot satisfy an empty bucket without a configuration change.

Reservations, cancellation and fairness. Reserving future capacity under the lock and sleeping outside it is another policy, but a caller interrupted before use must not simply roll back the shared tail when later reservations exist. Keep the unused reservation charged, or maintain a cancellable reservation queue. Recheck monotonic deadlines after waking, propagate Java interruption, and define shutdown. Unordered waiters can starve a large request while small requests keep consuming credit; FIFO reservations improve fairness at the cost of head-of-line blocking. Rate tokens are normally consumed permanently, unlike semaphore permits returned on completion.

Changing limits safely. An immutable configuration reference gives a consistent rate/capacity pair, but does not coordinate it with token state. Update under the same bucket lock: refill at the old rate to the change instant, clamp to the new capacity, install the new rate and timestamp, and wake waiters. Increasing capacity need not mint extra credit. A lazy per-key update needs versioned change times or a stated effective-time policy so it does not apply the new rate retroactively.

Hot keys and global quotas. GCRA removes the explicit bucket mutex but still contends on one atomic cache line. Static disjoint shares whose sums stay within the total rate and burst can preserve a hard global bound, while rejecting early under uneven traffic. Rebalancing requires revoking or expiring old grants before reissuing them; overlapping grants or reinitializing full buckets can create budget. Chunk leasing reduces network calls only if unused and outstanding grants are accounted for.

Several limits on one request. A global bucket and a per-user bucket require an explicit composition policy. Acquiring one and then failing the other can waste credit; blindly refunding later can conflict with refill and other admissions. For all-or-nothing admission in one process, take all relevant bucket locks in a fixed order, refill each, check every budget, then debit all or none. A fixed lock order prevents deadlock but can increase contention. Across servers this needs an authority that atomically covers all affected budgets, or a documented conservative policy that may charge rejected attempts.

Test the contract. Freeze time for a simultaneous burst, advance just short of and exactly to a refill or expiry boundary, and verify fractional accumulation and the capacity cap after long idle periods. Race lookups with eviction, check independent keys and configuration changes, and count admissions rather than just final tokens. ThreadSanitizer finds exercised C++ data races; a lost logical invariant between atomic operations needs assertions too.

Key takeaways

  • Token bucket for bursts plus a rate, leaky bucket to smooth output, fixed window for simplicity, sliding log for exactness, sliding window counter for a constant-space estimate.
  • Every limiter's refill, check and record must be one atomic step: a lock per key, or one CAS on one number.
  • Refill lazily from a monotonic clock, and inject the clock so tests can control time.
  • Per key: a concurrent map, an atomic get-or-create, a lock per bucket, and eviction of idle full buckets.
  • Distributed limits need one atomic decision in a shared store or quotas split across servers, and a rate limit is not a concurrency limit.

Quick check

Answer each question, then read why. Your score is saved for your module test.

A limiter has admitted 99 of 100 requests. Two threads each atomically load count, check it is below 100, then atomically increment and return true. The check and increment are separate operations. Which statement about the printed results is correct?
Why. Both loads can see 99, then both increments can succeed, producing count 101 and two successful admissions. No data race is needed: each operation is atomic but the decision is not. Exactly one admission is possible but not guaranteed; both rejections are impossible with only these two callers and this initial count, and atomic increments do not throw on crossing a policy limit. Use one lock or a conditional CAS loop.
Which clock should measure elapsed time for refilling a token bucket?
Why. Wall clock time can be corrected backwards or forwards at any moment, which corrupts elapsed time. Elapsed-time sources avoid wall-clock corrections. Clock values may repeat at the available resolution, and Java nanoTime values may wrap, so use bounded differences. Choosing milliseconds does not make wall time monotonic. Counting timer callbacks misses time when dispatch is delayed.
A fixed window counter allows 100 requests per minute, with windows starting on the minute. What is the most a client can get through in about two seconds?
Why. 100 at 12:00:59 fill the old window, and 100 at 12:01:00 fill the new one. A two-second interval can overlap at most two minute windows, so 200 is achievable and cannot be exceeded. Neither 100 nor 101 is the maximum, and each window's finite quota rules out unlimited admissions. An exact sliding log removes this boundary burst; a weighted sliding counter only approximates a rolling limit.
Spot the bug. A bucket refills whole tokens: long add = (now - last) / intervalNanos; tokens = Math.min(capacity, tokens + add); last = now;. Requests arrive every 0.5 intervals. What happens?
Why. Assuming a positive interval and bounded arithmetic, every division yields zero and last = now discards the partial interval for good. It neither refills normally nor twice as fast, and integer division by a positive interval does not throw. Keep fractional tokens, or advance last by add * intervalNanos while below capacity so the remainder carries over. At capacity discard overflow credit rather than banking it beyond the cap.
A token bucket has capacity 10 and a rate of 1 token per second. It has been idle for a minute when 30 unit requests are evaluated at the same frozen timestamp. How many are admitted immediately?
Why. Tokens accumulate only up to capacity, so after a minute there are 10, not 60. The rate of 1 does not restrict the initial burst to one request, and 30 exceeds the stored budget. No time passes during the decisions, so exactly 10 succeed; the other 20 must wait for future tokens or be rejected. Capacity sets the burst, rate sets the long run average.
A sweeper verifies fullness under the bucket lock, retires the bucket so old users retry, then removes that exact mapping. New buckets start full with the same configuration. Why does this preserve admission history?
Why. A new bucket starts full, so a full bucket with unchanged configuration carries no budget history worth keeping. It can certainly be used again, but retirement forces such callers to retry with the current mapping. A concurrent map alone cannot stop old references from admitting. The stated retirement and identity checks close that race, so exceeding the limit is not inevitable. Partly empty buckets must stay because they remember recent traffic.
For a limit of K requests per window, how much memory does a sliding log use per key in the worst case?
Why. It stores at most K admitted timestamps, plus constant metadata, so worst-case space is O(K). Rejections add no timestamps, and window duration alone does not determine the count. Constant space describes counters, not an exact log. With large K and many keys, use an approximate counter only if the contract permits its error.

Checkpoint

Write your own answer first. Then compare with the model answer, or ask Bar Raiser to mark it like an interviewer would.

Write code
LinkedIn's version: implement allowRequest(clientId) that allows at most maxRequests per client in any rolling window, thread-safe for many concurrent clients, with an injectable clock.
Model answer

Each client gets its own log, created once with an atomic get-or-create, and its own lock, so the trimming, the check and the record are one atomic decision per client while different clients run in parallel. Rejected requests are not recorded, so a client that keeps retrying is not punished further; recording them is a valid stricter policy if you say so. Memory is up to maxRequests timestamps per active client; to reclaim an idle log, trim expired entries and verify emptiness under its lock, mark it retired, then conditionally remove that exact mapping. Callers holding an old reference must retry lookup, just like the token-bucket retirement protocol. Merely checking emptiness and removing the mapping permits two live logs for one client. This fragment deliberately omits eviction; idle reclamation alone also does not cap the number of simultaneously active keys.

Design
Your limiter runs on 20 servers behind a load balancer, and each customer may make 1,000 requests per minute in total. Give two designs and their trade-offs.
Model answer

Design one: a shared store. Every request makes one atomic call to a shared in-memory store, for example a script that checks a fixed-window count and manages expiry atomically, or runs a sliding-log or token-bucket transition in one step. It is exact (up to the window algorithm) and simple, but it adds a network round trip to every request, the store becomes a dependency on the hot path, and one hot customer is one hot key. Decide what happens during outages: fail closed preserves the bound but rejects traffic; fail open sacrifices it. Local fallback must use separately reserved budget to preserve a strict global bound. Failure and replication semantics also matter: losing accepted counts during failover can over-admit.

Design two: local buckets with shares of the quota. For aligned fixed windows, each server can enforce 1,000 divided by 20, so 50 per window, with no per-request network call. The sum preserves the same global fixed-window bound, but uneven routing wastes quota and it is not an exact rolling-window policy. A coordinator can transfer unused grants only after revoking or expiring the old authority. A hybrid leases chunks from a shared store; it can retain a hard bound if all outstanding grants are counted and never duplicated, at the cost of stranded unused capacity.

Numbers
A sliding window counter allows 100 per minute. The previous minute admitted 80 requests, and the current minute has admitted 30 so far. It is 15 seconds into the current minute. Is the next request allowed? How many more could be admitted right now?
Model answer

15 seconds into the window, the share of the previous window still inside the last minute is 1 minus 15/60, so 0.75. The estimate is 80 times 0.75 plus 30, which is 60 plus 30, so 90. 90 plus the next unit request is at most 100, so it is allowed. A request is admitted while 60 plus the current count plus one is at most 100, so requests keep being admitted as the count climbs from 30 to 40, which is 10 more right now; at a count of 40 the estimate reaches 100 and the next request is rejected. As time passes, the previous window's weight shrinks by 80/60 of a request per second, so roughly one more request is allowed every 0.75 seconds as the estimated contribution of the previous window fades, not because this algorithm has tokens.

"Design a thread-safe limiter that allows bursts of ten and then two requests per second. How would you prove it works?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Choose a token bucket with capacity ten and rate two, starting full
  • Put refill, timestamp update, check and consumption in one critical section
  • Use injected monotonic elapsed time and preserve fractional credit
  • Explain the last-token interleaving and the admission bound
  • Test frozen-time contention and exact refill boundaries without sleeps
  • Distinguish rate control from concurrency control
How a senior would say it
I would use a token bucket with capacity ten, starting full, and refill it at two tokens per second. That allows the initial burst and then the sustained rate. On each call I take the bucket lock, read an injected monotonic clock, add elapsed time times the rate up to capacity, and update the timestamp. I admit only if a token is available, and consume it before releasing the lock. The whole transition must be protected: otherwise two callers can both see the last token and both return success. Fractional credit must survive between calls. The mathematical bound is the initial capacity plus the rate times elapsed time, not two requests in every rolling second. For tests I freeze time, launch many callers, and require exactly ten successes. Then I advance just short of half a second and to exactly half a second, checking when the next request passes. I also test a long idle period to verify the cap. Finally, this bounds admissions, so I would add a separate concurrency limit if slow downstream work could accumulate.

Likely follow up: What would you change for requests that each consume a different number of tokens?

"The requirement is at most one hundred requests in any rolling minute. Which algorithm would you choose, and why not the cheaper counter?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Choose an exact sliding log for the strict rolling-window requirement
  • Expire timestamps at the specified boundary and atomically check and append
  • Explain a fixed-window boundary burst with concrete numbers
  • Explain the weighted counter's approximation and possible over-admission
  • State O(K) storage and amortized O(1) trimming with an O(K) worst call
  • Use deterministic boundary and concurrent-last-slot tests
How a senior would say it
I would use a sliding log because the requirement says any rolling minute. For each client I keep the timestamps of admitted requests. Under one client lock, I remove entries at least one minute old, reject if one hundred remain, and otherwise append the current timestamp. That defines the window as excluding its left endpoint and including now. A fixed counter would allow one hundred requests just before a minute boundary and another hundred immediately after it, which violates this requirement. The weighted sliding counter saves memory, but assumes the previous window's requests were evenly distributed. If ninety requests arrived at its very end, halfway through the next window they still all count in reality, while the estimate counts only forty-five. So it can over-admit even when its arithmetic is implemented correctly. The exact log stores at most one hundred timestamps per client. Each timestamp is removed once, giving amortized constant work, although one call can trim the entire log. I would test the exact expiry boundary and race several callers for the final available slot.

Likely follow up: How would you bound memory if an attacker continually sends requests with new client identifiers?

"You delete idle full buckets, but production sometimes admits twice the burst for one customer. Walk me through the likely race."
What a strong answer covers
  • Identify simultaneous use of an old bucket and its replacement
  • Verify fullness and mark retirement while holding the bucket lock
  • Require old-reference callers to retry and remove only the matching mapping
  • Keep the C++ bucket alive until its lock guard has released the mutex
  • Account for unchanged configuration and active-key memory limits
  • Describe a targeted eviction-versus-admission test
How a senior would say it
I would look for two live buckets making decisions for the same key. A request may obtain the old bucket, pause, and then resume after a sweeper removes it and another request creates a full replacement. If both buckets can admit, the customer receives two independent budgets. Fullness alone is therefore insufficient. Under the old bucket's lock, the sweeper must verify that it is full and idle, then mark it retired. Any caller holding that reference checks the flag under the same lock and retries the map lookup. Removal must also match the bucket identity, so a stale sweeper cannot remove a replacement. In C++ I retain a local shared pointer through the entire critical section; erasing the map's last reference must not destroy a still-locked mutex. The full-equals-new argument assumes unchanged configuration. It also only reclaims idle keys, so I still need a policy for too many active keys. My test would pause a caller after lookup, race retirement and replacement, then verify that the old reference cannot grant another burst.

Likely follow up: Can a generic cache with time-based eviction provide these guarantees by itself?

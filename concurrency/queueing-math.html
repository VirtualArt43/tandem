Queueing math for design rounds

Little's law four ways, why latency explodes near full utilization, waiting time versus time in system, and how fan-out multiplies tail latency.

Asked atDatabricksMetaUber
Before this:Sizing a pool

In a design interview, you need to turn traffic and latency into a capacity estimate. Little's law, a queueing model and a fan-out calculation let you explain the numbers, provided you state their assumptions.

In plain words

A bank branch has one teller, and each customer takes 5 minutes on average. If customers arrive about every 10 minutes, the teller is busy half the time and has room to catch up after a rush. If they arrive about every 5 and a half minutes, the teller is about 90 percent busy, and the queue can stretch out of the door, even though the teller is still idle one minute in ten.

Two things ruin you together: being busy most of the time, and randomness. Customers do not arrive evenly, and the teller's idle minutes cannot be saved up for the rush.

The vocabulary

SymbolMeaningExample: one M/M/1 server
λ (lambda)Admitted arrival rate: requests per second70 per second
SMean service time: time occupying a server, excluding the queue10 ms

WTime in system: mean time inside the chosen boundary, W = Wq + S33 ms
LMean number in the system: waiting plus being served (also called "in flight")70 × 0.033, about 2.3

Keep W and Wq apart from the first minute. Calling one the other gives the wrong latency, and the difference is a whole service time.

Little's law: L = λ × W

Little's law says that, averaged over time in a steady state, the number of requests in a system equals the arrival rate times the time each spends in it. It needs no assumption about how requests arrive, how long they take, or in what order they are served. Use finite long-run averages in a stable system, and make L, λ and W describe the same boundary and request population. Apply it to the queue alone and you get Lq = λ × Wq; to the servers alone and you get mean busy servers, λ × S, if each admitted request receives one service.

Loading the littles law simulator

It answers four kinds of questions:

  1. How much concurrency do I need? 2,000 requests per second at 50 ms each: L = 2,000 × 0.05 = 100 requests in flight on average, That means 100 occupied threads only if each request holds one thread for the entire 50 ms. Connections need a separate calculation using connection hold time; asynchronous requests need not occupy a thread while waiting.
  2. What is the most a fixed capacity can carry? 64 threads, each request holding one for 80 ms: the ideal service-capacity ceiling is 64 / 0.08 = 800 requests per second, assuming no other bottleneck. With variable arrivals and service, operate below that ceiling; it is not a stable latency target.
  3. How long is this queue, in time? 500 requests ahead in a first-in, first-out (FIFO) queue, drained at a sustained 1,000 per second: a new arrival waits about 500 / 1,000 = 0.5 s. This snapshot estimate assumes later arrivals cannot overtake it and the drain rate stays constant.
  4. Who got slow? Across two stable measurement windows, average in-flight requests rose from 100 to 800 while throughput stayed at 2,000 per second. Then W rose from 50 ms to 400 ms. The law diagnoses increased residence time, not its cause: investigate queues, locks, dependencies and scheduling.

Always convert units first. 2,000 per second times 50 milliseconds is 100, not 100,000. Do not substitute p99 for mean W: Little's law relates averages, not percentiles.

Count requests that actually enter the boundary, including failures and cancellations until they leave it. Offered traffic before rejection can exceed admitted throughput. A growing backlog is not steady state: during a short incident window, unfinished requests cross the window edges, so multiplying observed completion rate by completed-request latency need not reproduce current occupancy.

Utilization and the hockey stick

Little's law relates averages; it does not say how long the queue gets. For that you need a model. The classic model is M/M/1: one server, Poisson arrivals, independent exponential service times with mean S, FIFO service, and unlimited waiting room with no abandonment. These steady-state formulas require ρ = λ × S < 1; arbitrary "random" traffic is not enough:

  • Waiting time: Wq = S × ρ / (1 - ρ)
  • Time in system: W = S / (1 - ρ), which is S + Wq
  • Number in system: L = ρ / (1 - ρ), and you can check it with Little's law: λ × S / (1 - ρ) = ρ / (1 - ρ)
Utilization ρ50%70%80%90%95%99%
Waiting time, in service times: ρ / (1 - ρ)12.3491999
Time in system, in service times: 1 / (1 - ρ)23.351020100

The curve rises throughout and steepens continuously near 100 percent; there is no universal knee at 70 or 80 percent. Going from 50 to 90 percent utilization multiplies the waiting time by 9 while the load grew by only 1.8 times. That is why you keep headroom on the bottleneck, and why an unbounded queue in front of a busy resource can create extreme latency. Choose a utilization target from the latency objective, variability and pool size, then validate it under load.

Loading the utilization simulator

Two formulas, two meanings

Waiting time is S × ρ / (1 - ρ). Time in system is S / (1 - ρ). In this M/M/1 model at 90 percent with S = 10 ms: the mean wait is 90 ms and the mean total is 100 ms. The slip "1 / (1 - ρ) is the waiting time" is off by one service time, which matters most at low load: at 50 percent the wait equals S (10 ms) while the total is 2S (20 ms).

Tail latency

Averages hide the pain. Tail latency is the slow end: p99 is a latency threshold at or below which at least 99 percent of requests finish. In the stationary FIFO M/M/1 model the time in system follows an exponential distribution with mean W, so p99 is about ln(100), roughly 4.6 times the average. With S = 10 ms at 80 percent utilization: average 50 ms, p99 about 230 ms. Real systems add their own tail makers on top: garbage collection pauses, lock convoys, a slow replica, retries.

In this model p50 = ln(2) × W, so p50 and p99 grow by the same factor as load rises, although p99 rises much more in absolute time. In real systems, measure the full distribution; a mean alone cannot determine p99. MIT's FIFO M/M/1 derivation states the model assumptions.

Fan-out multiplies the tail

A request that fans out to n backends in parallel and waits for all of them is as slow as its slowest call. If calls are independent and each exceeds the same latency threshold with probability p = 0.01, the chance that at least one of n calls is slow is 1 - (1 - p)^n:

Fan-out n11050100200
Requests that hit at least one slow call1%9.6%39.5%63.4%86.6%

This is the independent-call calculation illustrated in Dean and Barroso's "The Tail at Scale" (2013): if each server independently exceeds 1 s on 1 percent of calls, a request that touches 100 servers is slower than 1 s 63 percent of the time. Turned around: to keep a user-facing p99 with a fan-out of 100, each backend has to be fast at roughly its p99.99, since 0.9999^100 is about 0.99. More exactly, the per-call success probability must be at least 0.99^(1/100), about 0.9998995. That is why fan-out amplification pushes large systems toward hedged requests, partial results and smaller fan-outs (see hedged requests).

Independence is an assumption, not a property of fan-out. Shared storage, hosts or network paths correlate delays, so the product formula may fail. Perfectly correlated 1 percent slow events give 1 percent slow user requests, not 63 percent. Hedging also adds load: cap it, use safe operations and cancel redundant work where possible.

Worked example: the numbers interviewers ask for

1. "How many threads for this service?" 5,000 requests per second, 40 ms mean thread occupancy, mostly waiting on other services, one thread held per request. Little's law: 5,000 × 0.04 = 200 in flight on average. An illustrative 60 percent occupancy target suggests ceil(200 / 0.6) = 334 threads; that target needs load testing. Then check the downstreams: if the database accepts only 100 concurrent queries, its limit decides, not the formula.

2. "Traffic grows 40 percent. What happens to latency?" Assume an unchanged FIFO M/M/1 bottleneck. Today it is 60 percent busy with S = 10 ms, so W = 10 / 0.4 = 25 ms. After growth, ρ = 0.6 × 1.4 = 0.84 and W = 10 / 0.16, about 62 ms: 2.5 times worse on average for 1.4 times the load, and p99 goes from about 115 ms to about 290 ms. The senior answer is to add capacity before peak, not after.

3. "A disk is modelled as M/M/1, with 5 ms mean service time. Requests arrive at 160 per second." ρ = 160 × 0.005 = 0.8, so the average request waits 4 × 5 = 20 ms and takes 25 ms in total. At 190 per second, ρ = 0.95: waiting 95 ms, total 100 ms. 18.75 percent more traffic, four times the mean total latency. If service were a fixed 5 ms, this would be M/D/1 and these waiting-time numbers would not apply.

4. "How many machines?" Peak 12,000 requests per second, 20 ms of CPU each: 12,000 × 0.02 = 240 cores busy. Targeting 60 percent CPU at peak, you need 240 / 0.6 = 400 cores, so 25 machines with 16 cores each, plus spares for failures and deploys. This assumes the work parallelizes across cores and CPU service demand does not rise with contention.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing units. Requests per second times milliseconds is off by a thousand.
  • Calling 1 / (1 - ρ) the waiting time. Those are multiples of mean service time for M/M/1: total 1 / (1 - ρ), wait ρ / (1 - ρ).
  • Sizing for the average. Size for peak traffic and the slow tail, then add headroom.
  • Expecting latency to grow linearly with load. Near the knee, a little more load means a lot more waiting.
  • Running the bottleneck at 95 percent to save money. In FIFO M/M/1 the mean total is 20 service times there, and p99 is worse; other models need their own calculation.
  • Applying Little's law across mismatched boundaries, such as counting queued requests in L but measuring W from the start of service.

In the interview

These numbers show up in design rounds and in concurrency deep dives: a Databricks interviewer asked about thread pool size versus dataset size and disk and network bottlenecks; an Uber interviewer asked what would happen with 10,000 concurrent requests and where the bottleneck would be; a Meta interviewer asked how many threads a file downloader should use and what a safe number is. These reports support practising capacity and bottleneck reasoning; they do not establish that the interviewer required a particular queueing formula.

At a stable 2,000 requests per second and 50 ms mean residence time, Little's law gives 100 requests in flight. That is an average, so I would choose and test a concurrency limit with headroom and check each downstream limit separately. For a FIFO M/M/1 bottleneck, mean waiting is about 2.3 service times at 70 percent utilization and 9 at 90 percent. With 50 independent calls, each above our threshold 1 percent of the time, about 40 percent of requests hit a slow call. I would consider partial results or carefully budgeted hedging.

Follow ups: "What if arrivals double?" (recompute ρ; if it exceeds 1 and no work is rejected or abandoned, backlog grows; shed or scale). "Is that the wait or the total?" (say which). "Why not run at 90 percent to save hardware?" (for M/M/1, nine service times of mean waiting; validate the actual workload).

Senior depth

Pooling can reduce waiting. Under independent Poisson traffic split equally between identical exponential servers, two separate M/M/1 queues at 90 percent utilization each give a mean wait of 9 service times. The same two servers fed from one shared queue (M/M/2) give about 4.3, because a free server never sits idle while the other has a line.

For M/M/c, Wq = P(wait) × S / (c × (1 - ρ)), where P(wait) is the Erlang C probability that all c servers are busy. For c = 2 and ρ = 0.9 it is about 0.853, giving Wq ≈ 4.26S. Pooling helps in this model; locality, shared-queue contention, work stealing and task affinity can change the engineering choice. In this model, more servers also move the knee to the right: a large pool can run hotter than a single server at the same waiting time.

Variability matters as much as load. Kingman's approximation for a single server says Wq ≈ S × (ρ / (1 - ρ)) × (ca² + cs²) / 2, where ca and cs are standard deviation divided by mean for independent interarrival and service times. Both equal 1 for M/M/1.

With Poisson arrivals, fixed service (cs = 0) halves the M/M/1 wait; with exponential service, ca² = 4 gives an approximate 2.5-fold multiplier. Kingman is a GI/G/1 approximation, most useful near heavy traffic, with finite variances; it does not capture every correlated burst. Reducing service variability while keeping mean service demand fixed can cut waiting without adding servers. Splitting a large job helps other requests interleave, but adds overhead and does not automatically shorten that job's completion time.

Open versus closed load. An open workload schedules arrivals independently of responses; a closed workload has a fixed client population that waits for replies, possibly with think time. Either can describe real traffic. A closed test of a service meant to handle open arrivals reduces offered load during stalls and can hide the queue and understate latency. That is the coordinated omission trap from performance and contention.

Key takeaways

  • Little's law, L = λ × W, holds for any stable system: use it to size concurrency, find capacity, relate mean queue length to mean waiting, and identify increased residence time.
  • For stationary FIFO M/M/1, waiting time is S × ρ / (1 - ρ) and time in system is S / (1 - ρ). Keep them apart.
  • Waiting steepens near saturation; there is no universal safe utilization threshold.
  • For M/M/1, p99 is about 4.6 times the average; p50 and p99 both scale with the mean in that model.
  • For independent calls, the slow-request probability is 1 - (1 - p)^n, 63 percent at n = 100 and p = 1 percent.

Quick check

Answer each question, then read why. Your score is saved for your module test.

A stable service admits 3,000 requests per second and each takes 80 ms on average from arrival to response. How many requests are in the service at any moment, on average?
Why. Little's law: L = λ × W = 3,000 × 0.08 = 240. The 37.5 option divides 3,000 by 80 instead of multiplying by seconds; 2,400 and 24,000 use incorrect unit conversion. L is an average occupancy, not a maximum.
A fixed pool of 32 threads runs requests that each hold a thread for 40 ms. Ignoring overhead and other bottlenecks, what is its ideal service-capacity ceiling?
Why. λ = L / W = 32 / 0.04 = 800 per second. The 1,280 option multiplies incompatible units; 32 is a thread count, not this service rate. Queueing cannot create capacity. At the 800 ceiling, variable traffic has no spare capacity to clear bursts; choose a lower admitted rate for finite steady-state latency.
A stationary FIFO M/M/1 server has mean service time 20 ms and utilization 90 percent. What is the average time a request spends in the system?
Why. Time in system is S / (1 - ρ) = 20 / 0.1 = 200 ms. The 180 ms option is the waiting time, S × ρ / (1 - ρ), which leaves out the 20 ms of service itself. The 20 ms option omits queueing altogether, and 22 ms does not follow either formula.
In the simulator, one customer has wait = 3, sampled service time s = 2, and next-arrival gap gap = 4, all in the same units. What are this customer's total time and the next customer's wait?
Why. This customer spends 3 + 2 = 5 units in the system. Lindley's recursion gives the next wait as max(0, 3 + 2 - 4) = 1. The second option omits service, the third omits the arrival gap, and the fourth ignores the unfinished unit of work at the next arrival.
Each request fans out to 50 shards in parallel and waits for all of them. Each shard is slow on 1 percent of calls, independently. Ignoring orchestration overhead and calling the user request slow if any shard is slow, roughly what fraction are slow?
Why. 1 - 0.99^50 is about 0.395. The request is as slow as its slowest call, so small per-call tails multiply. Adding 50 individual 1 percent probabilities gives only a 50 percent upper bound because slow events can overlap. One percent ignores fan-out; 99 percent is one shard's success probability, not the chance of any slow shard. At fan-out 100 the slow-request probability would be about 63 percent.
Across two stable windows, admitted throughput is 2,000 requests per second and mean in-flight count rises from 200 to 1,000. CPU remains mostly idle. What does Little's law establish?
Why. W = L / λ: 200 / 2,000 = 100 ms before, 1,000 / 2,000 = 500 ms after. Idle CPU suggests waiting, which could be a lock, queue or dependency. The law alone proves neither a cause nor a memory leak. Stable windows support the calculation; applying it to a transient jump without accounting for unfinished requests would be unjustified.
A FIFO work queue holds 3,000 items and workers continue draining it at 1,500 items per second, with no overtaking or cancellation. Roughly how long will an item that joins now wait before a worker picks it up?
Why. Waiting time is the queue length divided by the drain rate: 3,000 / 1,500 = 2 s. This is a FIFO backlog estimate with a constant drain rate. Little's law instead relates long-run average queue length and waiting time. The 0.5 s option reverses the ratio, 3,000 ms per item has the wrong units, and worker count alone does not specify throughput.

Checkpoint

Write your own answer first. Then compare with the model answer, or ask Bar Raiser to mark it like an interviewer would.

Numbers
A payments service peaks at 1,200 requests per second. Each request holds a database connection for 15 ms, and the pool has 30 connections. (a) How many connections are busy on average, and what is the pool's utilization? (b) Can you infer the mean wait from those numbers alone, or use the M/M/1 formula because there is one queue? (c) Marketing plans an event at 2,000 requests per second. What happens, and what are your options?
Model answer

(a) Busy connections = λ × S = 1,200 × 0.015 = 18 on average, so utilization is 18 / 30 = 60 percent.

(b) No. One queue feeding 30 servers is not M/M/1. Arrival variability, hold-time variability and scheduling determine the wait. If we additionally assume Poisson arrivals, independent exponential hold times and FIFO service, M/M/30 gives P(wait) ≈ 0.00653 and Wq = P(wait) × 15 / (30 × 0.4) ≈ 0.0082 ms. That idealized result is not a database latency prediction; correlated queries and shared database limits can dominate.

(c) At 2,000 per second the demand is 2,000 × 0.015 = 30 busy connections, exactly the pool size, so utilization is 100 percent. With ongoing arrival or service variability, this leaves no recovery margin; M/M/30 has no finite steady-state mean wait at 100 percent. Perfectly synchronized deterministic work is a special case, not a safe operating assumption.

Options: raise the pool, if the database can take more concurrent queries; shorten the hold time (faster queries, no remote calls while holding a connection, batching); keep a bounded wait for a connection and shed or degrade beyond it (fail fast with a retryable error); or move non-critical writes to an asynchronous queue. For an illustrative 70 percent occupancy target, ceil(30 / 0.7) = 43 connections. I would validate that target and confirm that extra concurrent queries do not increase hold times or overload the database.

Explain
A product manager asks: "The servers are only 85 percent busy at peak. Why is the p99 so bad, and why do you want more machines?" Explain without jargon.
Model answer

Requests do not arrive evenly. Even when the average load is 85 percent of what a server can do, there are moments when several arrive at once, and a queue forms. At low load the queue clears quickly, because there is plenty of spare time between bursts. At 85 percent there is very little spare time, so each burst takes a long time to drain, and the next burst often arrives before it has.

The waiting adds up: for a single server with variable work, an average request can wait several times longer than the work itself, and the unlucky ones, the p99, wait far longer still.

The relationship need not be a straight line. A simple single-server model with random arrivals and exponential service predicts about six times as much mean waiting at 85 percent as at 50 percent, and about 3.4 times more at 95 than at 85 percent. Spare capacity is what absorbs bursts, the way an empty lane keeps traffic moving. I would first find the saturated resource and check burstiness and tail latency. Extra machines help if they actually expand that bottleneck; they do not fix a shared database or hot lock. We would test a lower operating load against the latency target.

"Our bottleneck is ninety percent busy. Explain why requests wait so long, and tell me what your formula assumes."
What a strong answer covers
  • Bursts need spare service capacity to drain; average capacity alone is insufficient
  • State FIFO M/M/1 assumptions and utilization below one

How a senior would say it
Requests wait because bursts take time to drain, and at ninety percent utilization there is little spare capacity. For a concrete calculation, assume one FIFO server, Poisson arrivals, independent exponential service times, and no rejection or abandonment. The mean service time is ten milliseconds. Mean waiting is service time multiplied by rho over one minus rho, so ninety milliseconds. Adding service gives a hundred milliseconds in the system. Those are averages, not deadlines. The steady-state formula requires utilization below one. I would not apply it directly to thirty parallel database connections just because they share one queue. That needs a multi-server model and realistic hold times. There is also no universal safe threshold at seventy percent. I would measure the bottleneck, variability and latency distribution, then test whether added capacity or reduced service demand meets our objective.

Likely follow up: Could perfectly regular arrivals and service behave differently at full utilization?

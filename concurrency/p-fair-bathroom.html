The fair bathroom (and the team playground)

Ordering puzzleshard45 minThe unisex bathroom from The Little Book of Semaphores, in Rubrik's political bathroom and team playground formsRubrik

Rubrik has asked this unisex bathroom problem problem in two disguises: a "political bathroom" where Democrats and Republicans may not share and at most 3 fit (2024, and again in a May 2025 loop), and a playground used by one team at a time with at most 10 players (2025), with fairness and starvation among the reported follow ups. The exclusion is easy; the fairness is the interview.

The task

Warm-up, the political bathroom. One bathroom, two parties, Democrats (group 0) and Republicans (group 1). At most 3 people inside, and never people of both parties at once. People queue when they cannot go in, and nobody may wait forever.

The general version, the playground. Any number of groups (teams), identified by non-negative integers, and a capacity K. At most K people inside, all from the same group. The reported playground uses K = 10. This exercise generalises the group IDs and requires strict FIFO admission, so your class handles both forms with the same code.

The fairness rule: first come, first served. Each call to enter registers in one line while holding the state lock. This registration defines arrival order; overlapping calls may acquire the lock in either order. Admission follows registration order:

  • The person at the front of the line goes in when the room is empty, or when people of their own group are inside and there is room.
  • Nobody overtakes. A Democrat behind a waiting Republican waits, even if Democrats are inside and there is room, because otherwise a steady trickle of Democrats could keep the Republican out forever.
  • Because the next person becomes the front as soon as the current front goes in, a run of same-group people at the front of the line goes in together, up to the capacity.

A registered person can be preceded only by people already inside or registered ahead: bounded waiting measured in admissions, for any number of groups. This gives no starvation after registration if every admitted visitor eventually leaves and lock acquisition and thread scheduling eventually make progress. It does not promise a wall-clock waiting limit or fair acquisition of the initial lock. Admission order is the protected state transition, not the order callers happen to record after enter returns.

Rules. Build it from one lock and condition variables (no library semaphores needed). How long a person stays inside is up to the caller (in Rubrik's version, a function of their name). enter is not interruptible: an interrupted person keeps their place in line (restore the interrupt flag in Java). Capacity is positive, including 1. Every non-negative int group ID is valid, including the largest int. Each instance has independent state. Calls are balanced: after a successful enter, the same thread calls leave exactly once with the same group. Invalid inputs, unbalanced calls and cancellation are outside this task.

waiting() takes an exact snapshot under the state lock and returns without waiting for an admission. Count a caller from registration until its admission, excluding occupants and callers still acquiring the lock. This small observer makes queue order testable without assuming a thread has registered just because some time has passed. Protect its reads with the same lock as the updates.

What the tests check

  • Capacities 1, 3 and 10: fill every seat, queue one same-group replacement and one different group, release a single seat, then drain. Exactly one replacement enters; the other group waits for the last occupant to leave. IDs 17 and 33 must remain distinct.
  • Capacity 1: a registered line with consecutive same-group visitors must enter in individual FIFO order. The line also includes other groups and the largest non-negative int ID.
  • Capacity 3: the registered line A A B B A A A A C behind another group enters as batches of 2, 2 and 3. The fourth A waits for one seat, and C waits for every A to leave. Later callers cannot overtake an earlier different group even when seats are free.
  • waiting() is zero initially and after draining, matches each acknowledged registration, and decreases on admission. Two separate bathrooms with different capacities and groups can be occupied together.
  • Twenty-four rounds where an occupant leaves and immediately joins the waiting group. The existing front and its same-group successor must both enter before either leaves, exercising the notification needed on entry.
  • Three fixed-seed runs of 12 workers: capacity 1 with 4 teams for 480 visits, capacity 3 with one team for 2160 visits, and capacity 10 with 3 teams for 480 visits. Check exact completion counts, no mixed groups, no capacity overflow and no stranded waiters.
  • Java: pre-existing and repeated interrupts preserve both queue position and the interrupt flag, including an uncontended entry. C++ runs under ThreadSanitizer.
  • Timed worker completion and a whole-suite watchdog bound failures, including a candidate that blocks inside waiting(). Workers are joined on success; a failed run prints its result and exits.

Queue registration is observed directly, and held visits determine the handoff checks. A short observation window checks continued blocking; it does not establish arrival order. Stress inputs are repeatable, but the operating system still chooses interleavings. These tests exercise safety and progress; no finite run proves correctness under every schedule.

Taught in: The sleeping barber and the unisex bathroom

Open them one at a time. Each one gives away a little more.

Start with the obvious monitor: count who is inside and which group they are. A person may enter when the room is empty, or when their group is inside and there is room. It is correct about exclusion. Now replay it with Democrats arriving every few milliseconds and one Republican waiting: when does the Republican get in?
Fix it with a line. Hand out tickets on arrival (nextTicket), as a ticket lock does, and keep nowServing, the ticket at the front. Only the front may try to enter, and it enters if the room is empty or holds its own group with space left. This is the fair read-write lock with "readers" generalised to groups and a capacity.
Advance nowServing when the front enters, and broadcast, so a same-group person right behind can join at once. leave must also broadcast: the front may be waiting for space (same group) or for an empty room (another group), and you do not know which.

What the interviewer asks after your first version works. Try to answer each one out loud before you open it.

Rubrik asked candidates to "admit the most eligible person" and to discuss allocation policies before coding. What are the options?
First come, first served (this problem): no starvation, simple to prove, but it can leave space unused, because a same-group person behind a different-group front must wait. Batching with a limit: while another group waits, let at most B more of the current group in, then let the room drain and switch; at most B further current-group admissions before a switch. Choose among waiting groups fairly as well. Round robin over groups that have waiters. Shortest visit first, when visit times are known (the f(name) in Rubrik's version): can reduce average wait, but long visitors can starve unless you add aging. Name the one you build and why.
First come, first served wastes capacity. How do you win some back without allowing starvation?
Allow bounded overtaking. Let a same-group person pass the waiting front only while the front has been overtaken fewer than B times (keep a counter on the front), or switch to batch mode as above. Both keep a bound on waiting (B plus the people ahead), and B becomes a knob between fairness and throughput. Strict FIFO is the special case B = 0.
Why does "alternate between the two parties" not generalise to the playground?
With two groups, alternating finite batches prevents group starvation; order people fairly within each group too. With N groups you need an order among the waiting groups, or one group can be skipped forever while others alternate. A per-person line, as here, handles any number of groups for free; a group level alternative is a queue of groups that have waiters, served round robin.
How would you build it with semaphores?
The Little Book of Semaphores' unisex bathroom uses a light switch per group (the first in locks the room, the last out unlocks it), a multiplex semaphore with 3 permits for the capacity, and, for the no-starvation version, a turnstile that everyone passes through, so a waiting member of the other group blocks newcomers. The fairness then depends on the turnstile waking threads in order, which a plain semaphore does not promise; tickets make the order explicit.
Every change wakes every waiter. Does that matter, and how would you fix it?
With many waiters, a broadcast wakes all of them, although only the eligible same-group prefix can proceed: a thundering herd that costs context switches, not correctness. Give each waiter its own condition in a FIFO queue and wake only the front (plus the same-group run behind it). It is the same optimisation as in a production fair lock.

Try for at least twenty minutes first. Reading a solution feels like learning; writing one is learning.

The idea

This is group mutual exclusion with arrival order. State under one lock: nextTicket, nowServing, the number of people inside and the group inside. The invariants: everyone inside belongs to one group, at most capacity are inside, and people enter in ticket order. The third invariant is the fairness: a person waits only for those registered ahead, so later registrations cannot overtake them. Completion still depends on finite visits and eventual progress of scheduling and lock acquisition.

Why each line is there

  • Take the ticket under the lock. It defines registration order and must be unique among outstanding calls. The lock acquisition order need not match wall-clock invocation order.
  • Only the front may try. ticket == nowServing is what stops a same-group newcomer from slipping past a waiting member of another group.
  • The front enters if the room is empty, or holds its group with space. That is the exclusion rule plus the capacity.
  • Advance nowServing on entry, then broadcast. The next person in line becomes the front and checks immediately; if it is the same group and there is space, it joins, so batches form naturally.
  • leave broadcasts. The front may be waiting for space or for an empty room; waking everyone and letting each re-check is simple and correct.
  • waiting() uses that same lock. nextTicket - nowServing counts registered callers not yet admitted. A snapshot does not reserve a place or grant permission to enter.
  • Uninterruptible waits. A person who left the line would strand their ticket and block everyone behind it.

Cost and trade-offs

O(1) explicit bathroom state and bookkeeping per call, including waiting(), plus broadcasts that can wake O(W) waiters for W registered callers. The condition wait queue and blocked calls still require O(W) storage. Lock acquisition and waiting can take unbounded wall-clock time. Strict arrival order can leave seats empty (the front's group differs from the group inside), which is the price of bounded waiting; batching with a bypass limit buys some of it back. Say this trade-off out loud: it is what the "explain allocation policies" part of the question is fishing for.

How to say it in the interview

The exclusion part is a monitor that tracks how many are inside and which group. The hard part is starvation: if same-group people may join whenever there is room, the other group can wait forever. So I define arrival as queue registration under the lock and serve people with a ticket counter: only the person at the front may try, and they go in if the room is empty or holds their group with space. On entry I advance the front and broadcast, so a run of the same group goes in together. Everyone waits only for the people who came before them, for any number of groups and any capacity. If throughput matters more, I would allow a bounded number of same-group overtakes.

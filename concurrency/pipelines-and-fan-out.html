Pipelines, fan-out, fan-in and batching

Stages joined by bounded queues, fan-out and fan-in, batching, ordered results with bounded parallelism, and back-pressure from the last stage to the first.

Asked atDatabricks
Before this:Producer and consumer with a bounded bufferBlocking queuesFutures and promisesSingle writer, actors and message passing

Most real concurrent programs are not one pool doing one thing. They are an assembly line: read, parse, transform, write. Each stage runs at its own speed, and the queues between the stages decide whether the line flows, floods or stalls.

In plain words

A wedding caterer's kitchen makes rotis for five hundred guests. One person rolls the dough, three people cook on three tawas (they share one pile of rolled dough, whoever is free takes the next), and one person stacks the cooked rotis into boxes. Between stations sit trays that hold only ten.

When the packer falls behind, the tray in front of her fills, so the cooks stop; then their tray fills, so the roller stops. Nobody is told to slow down, yet the whole line slows to the packer's pace and the kitchen never drowns in rotis. Small trays between stations are what keep a fast station from burying a slow one. When the order is complete, the roller sends one "last one" card for each cook, and the packer waits for all three cards before closing.

Stages joined by bounded queues

A pipeline is a chain of stages. Each stage is one thread or a small pool that takes an item from its input queue, does its part, and puts the result on its output queue. Three numbers describe it:

  • Throughput cannot exceed the slowest stage's capacity for a one-input, one-output pipeline. With sufficient input, independent resources and small handoff overhead, it approaches that capacity. A stage that handles 2,000 items a second caps the whole pipeline at 2,000, however fast the others are.
  • Latency for one item is the sum of the stage times plus the time it spends waiting in queues.
  • Queued items are bounded by the sum of the queue capacities. Total memory also includes active workers, batches, reorder buffers, retained inputs and outputs, and payload sizes. Bound those too; a capacity in items is not a capacity in bytes.

Two parts of the design are easy to forget. Stopping: after all producers finish, send an end of stream marker (a poison pill) for each worker that exits on taking one. Our single collector counts one marker per worker. If the next stage has several consumers too, use a coordinator to count upstream completions and send exactly one marker per downstream consumer; blindly forwarding markers across different worker counts is unsafe. Failing: if one stage dies silently, the stage before it blocks forever on a full queue and the stage after it waits forever on an empty one, so a failure must travel both ways.

Fan-out and fan-in

Fan-out means several workers take from one queue: whoever is free takes the next item, so load spreads by itself and other workers can process independent items while one item is slow. Fan-in means several workers put into one queue that a single downstream thread drains. Together they parallelize the slow stage of a pipeline. This is work sharing, not broadcasting: each input goes to one worker. Fan-in can also feed several downstream consumers. The price is order: enqueue order after processing need not match input order, so attach sequence numbers if order matters.

This first program demonstrates normal completion only: no cancellation, allocation failure or processing failure is injected. Start consumers before producing or joining workers, so bounded output queues can drain. The failure protocol below explains what must change for shutdown under faults.

Count the end markers. The source sends four, one per worker, because each worker stops after taking one. Each worker forwards its marker, so the collector stops after seeing four. Send only one marker and three workers wait forever on an empty queue; stop the collector after the first marker and it can miss later results or leave workers blocked on a full output queue. FIFO insertion ensures that each worker's results precede its own marker, so seeing all four proves all their results were drained. Queue transfer publishes preceding writes, but it does not make subsequent mutation of a shared payload safe: transfer ownership or use immutable messages.

Back-pressure, from the last stage to the first

Blocking bounded queues propagate back-pressure between these stages. When the last stage slows down, its input queue fills, the stage before it blocks on put, then that stage's own input fills, and so on until the source blocks. The pipeline settles at the speed of its slowest stage, and queued item counts stay within capacity; worker state, batches and other retained data need separate bounds.

At the very front, where requests come from outside, blocking is only one choice. You can block (push the back-pressure to your callers), reject and shed load (fail fast with an error the caller can retry later), drop or sample (fine for metrics and logs that tolerate loss), or spill to disk (absorb a burst at the cost of complexity). Back-pressure and load shedding covers how services choose.

Batching

Some steps have a large fixed cost per call and a small cost per item: an fsync, a network round trip, a system call, taking a contended lock. Batching pays the fixed cost once for many items. The usual policy is up to B items or up to T milliseconds, whichever comes first: the size limit bounds the batch, and the time limit caps intentional batch assembly delay after the first item is taken. Earlier queueing, scheduling delays and the write itself still add latency.

Use an illustrative cost model, not a disk benchmark: assume a flush costs 1 ms and ignore transfer cost. One flush per event then caps a serial writer at 1,000 events a second; batches of 100 raise that ideal ceiling to 100,000. Real latency depends on the storage stack and load, and has no universal 10 ms upper bound. A durable acknowledgement comes only after the batch write and required durability operation succeed, not when the batch fills; see file I/O and durability.

These fragments assume one batching consumer, ordinary nonthrowing value moves in C++, and a timeout whose computed deadline is representable. In Java, interruption before the first item propagates; interruption after taking ownership of items returns the partial batch with the interrupt flag restored. The caller must then flush or explicitly discard that batch according to its cancellation contract. They do not yet handle end markers: the owner must flush a partial batch on close and must not pass a control marker to the data-writing function. Java's deadline arithmetic uses nanoTime differences; C++ uses steady_clock, so wall-clock adjustments do not restart the window.

Under sustained heavy load batches tend to fill before the deadline; under light load the time limit flushes small batches so batch assembly does not wait forever. This amortizes fixed cost until the writer saturates; beyond saturation, more traffic increases queueing and rejection, not throughput.

Ordered results with bounded parallelism

A common requirement: apply a slow function to a list, with at most K calls running at once, and return the results in input order. Fan-out and fan-in lose the order, so you need one of three techniques:

  • A window of futures in input order. Start up to K calls; before starting another, wait for the oldest and emit it. Order is automatic and at most K tasks and un-emitted results occupy the window. A returned list still retains all N emitted results; stream them to a sink for bounded total result storage. A slow call at the head holds up emitting later ones that already finished: head of line blocking.
  • Sequence numbers and a reorder buffer. Tag each item with its position; workers finish in any order; the collector keeps early arrivals in a map and emits whenever the next expected position arrives.
  • Write into slots. Each worker writes its result into slot i of a preallocated array, and the caller reads the array after all workers finish. Simple when you only need the whole result at the end. Allocate before launching, give each slot one writer, and join before reading. Do not resize a shared C++ vector during writes, and avoid std::vector<bool> because distinct bits can share storage.

The samples require a callable safe to invoke concurrently, copyable C++ inputs and callable, and a non-void value result. Peak observed concurrency may be below K because scheduling is nondeterministic; the guarantee is the upper bound. The C++ samples use C++17 facilities and also compile as C++20.

The reorder buffer version has one trap worth knowing before an interviewer springs it. If you limit parallelism with a semaphore and release the permit when a call finishes, a slow item at the head lets every other item finish and pile up in the buffer: K calls in flight, but the buffer can grow without bound. Release the permit when a result is emitted, not when it is computed, and at most K admitted items remain un-emitted. Acquire that permit before admission, and keep it through processing and ordering.

Do not deadlock the reorder buffer. A collector that stops draining a full completion queue while waiting for item 1 can prevent item 1 from entering that queue. Bound admission instead, and keep draining every admitted result into storage sized for the whole window. Release permits on cancellation too, or explicitly tear down the entire admission mechanism. A slow first item still causes head of line blocking; bounded memory does not remove that latency.

C++

std::async with std::launch::async runs each call as if in a new thread, so thread creation costs remain even though at most K calls run at once. Releasing the last reference to an unfinished async shared state can wait for completion. These futures therefore wait during unwinding if get() throws; the remaining work is not cancelled. A reusable pool avoids per-item thread creation, and cooperative cancellation must be designed separately.

Java

ExecutionException wraps whatever the function threw; unwrap it with getCause() if callers should see the original. shutdownNow() attempts interruption; it does not wait and cannot force an uncooperative task to stop. The subsequent Java 21 close() waits for termination. Thus this map owns its workers until they finish, but an uncooperative function can prevent it from returning. Also, retrieving futures in input order can delay discovery of a later failure behind an earlier slow call.

Failure and cancellation need their own path

Graceful close drains; abort wakes and rejects. Normal completion sends markers after the last accepted item and flushes the final partial batch before reporting success. For abort, record the first failure and stop accepting work, then wake both blocked puts and blocked takes. Merely checking an error flag before a blocking call leaves a race where failure occurs immediately after the check.

A custom abortable channel keeps aborted under the queue mutex. A put waits for space or abort, a take waits for data or abort, and both reject once aborted. Its abort operation sets the flag and broadcasts on both conditions; call it on every channel without holding another channel's lock. Java BlockingQueue has no built-in close: a supervisor can instead interrupt all stage threads, provided every stage treats interruption as terminal and performs no blocking marker insertion afterwards. Ordinary C++ condition-variable waits do not respond to a stop token automatically.

After requesting abort, join all stage threads, release resources and report the first failure. External I/O and transforms must be bounded or cooperate with cancellation; no queue protocol can force an arbitrary stuck function to return. Do not run all blocking stages on a fixed pool that can fill entirely with upstream tasks waiting for downstream tasks that have not started. Use independent stage capacity or nonblocking composition. Across a process crash, retries require idempotency or durable tracking; an in-memory pipeline does not provide exactly-once side effects.

Sizing the stages

Little's law ties the numbers together: the items in a stage on average equal its throughput times the time each item spends there. If parsing takes 4 ms per item and you need 1,000 items a second, an average of about 4 items must be actively parsing, so you need at least 4 parser threads (and 4 free cores, since parsing is CPU work). These are ideal lower bounds, with no CPU headroom. Size queues from a latency budget and a burst allowance: a 1,000-item backlog before a 1,000-item-per-second server takes roughly one second to drain with no new input. Also budget bytes and downstream limits such as connection capacity. A long queue does not add throughput; it only adds latency and hides the moment you fall behind.

Worked example: a log indexing pipeline

The task: read log lines from files, parse each line into fields, and write the parsed records to an index, in order. The machine has 8 cores. Assumed costs for this illustrative capacity calculation: reading takes 0.01 ms per line, parsing 0.4 ms per line of CPU, and each write to the index costs about 5 ms whether it carries 1 record or 100.

StageDesignCapacity
Read1 thread1 / 0.01 ms = 100,000 lines a second
Parse7 threads (fan-out), leaving roughly one core for other work7 times 1 / 0.4 ms = 17,500 lines a second
Write1 owner thread, batches of up to 100 or 20 ms100 per 5 ms = 20,000 lines a second

Ignoring handoff overhead, contention and incomplete batches, the capacity ceiling is about 17,500 lines a second: parsing is the bottleneck, and the writer keeps up only because it batches. Without batching the writer would manage 1 line per 5 ms, 200 a second, and everything else would sit blocked behind it. The index must be in order, but parsing fans out and finishes out of order, so the writer's input needs sequence numbers and a small reorder buffer, with the permit rule from above so the buffer stays bounded. If the disk slows to 50 ms a write, the writer drops to 2,000 lines a second, its queue fills, parsers and then the reader block, and the whole pipeline runs at 2,000 a second with bounded retained work if payloads, active work, queues and reorder storage all have bounds.

Common mistakes

  • Unbounded queues between stages. A fast stage in front of a slow one fills memory until the process dies. Every queue gets a capacity.
  • One end marker for N workers. One worker stops; the others wait forever. Send one per worker, and have the next stage count them.
  • A stage that dies quietly. If a worker throws and exits without passing its end marker, the collector waits forever, and if the workers stop taking, the source blocks forever on a full queue. Record the first error and choose a shutdown protocol: drain with all consumers still alive, or abort by waking every blocked producer and consumer. A marker put in finally can itself block forever if the downstream stage has exited.
  • Batching without a time limit. At low traffic a batch never fills, so the first item waits indefinitely.
  • Releasing the ordering permit on completion. The reorder buffer grows without bound behind a slow head. Release on emit.
  • Too many tiny stages. Every hand off is a queue operation and often a thread wakeup. Merge cheap adjacent stages; split only where a stage needs different parallelism or is the bottleneck.

In the interview

The collected Databricks reports repeatedly describe concurrent log or event writers. Across those reports, topics include batching to reduce flushes, ordered writes, durability acknowledgements, close and back-pressure. Another Databricks round, a cached file reader over slow storage, was answered with fixed-size chunks fetched in parallel by a thread pool, each chunk fetched once. An older Google phone screen (2020) asked for a multithreaded C++ queue that notified consumers in batches of N records or after T seconds, which is the batching policy above.

I will build it as stages connected by bounded queues: the source, a fan-out stage of parser threads sized to the cores, and a single writer that owns the output and batches up to 100 records or 20 milliseconds. Bounded queues give back-pressure: if the writer slows down, the parsers block and then the source blocks, so queued work stays bounded and throughput drops to the writer's pace. I also bound payload bytes, active work and batch storage. Shutdown is an end marker per parser, counted by the writer, which flushes the last batch before it exits. Because parsing finishes out of order, the writer reorders by sequence number, and I release the parallelism permit when a record is emitted, so the reorder buffer stays bounded.

Follow ups to expect: "What happens when the queue is full?" (block, reject, or drop, and why). "How big should each queue be?" "What if a parser throws?" "Which stage is the bottleneck, and how would you find it?" (measure each stage's throughput and queue depth; a persistently full queue identifies a downstream constraint, but also check downstream queue depths, service time and worker utilisation). "How do you make the output ordered?"

Senior depth

Back-pressure across asynchronous boundaries needs a protocol. Blocking put works between threads in one process. When stages are asynchronous (callbacks, non-blocking I/O), nothing blocks, so demand must be signalled explicitly. Java's java.util.concurrent.Flow interfaces define that protocol: a subscriber requests a positive number of additional items with Flow.Subscription.request(n), and a publisher must not deliver more data items than the outstanding demand permits. Terminal completion or error signals do not require demand. Reactive libraries build on the same idea.

Pipelines and divide and conquer are different shapes. A pipeline suits a stream of independent items flowing through fixed steps; fork and join with work stealing suits one big task that splits recursively. They can be combined: a pipeline stage may use fork/join internally. Choose based on dependencies and blocking behaviour, not the pattern name.

Across machines, the queue becomes a log. When stages are separate services connected by a durable log, consumer lag can grow independently of producer progress until retention, quota or storage limits intervene. Back-pressure then becomes a monitoring and scaling decision (add consumers, slow the producer, or shed load) instead of a blocking put.

Key takeaways

  • The slowest stage caps pipeline throughput. Bound queues, active work, batches, reorder buffers and payload bytes to bound retained memory.
  • Blocking bounded queues propagate back-pressure: a slow last stage slows the first stage instead of filling memory.
  • Fan-out spreads a slow stage over several workers and gives up order; one end marker per worker shuts it down cleanly.
  • Batch expensive steps by size or time, whichever comes first.
  • For ordered results with K in flight, keep a window of futures, or a reorder buffer whose permit is released on emit.

Answer each question, then read why. Your score is saved for your module test.

With sustained input above 8,000 items per second, bounded blocking queues and negligible overhead, a three stage pipeline can do 50,000, 8,000 and 20,000 items per second in its three stages. What is the pipeline's throughput, and which queue fills up?
Why. The slowest stage sets the pace. Work arrives at the second stage faster than it can handle, so the queue in front of it is the full one, and with bounded queues the first stage then blocks. The full queue points at the bottleneck.
A stage has 4 workers sharing one input queue. Each worker exits after consuming one marker and never puts it back. When the input ends, the source puts one end marker on the queue. What happens?
Why. A queue hands each item to exactly one taker. Send one marker per worker, and have the downstream stage count one marker per upstream worker before it stops.
A producer makes 5,000 items a second, a consumer handles 3,000, and the queue between them is unbounded. Assume constant rates and enough memory for ten minutes. How much backlog accumulates?
Why. The excess is 5,000 minus 3,000 = 2,000 per second; 600 seconds adds 1,200,000 items. There is no automatic dropping or throttling in this model, and the slower consumer cannot catch up while rates stay constant. A bounded queue would make the producer block at the capacity, settling the item flow at 3,000 a second with a bounded queue backlog.
An ordered parallel map uses a semaphore of K permits. Each worker releases its permit as soon as its call finishes, and a collector emits results in input order from a reorder buffer. A fixed pool of K workers pulls from a source that keeps supplying items, with no separate admission bound. Item 1 is very slow. Which structure can grow without a bound tied to K?
Why. Permits released on completion let new calls start while the head is stuck, so finished results pile up. Release the permit when a result is emitted, and the buffer holds at most K results.
A batching writer flushes when it has 100 events or when 5 ms have passed since the first event of the batch. Traffic is one event every second. Ignoring queueing, scheduling and flush duration, how much intentional batch assembly delay does each event incur?
Why. The size limit never triggers at this rate, so the time limit decides. Without a time limit the event would wait for 99 more, which is why a continuously running stream with a latency target needs a time trigger as well as a size trigger. A finite job could instead flush on end of input.
In a Java fan-out stage, one worker throws an exception on a bad item, and its thread ends without forwarding anything. Assume the remaining stages continue running. The collector waits for one end marker per worker. What happens at the end of the input?
Why. The dead worker never takes its end marker, so it never forwards one. Recording an error is not enough: use the drain or abort protocol below to unblock every stage. An uncaught exception escaping a C++ thread entry instead calls std::terminate, so this silent thread-exit scenario is specifically Java.
In the first program, main joins every worker and the collector before printing. The collector alone updates sum and count. Which pair can it print after normal completion?
Why. Each input is consumed once, each square is added once, and each worker's marker follows its results. The collector waits for all four markers, and joining it publishes its writes to main. The sum is 200 times 201 times 401 divided by 6 = 2,686,700. Order does not change this sum; markers increment a separate counter.

Checkpoint

Write your own answer first. Then compare with the model answer, or ask Bar Raiser to mark it like an interviewer would.

Numbers
A thumbnail service downloads an image (about 200 ms of waiting on the network), resizes it (about 20 ms of CPU), and uploads the result (about 100 ms of waiting). The machine has 8 cores, and the target is 400 images a second. How many threads does each stage need, and what limits the target? Use Little's law and treat the numbers as rough.
Model answer

Little's law: items in a stage = throughput times time per item. Download: 400 a second times 0.2 s means about 80 downloads in flight, so about 80 threads (or async I/O), and almost no CPU. Resize: 400 times 0.02 s means about 8 resizes in flight, and since each is pure CPU, that needs all 8 cores busy all the time. Upload: 400 times 0.1 s means about 40 in flight.

The limit is the resize stage: 8 cores times 50 resizes per core per second is exactly 400 a second, with no headroom for anything else on the machine, including the download and upload threads' own CPU. A provisional target of 300 to 350 a second leaves some headroom, but load tests must choose the real target. More I/O threads help only while bandwidth, connection limits and remote quotas permit. Queue capacities come from burst size, payload bytes and latency budgets, not a fixed multiplier of worker count.

Write code
Implement graceful draining after a processing failure: a Java transform throws RuntimeException, or a C++ transform throws. Assume finite, terminating transforms, live source and collector, no external interruption, and successful queue operations. Stop starting new transforms once failure is observed, drain pending input, join all stages, and report the first error. How would you extend this to cancellation or failure of the collector?
Model answer

Under the stated assumptions, a processing failure is recorded and workers keep draining without starting further work after they observe it. Work already started may finish. The collector must continue consuming output, even if it discards results after a failure, so marker insertion can complete. The source checks the shared error before each put and, once it is set, stops producing and sends the end markers early. The collector counts end markers as usual, and when it is done the caller checks the shared error and rethrows it (in C++ with std::rethrow_exception). This is graceful draining, not immediate cancellation. The Java interruption paths record the error and restore the flag, but require the supervisor abort protocol below; an end marker cannot repair a dead downstream consumer.

Explain
Explain back-pressure in a pipeline to an interviewer: how it works with bounded queues, and what the front of the system should do when it cannot keep up.
Model answer

Every queue between stages has a capacity, and a stage that finds its output queue full blocks on put. So when the slowest stage falls behind, its input queue fills, the stage in front of it blocks, then that stage's input fills, and the blocking travels back stage by stage to the source. Nothing has to be coordinated: the whole pipeline settles at the speed of its slowest stage, and queued item counts stay within the sum of capacities. Active tasks, batches, payload bytes and retained outputs still need independent bounds.

At the front, where requests arrive from outside, I have to choose what blocking means. If callers can wait, I let them block or time out, which pushes the back-pressure to them. If they cannot, I shed load: reject quickly with an error they can retry later, which keeps latency low for the requests I do accept. For telemetry that tolerates loss, dropping or sampling is fine. Spilling to disk absorbs bursts but only delays the problem if the overload lasts. Whatever I choose, I watch queue depth per stage, because the full queue tells me where the bottleneck is.

I would use bounded queues, a limited pool of parsers, and one writer that owns the output. I would number records at admission so the writer can restore input order. The admission limit would cover everything accepted but not yet emitted, including completed records waiting behind a slow earlier record. Otherwise a fixed worker count can hide an unbounded reorder buffer. The memory budget includes queue contents, active work, batches, reorder storage, and payload bytes. When the writer slows, its queue fills, parsers block, and eventually the source stops accepting more work. At the service boundary I would choose a deadline or rejection policy instead of letting callers wait indefinitely. The writer batches by size or elapsed time. On graceful close, I stop admission, let accepted work drain, count every parser's completion, and flush the final partial batch. If durability is required, acknowledgements follow the successful durability operation. If the writer fails, I record the first error and abort every channel so blocked producers and consumers wake. I do not rely on inserting a final marker into a full queue. I then join all stages and report the error. That requires transforms and I/O to terminate or cooperate with cancellation; a stop flag cannot force arbitrary code to return.

Out loudabout 80 s
"Only four calls can run at once, but memory keeps growing behind a slow first result. What is wrong, and how would you fix it?"
What a strong answer covers
  • A running-call limit does not bound completed but un-emitted results
  • Releasing permits on completion keeps admitting later work
  • Hold admission permits until ordered emission or explicit cancellation
  • Drain all admitted completions so the missing head can reach the collector
  • State the head of line and retained-output trade-offs
How a senior would say it
The limit is on running calls, but the growing memory is in completed results waiting for their turn. Suppose the first call is stuck. The other three finish, release their permits, and let three more calls start. That can repeat across the whole input, even though four calls are never exceeded. I would acquire a permit before admitting each item and keep it until its result is emitted in order. That bounds admitted but un-emitted work, including the reorder buffer. Cancellation must release those permits or tear down admission explicitly. The collector must still drain completions for every admitted item. If it stops reading a full completion queue while waiting for the missing first result, that first worker may be unable to enqueue its result. I would reserve reorder storage for the entire admission window. A simpler alternative is a window of futures retrieved in input order. Both approaches preserve the memory bound but still suffer head of line blocking. Also, if I collect every emitted result in a returned list, that list still takes space proportional to the input. Streaming emission is needed for bounded total result storage.

Bulkheads, timeouts, retries and circuit breakers

Isolate slow dependencies with bulkheads, propagated deadlines, cooperative cancellation, bounded retries, circuit breakers and carefully budgeted hedging.

Asked atDatabricksUberRubrik
Before this:Backpressure, bounded queues and load sheddingSemaphores: permits as a resource limit

A service can fail when one dependency gets slow while everything that calls it waits, retries and piles up. Bulkheads, timeouts, retries and circuit breakers are the four tools that keep a slow dependency from becoming a dead service, and they work best as a deliberate policy.

In plain words

A ship's hull is divided into watertight compartments called bulkheads, so one hole floods one compartment instead of sinking the ship. A sensible caller hangs up after a while instead of holding forever. If the line is busy, they try again later, not every second, and not all at the same moment as everyone else. And after a few failed calls to a number that is clearly down, they stop dialing for a while and check back once in a bit.

Those are a bulkhead, a timeout, a retry with backoff and jitter, and a circuit breaker. Each protects you from a different way that waiting spreads.

How one slow dependency takes down a service

A service has one pool of 200 request threads. The payments provider, normally 50 ms, slows to 5 seconds. Payment requests now hold a thread 100 times longer, and 40 completed payment calls per second at a mean duration of 5 seconds would occupy all 200 threads on average (40 × 5 = 200). Now login, search and health checks wait for threads too. The load balancer sees failing health checks and pulls the instance, which pushes its traffic onto the others, which fill up the same way. A failure in one dependency has become an outage of everything, and nothing crashed.

Three things went wrong: one shared resource (the pool) for every kind of work, no limit on how long a call may wait, and nothing stopping the service from sending more calls to a dependency that was clearly struggling. The four tools address different parts of that chain; none removes the need to bound admission.

Bulkheads: one compartment per risk

A bulkhead gives each dependency or class of work its own bounded resource, so it can exhaust only its own share. The two common forms: a separate thread pool per workload (CPU-heavy reports in one pool, user requests in another), and a semaphore per dependency, limiting how many calls to it may be in flight. Useful boundaries: per downstream dependency (the most important one), per endpoint class (cheap versus expensive), per tenant (so one customer's burst cannot starve the others) and background versus user-facing work.

Here, 8 checkouts hit a payments service that takes 200 ms, behind a bulkhead of 3 permits with a 50 ms wait. Two searches run at the same time.

In this illustrative trace, three checkouts run and five are rejected after roughly 50 ms; no retry or queue is created. Scheduling can change that split and the timestamps. The 12 request workers leave room for searches in this small demo; a semaphore alone does not reserve workers for other endpoints.

Estimate mean occupancy with Little's law using the dependency's admitted rate and mean latency, then choose headroom within its measured capacity, and keep the wait short: a long tryAcquire timeout quietly turns the bulkhead back into a queue. The cost to say out loud: partitioned resources are used less efficiently than one shared pool. You are trading peak efficiency for a smaller blast radius.

Bound the number of callers waiting for admission too. Thousands of request threads waiting 50 ms for three permits can still exhaust a shared pool; immediate rejection or a separate bounded executor gives stronger isolation. The C++ semaphore and std::jthread need C++20; in C++17 use a mutex with a condition variable and a permit count, plus explicitly joined std::threads.

Timeouts, and deadlines that travel

Every remote call needs a timeout; an unbounded blocking call can occupy a worker indefinitely. Three rules make timeouts work:

  • Set them from data. Choose an acceptable false-timeout rate and the matching observed latency percentile, then add network and setup margin within the caller's budget. A p99-based timeout can expire roughly 1 percent of otherwise healthy calls; the appropriate percentile depends on the service. A 30 second default on a call that normally takes 20 ms can leave each stalled blocking call holding a worker for 30 seconds.
  • Per attempt and total are different numbers. Three attempts of 2 seconds each plus backoff can take 7 seconds; if the caller's budget is 1 second, the retries are meaningless.
  • Propagate a deadline, not a fresh budget. The edge gives the request 800 ms; queueing, connection setup, work and retries all spend it. Each hop uses the remaining time and reserves time to return the response. gRPC Java stubs expose withDeadlineAfter(long, TimeUnit) and inherit the current RPC context's deadline; preserve that context when offloading work. In C++, grpc::ClientContext::set_deadline sets a deadline, and grpc::ClientContext::FromServerContext creates an outgoing context with propagation options.

Use monotonic time locally. Java uses System.nanoTime() differences; C++ uses std::chrono::steady_clock. Their origins are local, so do not serialize those values to another process. At the gRPC C++ API boundary, set_deadline accepts an absolute deadline such as a std::chrono::system_clock::time_point; derive it from the remaining local budget. gRPC transmits a remaining timeout to avoid requiring synchronized wall clocks. Without such a protocol, define how transit time and clock skew are handled rather than resetting the original timeout at every hop.

Set each attempt's timeout to the smaller of its configured cap and the remaining request budget minus response overhead. Include DNS, connection establishment and TLS in the total budget; a socket read timeout alone need not cover them. Neither a percentile nor a deadline predicts an individual completion time, and scheduling can delay timeout handling.

A timeout is not a cancellation

When a caller's timeout fires, the caller stops waiting. The work does not stop unless something tells it to. A worker thread keeps computing, a database keeps executing the query, a downstream service keeps processing the request. Under overload, that is how a fleet fills up with work nobody will ever read, the zombie work from the previous lesson.

Cancellation is cooperative in both languages: the caller can only ask, and the work has to check. In Java that means Future.cancel(true) plus code that responds to interruption (blocking calls that throw InterruptedException, or loops that check Thread.currentThread().isInterrupted()). In C++ it means a std::stop_token checked between steps. Across the network, use the transport cancellation mechanism and server-side deadline checks. A disconnected client does not guarantee that application work stops, especially with proxies or multiplexed connections; a database needs its own query cancellation or statement timeout. Cancellation cannot roll back a committed side effect. See interruption and cooperative cancellation.

Two sharp edges catch people:

  • CompletableFuture.cancel(true) does not interrupt anything. Its documentation says the flag has no effect, and orTimeout only completes the future exceptionally while the underlying task keeps running.
  • Releasing the last reference to an unfinished std::launch::async shared state can block until its task finishes, which is why the C++ program above still waits for job A at the end.

Keep a bulkhead permit until the protected operation actually stops using the resource, not merely until its future reports cancellation. Releasing it early admits replacements while the original operations still run. A std::jthread destructor requests stop and joins; it is also not a way to abandon uncooperative work. In C++17, pass a shared atomic stop flag and join explicitly.

Retries: medicine with a maximum dose

A retry can turn a transient failure (a dropped connection, one overloaded replica) into a success. It also multiplies load at exactly the moment a dependency is struggling. The discipline:

  • Retry only what is safe to repeat. Reads, and writes that are idempotent or carry an idempotency key (see idempotency). Retrying a card charge without a key is how customers get charged twice.
  • Retry only retryable errors. Timeouts, connection resets and 503s may be retryable under the operation contract; they are not automatic permission to retry. A timeout leaves a write outcome unknown. An unchanged 400 Bad Request normally needs correction, not another attempt.
  • Exponential backoff with full jitter. Wait a random time between 0 and min(cap, base × 2^k), where k = 0 for the first retry. Backoff spreads the retries out in time; jitter spreads them across clients, so a thousand clients that failed together do not all come back at the same instant.
  • Stay inside the deadline. Check the remaining budget before every attempt and after every backoff; give the operation that same deadline. Skip a retry when the remaining time is too small for useful work.
  • Respect the server's signal. If a 503 or 429 carries Retry-After, do not retry earlier; if its delay does not fit the deadline, give up. The header can contain delay seconds or an HTTP date.

The helper checks its deadline before each attempt, passes it to the operation and rejects a late result. It cannot forcibly stop an operation that ignores the deadline; real I/O must set its own timeout from the remaining budget. A late write may already have committed. This is a per-request helper: a shared retry-ratio budget and Retry-After handling belong in the policy around it. C++ backoff here uses sleep_for, which is not stop-token aware; a cancellable caller needs a stop-aware wait as well.

Java and C++ use different random distributions and can choose different sleeps. With full jitter, only the ceiling grows exponentially, not each sampled delay. The fixed seed makes the demo repeatable; real clients must use independently seeded generators so they do not repeat the same retry schedule. The AWS Architecture Blog's well known comparison of backoff strategies found full jitter cuts total work sharply compared with plain exponential backoff, because synchronized clients stop colliding.

Retry amplification, and why budgets beat counts

Retries multiply across layers. If a user request passes through three services and each makes up to 3 attempts (the first try plus 2 retries) when the layer below fails, then when the bottom dependency is down, one user request can cause up to 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 calls to it. Four layers make 81. The dependency is buried by retries at exactly the moment it is trying to recover.

  • Choose one retry owner for an operation, with enough context to know whether repetition is safe. Audit SDK, proxy and application retries together; nested retry loops multiply even if each looks small.
  • Use a retry budget as well as an attempt limit. For example, permit at most 10 retries per 100 original requests over a defined window: extra attempts are then bounded at 10 percent over that window, not necessarily every second. Define the denominator; limiting retries to 10 percent of all attempts instead permits about 11.1 retries per 100 originals. The Google SRE book describes per-client budgets alongside per-request limits. gRPC retry throttling is related, but uses failure-depleted, success-replenished tokens rather than enforcing this exact ratio.
  • Do not retry on overload signals without the requested delay: a 503 with Retry-After is the server asking for less load.

Circuit breakers: stop calling what is clearly down

A circuit breaker sits in front of a dependency and watches results. Closed: calls flow, failures are counted. When failures cross a threshold, it trips to open: calls fail immediately, without touching the dependency, for a cool-down period. Then it goes half-open: a few trial calls go through; if they succeed it closes, if not it opens again. The breaker avoids another downstream wait and gives the dependency room to recover. It does not cap concurrent calls while closed; a bulkhead still does that.

Follow the trace: three failures trip the breaker at 200 ms; calls at 300 and 400 ms fail fast; at 1,200 ms the cool-down has passed, the breaker goes half-open and lets one trial through, which fails, so it opens again; at 2,300 ms the next trial succeeds and it closes. The fake clock makes every transition testable without sleeping.

Admission needs an identity. Each accepted call gets a ticket tied to the current state generation. Report through that same breaker; duplicate reports and reports from an earlier generation are ignored. The lock protects both admission and completion, but never the remote call itself. Consecutive failures here means completion order, not start order. Use a monotonic, nonthrowing clock whose differences fit the chosen integer type; the fake clock is single-threaded, while concurrent tests need an atomic clock. This small state machine assumes every admitted operation eventually reports; production integration must also handle abandoned probes and stuck operations.

Production breakers often use a failure rate over a sliding window with a minimum sample size. Resilience4j names these settings failureRateThreshold, slidingWindowSize and minimumNumberOfCalls; it tracks slow-call rate separately, using slowCallRateThreshold and slowCallDurationThreshold. Classify dependency failures separately from business errors, local bulkhead rejection and caller cancellation.

Every admitted call must report exactly one terminal outcome or explicitly relinquish its probe reservation. Arrange this on every completion path; a finally block does not execute while an operation is stuck. Half-open probes therefore need deadlines and a bounded probe lifetime. Envoy also has per-host outlier ejection; its upstream circuit-breaking limits cover resources such as active requests, pending requests, connections and retries.

Hedged requests, with a warning label

A hedged request attacks tail latency: send to one replica, then optionally send a second copy if the first has not completed by a measured threshold, such as its normal p95 latency. Accept the first successful, usable response and request cancellation of the loser. At an unchanged latency distribution, a p95 trigger duplicates roughly 5 percent of calls; during an incident that fraction can rise sharply, so enforce a separate cap.

The warning label, which you should state in the same breath:

  • Only for idempotent operations: reads, or writes with an idempotency key. Hedging a non-idempotent write duplicates it.
  • It adds load exactly when things are slow. Cap hedges with a budget (a few percent of requests), and turn them off when the service is overloaded, or they become a retry storm.
  • It helps most when slowness is weakly correlated across replicas (one replica in a GC pause). If every replica is slow because of a shared database, a hedge can add load without improving the tail.
  • Cancel the loser, and account for any work it already did. Cancellation does not undo completed work or guarantee that the remote handler stops.

Worked example: a checkout with a 1 second budget

The edge gives checkout requests a deadline of 1,000 ms. Checkout calls inventory (p99 80 ms, idempotent reservation with a key) and then payments (p99 300 ms, charges carry an idempotency key).

  • Bulkheads. Assume payments has a measured mean latency of 100 ms and can tolerate 30 concurrent calls. At 60 checkouts per second, mean occupancy is 60 × 0.1 = 6; a cap of 30 is a capacity-tested choice, not a result obtained by substituting p99 into Little's law. Inventory has its own cap; both use immediate rejection so there are no permit waiters.
  • Timeouts. Inventory: 150 ms per attempt. Payments: 500 ms per attempt. Clip both to the remaining deadline and reserve 100 ms for local work, fallback and the response.
  • Retries. Inventory: at most two attempts, with at most 50 ms jitter between them. Payments: one foreground attempt, retaining its idempotency key for later reconciliation. Allocated budget: 150 + 50 + 150 + 500 + 100 = 950 ms. The absolute deadline still handles scheduling delays; attempting two full 500 ms payment calls would not fit.
  • Circuit breaker on payments, tripping at a 50 percent failure rate over the last 20 calls, only after 20 samples. Return "payment pending" only after durably recording the order and a recoverable payment job. That job reuses the idempotency key, reconciles uncertain outcomes and revalidates any expired inventory hold; otherwise return an honest error.
  • No hedging on payments (a write, and slow for everyone when slow), but hedging is reasonable on the idempotent inventory read path if its tail is caused by single slow replicas.

Common mistakes

  • One shared pool for every dependency: the slowest dependency decides everyone's latency.
  • No timeout, or a library default of 30 seconds or infinity.
  • Timeouts without cancellation: callers leave, the work continues, the fleet fills with zombies.
  • Retries at every layer, with no jitter and no budget: 27 times the load during an outage.
  • Retrying non-idempotent operations without an idempotency key.
  • A circuit breaker on a tiny sample (two failures out of three calls trips it) or with no fallback when open.
  • Hedging non-idempotent writes, or hedging without a budget during overload.

In the interview

These tools are asked about directly and inside designs. A Databricks candidate was asked to build a circuit breaker for a leader-follower database, with code that compiled and ran; an Uber L4 candidate built one that opens on request count, with OPEN, CLOSED and HALF_OPEN states, and was expected to answer the concurrency questions properly; a Rubrik thread pool question added a per-task timeout as a follow up. In design rounds the prompt is usually "a downstream gets slow; what happens?"

Its latency rise immediately inflates our in-flight count, since concurrency is rate times duration, so the semaphore in front of that dependency fills first, by design. Further calls fail fast in milliseconds and take the degraded path, cached data or a partial response, while the rest of the service keeps its threads. Every call carries a deadline derived from the request's budget and gets a cancellation request when it passes. I measure whether operations actually stop; cancellation alone does not prove that. Retries happen at one layer only, with exponential backoff, full jitter and an explicit budget, for example 10 retries per 100 originals, so we cannot multiply the load on a struggling service. If failures persist, the circuit breaker opens; only bounded recovery probes are admitted after its cool-down.

Follow ups: "Why jitter?" (synchronized clients retry together and re-create the spike). "What do you do while the breaker is open?" (a fallback: cache, default, queue for later, or a clear error). "Is your breaker thread-safe?" (one lock around state transitions, one trial in half-open, and exactly one outcome tied to its admission generation). "When would you hedge?" (idempotent reads with independent per-replica tails, under a budget).

Senior depth

The tools interact. Retries inside a bulkhead consume permits for longer; a breaker in front of a retry loop sees one failure per call, behind it one per attempt; hedges count against the same budget as retries. Decide the order deliberately: deadline check, breaker admission, bulkhead admission, then an attempt with a timeout and a retry decision. If the bulkhead rejects after reserving a half-open trial, release that reservation without recording a downstream failure. Release the resource permit when the attempt truly ends, before backoff, and acquire anew for each retry or hedge.

Thread pool bulkheads versus semaphore bulkheads. A separate bounded pool contains blocked workers if the caller can stop waiting without joining them, at the cost of a handoff and idle reserved capacity. Hung work still consumes that pool, and shutdown may wait indefinitely. A semaphore avoids the handoff, but synchronous work uses the caller's thread; bound admission waits and reserve enough capacity for unrelated work. Resilience4j offers both as ThreadPoolBulkhead and Bulkhead.

Key takeaways

  • Bulkheads give each dependency or workload its own bounded resource, so one slow dependency exhausts only its own share.
  • Every remote call needs a timeout set from data, inside a deadline that propagates; a timeout does not cancel the work unless you make it.
  • Retry only safe operations and retryable errors, with exponential backoff and full jitter, at one layer, under a budget: three layers of three attempts is 27 times the load.
  • Circuit breakers fail fast while a dependency is down and probe with trial calls before closing.
  • Hedge only idempotent requests, under a budget, when replica latency is sufficiently independent to justify the extra work.

Quick check

Answer each question, then read why. Your score is saved for your module test.

A request passes through four services. Each service makes up to 2 attempts (one retry) when the call below it fails. The bottom database fails immediately, all four layers exhaust their attempts, and the deadline does not stop them early. At most how many calls can one user request cause at the database?
Why. Each layer multiplies the attempts below it: 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16. With 3 attempts per layer across three layers it would be 27. That is why you retry at one layer and use budgets.
In the ticket-based breaker above, use threshold 1 and a 100 ms cool-down. Admit old call A and call B while closed; B fails and opens the breaker. At 100 ms admit probe P, then A reports success. Print the state; P then fails and you print again. What does the trace show?
Why. A's ticket belongs to the old closed generation, so its late success cannot close the half-open breaker. Only P's current ticket can resolve that probe, and its failure opens the breaker. CLOSED first would mean a stale success was accepted; OPEN first would ignore the admitted probe transition. The lock prevents concurrent field access, while the generation check prevents this logical race.
Which sleep before retry k, where k = 0 is the first retry, is "exponential backoff with full jitter"?
Why. Full jitter draws the whole sleep at random under an exponentially growing, capped ceiling. A fixed exponential schedule keeps clients that failed together in lockstep; a tiny jitter barely separates them; a constant small range does not back off at all.

Why. These candidates are idempotent and have sufficiently independent replica tails to justify a bounded hedge. A non-idempotent charge would be duplicated, and hedging during overload adds load exactly when the system can least afford it.
A request has 40 ms remaining on its propagated deadline. You reserve 10 ms to form and return the response. The dependency's configured per-attempt timeout is 200 ms. Ignoring setup overhead already accounted for, what is the largest timeout you may give this attempt?
Why. The attempt receives min(200, 40 - 10) = 30 ms. The response reserve is part of the caller's budget. Resetting a timeout on each retry would extend that budget. This calculation bounds useful waiting; it does not guarantee the query finishes in 30 ms or that cancellation stops it immediately.

Why. The CompletableFuture documentation says the mayInterruptIfRunning flag has no effect, because interrupts are not used to control its processing. If the work must stop, it needs its own cancellation signal, or you run it as a FutureTask on an executor and cancel that.

Why. Releasing this last reference to the unfinished std::launch::async state waits for the task. A timed out wait does not change that, so the "timeout" still blocks at scope exit. A std::jthread also joins on destruction, so a stop token helps only if the worker cooperates. A longer-lived executor can own unfinished work while the request returns, but still needs bounded capacity and a shutdown policy.

Checkpoint

Write your own answer first. Then compare with the model answer, or ask Bar Raiser to mark it like an interviewer would.

Design
Your service calls a recommendations service (nice to have, p99 120 ms) and an inventory service (required, p99 60 ms) for every product page, with an overall page budget of 400 ms. Recommendations has started having 5 second stalls a few times an hour. Design the protection, with numbers.
Model answer

Bulkheads. Separate concurrency limits for each dependency. Assume 500 page views per second and measured mean latencies of 40 ms for recommendations and 20 ms for inventory: mean occupancy is 20 and 10. The supplied p99 values cannot determine those means. If load tests confirm downstream capacity, start with caps of 40 and 30 respectively and immediate rejection. Separate bounded executors or nonblocking I/O keep stalled recommendations from exhausting inventory's request workers.

Deadlines and timeouts. The page carries a 400 ms deadline. Inventory gets a 100 ms timeout per attempt; recommendations 150 ms, and in any case no more than what remains of the budget minus what rendering needs. Both calls run in parallel. On timeout, request cancellation through the transport and use a server-side timeout too. Keep accounting for the slot until resource use really ends; a cancelled future alone cannot prove that a connection is safe to reuse.

Retries. Inventory reads are idempotent: one retry with up to 50 ms full jitter if 150 ms plus the rendering reserve remain, under a budget of 10 retries per 100 originals. Recommendations: no retries at all; it is optional, so a failure just means the page renders without it.

Circuit breaker on recommendations, tripping when the failure rate or the rate of calls slower than 150 ms reaches 50 percent over the last 50 calls, after at least 50 samples, with a 30 second cool-down and a few trial calls in half-open. While open, the page renders with a cached or generic "popular items" block, without calling recommendations. We target a 150 ms recommendations wait, plus scheduling and response overhead. The page deadline remains the final bound on useful work; monitor cancellation lag and fallback latency instead of promising perfect isolation.

Numbers
Three layers each make up to 3 attempts. There are 1,000 new user requests per second, normally one bottom-service call each. Assume immediate failures and enough budget to exhaust all attempts during a sustained outage. (a) What request rate can it see during the outage? (b) With at most 10 retries per 100 original requests over the measurement window at the top layer only, and no retries below, what is the average offered rate over that window instead?
Model answer

(a) Each user request can turn into 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 calls at the bottom, so the upper bound on the sustained offered rate is 27,000 requests per second, 27 times normal load, aimed at a service that is already failing. Even if it starts to recover, that load can knock it straight back down.

(b) With retries only at the top, limited to 10 percent of requests, the bottom sees at most an average of 1,000 original requests plus 100 retries per second over that window, 1,100 in total: 1.1 times normal. The lower layers fail fast instead of retrying, the extra load is bounded no matter how long the outage lasts, and the service has room to recover. Jitter reduces synchronization; it guarantees neither an even distribution nor a strict per-second cap.

Explain
An interviewer asks: "We already have timeouts on every call. Why would we still see overload get worse during an incident?" Explain.
Model answer

Because a timeout only ends the caller's wait. Unless the timeout also cancels the work, everything below keeps running: the worker thread finishes its computation, the database finishes the query, the downstream service finishes the request, all for a caller that has already left. During an incident that zombie work can be most of what the system is doing, so capacity goes to dead requests while live ones queue and time out too.

Timeouts also interact with retries. Each timed-out request is typically retried, so the system receives the original work, which is still running, plus a new copy. With retries at several layers this multiplies. And if the timeouts are longer than the callers' own, the work outlives every caller in the chain.

The fixes: propagate deadlines so each hop knows how much time is left and refuses work it cannot finish; cancel on timeout (interrupts, stop tokens, transport cancellation, database statement timeouts); check the deadline before expensive steps and when taking work off a queue; retry at one layer with jitter and a budget; and reject excess work before bounded queues grow past the useful latency budget.

"Payments gets slow and checkout starts timing out. Walk me through how you stop that from taking down login and search too."
What a strong answer covers
  • Bound payment concurrency and admission waits so unrelated work retains capacity
  • Propagate one end-to-end deadline and include queues, attempts and response overhead
  • Request cancellation but keep permits until the protected resource is really released
  • Retry only safe transient failures at one layer with jitter, attempt limits and a shared budget
  • Use a breaker with bounded probes and an honest, durable fallback
  • Measure cancellation lag, rejections and dependency latency to validate isolation
How a senior would say it
I would bound the damage at payments first. Give it a separate concurrency cap and reject quickly when that cap is full. If blocked calls could exhaust the request workers, use a separate bounded pool or nonblocking I/O, and reserve capacity for unrelated work. A semaphore with thousands of waiting callers is not enough. Next, carry one checkout deadline through admission, connection setup, every attempt and the response. For example, an eight hundred millisecond request cannot spend eight hundred milliseconds again at each dependency. On timeout I request cancellation, but I keep the operation counted until it actually releases its resource. Cancellation is cooperative, and an uncertain charge may already have committed. I retry only a transient, safely repeatable operation, using the same idempotency key for a payment. One layer owns those retries, with jitter, a small attempt count and a shared extra-attempt budget. Persistent failures open a circuit breaker; a bounded probe checks recovery. A payment-pending response is valid only after I durably record work that will be reconciled later. Otherwise I return an honest error. Finally I check rejection rates, active calls, cancellation lag and login latency. Those measurements tell me whether the isolation works under failure.

Likely follow up: What happens if the payment client ignores interruption and its socket timeout?

Out loudabout 75 s
"Your circuit breaker uses one lock, but an old successful call closes it while the recovery probe is still running. How would you fix that?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Identify a logical state-machine race that locking alone does not prevent
  • Walk through admission while closed, opening, probing and a late old success
  • Tie every admitted call to a state generation and ignore stale or duplicate completion
  • Allow only bounded probes and keep remote work outside the state lock
  • Bound probe lifetime and classify outcomes without counting local rejection as a dependency failure
How a senior would say it
I would attach an admission ticket to every call, including the breaker's current generation. One lock makes field updates atomic, but it cannot tell whether a result belongs to the current recovery attempt. Suppose call A starts while the breaker is closed. Other calls fail and open it. After the cool-down, probe P enters half-open. If A now succeeds, an untagged completion can incorrectly close the breaker and release a burst before P finishes. Each state transition therefore creates a new generation. Completion checks that its ticket still matches, and that the ticket has not already been reported. A's stale success is ignored; only P can decide whether this half-open generation closes or reopens. I use the lock for admission and completion, never across the network call. I also bound probe concurrency and lifetime so a lost completion cannot block recovery forever. Finally, I distinguish dependency failures from local bulkhead rejection or caller cancellation. Those local events must not be counted as evidence that the dependency is unhealthy.

Likely follow up: How would you test stale completions and simultaneous probe admission without relying on sleeps?

Out loudabout 60 s
"We already cap each request at three attempts. Why do you also want a retry budget and jitter?"
What a strong answer covers
  • An attempt limit still permits large aggregate load when all originals fail
  • Nested layers multiply attempts, for example three layers of three give 27
  • Define a shared budget using retries divided by original requests over a window
  • Jitter spreads retry timing but does not guarantee a per-second bound
  • Preserve idempotency, one deadline and server-requested delays
How a senior would say it
Three attempts caps one request, but can still triple traffic when every original fails. With three retrying layers, that becomes up to twenty-seven calls to the bottom service for one user request. I choose one retry owner and also cap aggregate extra attempts. For example, ten retries per hundred original requests over a defined window limits extra load to ten percent in that window. I specify the denominator because ten percent of all attempts is a different policy. Jitter solves another problem: clients that fail together should not all retry together. It randomises a capped backoff delay, but does not guarantee evenly spaced requests or a hard per-second limit. Every retry must still be safe to repeat, fit the remaining deadline and respect any server-requested delay. If those checks fail, I stop retrying and return a fallback or error.

Likely follow up: Where would you enforce that budget if the application, SDK and proxy can all retry?

Spinlocks, backoff and when spinning beats blocking

Build a spinlock, reduce contention with read polling and backoff, and decide when spinning, parking or a normal mutex fits.

Asked atNvidia
Before this:Atomic variables and compare-and-swapBusy waiting, and when spinning is right

A spinlock is the simplest lock there is: one atomic flag and a loop. It is also the easiest lock to misuse, which is why systems interviews ask about it, from "implement one" to "why did our latency get worse when we switched to one?"

In plain words

A train has one washroom. When it is occupied you can stand at the door and keep trying the handle, or you can go back to your seat and ask the attendant to call you when it is free. Standing at the door is the fastest way in if the person inside will be out in five seconds. If they will be ten minutes, you have wasted ten minutes and blocked the corridor.

And if the person inside has fallen asleep, everyone standing at the door waits for nothing, while the people in their seats at least get on with their lives. Spinning bets that the wait will be short and that the holder is awake and working. When either bet is wrong, it loses badly.

The simplest lock: test-and-set

A spinlock is a flag that says "taken". To lock, a thread atomically sets the flag and looks at what it was before, which is the test-and-set instruction. If it was clear, the thread now owns the lock. If it was already set, someone else owns it, so try again. To unlock, clear the flag.

It is correct: the atomic exchange lets exactly one thread see "was clear". The memory orders matter in C++: the acquire on lock and the release on unlock create the happens-before edge that makes the previous owner's writes visible to the next one. Java's atomics give you that automatically.

That assumes every access to the protected data uses this same lock, the lock is safely published, and only its owner unlocks it. These teaching locks do not check ownership, support interruption, or allow recursive acquisition. In C++17 initialize the flag with std::atomic_flag flag_ = ATOMIC_FLAG_INIT;; the default-clear initialization above is C++20.

Its problem is manners. Every waiting thread executes an atomic write on every pass. Each write needs ownership of the flag's cache line, so several waiters can make the line ping-pong between cores. The owner also competes for that line to unlock, so the waiters can delay the release they need.

This deliberately aggressive sketch has no fairness or wait-time bound. A stress run can become very slow, especially with instrumentation; correctness of the mutual-exclusion protocol is not a performance guarantee.

Polite spinning: test-and-test-and-set

The fix is to wait by reading atomically. Waiters can cache shared copies of the flag's line, so repeated reads usually avoid ownership traffic while the line remains unchanged and cached. Only when the flag looks clear does a waiter try the atomic write. That is test-and-test-and-set; a plain non-atomic C++ read would be a data race.

When the owner unlocks, its write invalidates every waiter's copy; they all re-read, see "clear", and race to test-and-set. One wins, the rest see "set" again and go back to quiet reading. Two more refinements make it a good spinlock:

  • A spin-wait hint inside the read loop. Thread.onSpinWait() in Java; _mm_pause() on x86 or the yield instruction on ARM in C++. A spin-wait hint may reduce power or contention for execution resources, depending on the CPU. Java permits a no-op implementation. It neither supplies memory ordering nor promises a scheduler yield; ARM's instruction is not std::this_thread::yield().
  • Exponential backoff and jitter with jitter after losing the race. If many waiters all lunge at the flag the moment it clears, most of them lose. Each loser waits a random time drawn from a range that doubles after every loss, up to a cap, so the herd spreads out instead of colliding in lockstep.

The C++ relaxed read only decides when to try; the successful acquire exchange grants ownership and imports the previous owner's writes. The class meets the BasicLockable requirements for ordinary std::lock_guard and std::unique_lock use. The stronger Lockable requirements also need try_lock(), added in the checkpoint. The hint helper uses compiler and architecture extensions; the empty fallback preserves correctness.

C++ guarantees lock-free test-and-set and clear for std::atomic_flag, but not for every std::atomic<bool> implementation; check is_lock_free() when that property matters. Even with lock-free atomic instructions, the spinlock algorithm is blocking in the progress-guarantee sense: a stalled owner can stop every waiter. Jitter reduces synchronized retries, not starvation, and the illustrative cap of 1,024 hints is neither a time bound nor a universal tuning value.

Why spinlocks go wrong in user space

Everything above assumes the holder is running and will release in a moment. In user space, nothing guarantees that. The operating system can take the CPU away from the holder at any instant: its time slice ends, it takes a page fault, or there are simply more runnable threads than cores.

Short protected work can become a millisecond-scale wait, an illustrative order of magnitude rather than a timing guarantee. On Linux without PREEMPT_RT, spin_lock disables local preemption, while the _irqsave form also disables local interrupts until restoration. On PREEMPT_RT, ordinary spinlock_t has different, sleeping-lock semantics; raw_spinlock_t remains a strict spinning lock. Even a raw spinlock is no promise against hardware stalls or host descheduling of a virtual CPU.

Interrupt exclusion is about correctness too. If an interrupt handler on the same CPU tries to acquire a lock held by the code it interrupted, it cannot let that owner resume. Use the appropriate kernel primitive and interrupt discipline; never copy these user-space examples into an interrupt handler.

Priority inversion becomes a hang

priority inversion is a high priority thread waiting for a low priority lock holder. A blocking mutex lets the waiter sleep, but medium priority runnable work can still starve the holder. A mutex configured for priority inheritance can help by temporarily boosting the holder; neither std::mutex nor ReentrantLock promises that feature. With a spinlock the high priority waiter itself can prevent the holder from running:

A normal mutex removes the active spinner from that cycle, although it does not by itself guarantee bounded waiting. Backoff, CPU hints and thread affinity do not implement priority inheritance.

When spinning wins

A contended blocking path may cost microseconds in scheduling and wakeup overhead, an order of magnitude that depends on the OS and workload; see parking and futex. A free mutex usually takes a fast path without sleeping. Spinning is worth evaluating when:

  • The expected remaining wait is tiny compared with parking and waking. Tens to hundreds of nanoseconds is an illustrative scale, not a cutoff. Include queued contenders, not just one owner's critical-section time.
  • The holder is likely running on another core, with low contention and spare CPU capacity. Pinning restricts where a thread runs; it does not stop preemption, interrupts, faults or VM pauses.
  • The environment forbids sleeping, such as certain kernel interrupt contexts. This is a separate constraint requiring the platform's own locking rules, not evidence that an arbitrary spinlock is fast or suitable for real-time deadlines.

One simple cost model charges one unit per unit of spinning and a fixed cost B to block and wake, independently of the holder. Spinning for B and then blocking costs at most twice the hindsight choice min(wait, B) in that model. Real scheduling delays, queue lengths and interference violate its assumptions, so this is intuition for a bounded spin phase, not a production latency guarantee.

HotSpot 21's inflated monitors include adaptive spinning before parking; glibc also offers the nonportable PTHREAD_MUTEX_ADAPTIVE_NP type. These are implementation choices, not guarantees of Java monitors or std::mutex. Start with a normal mutex in application code: it usually has a cheap uncontended path and can park under contention. Profile before replacing it.

Worked example: spin or block?

The following times are hypothetical inputs, not measured thresholds.

A statistics struct updated for 20 ns by 4 workers pinned to 4 of 8 cores. Spinning is worth measuring if contention and preemption are rare. Pinning does not guarantee the holder runs. Consider per-thread stats merged on read, or atomics if the invariant permits them.

A cache lookup that, on a miss, reads from disk for 2 ms while holding the lock. Block. A spinner would burn 2 ms of CPU per miss. (And move the disk read outside the lock.)

A web service with 200 request threads on 16 cores. Start with a mutex. If many of those threads are runnable and contending, spinners can occupy the CPUs the owner needs. Merely having 200 threads is not proof of oversubscription if most are sleeping.

A low-latency path with one pinned thread per core and a 40 ns lock hold. Benchmark both choices, including tail latency during preemption. A single-writer design may remove the shared lock altogether.

Common mistakes

  • A spinlock in ordinary application code. More runnable threads than cores increases the risk of delaying the owner. Use a mutex; it can park waiters, and many implementations spin briefly first.
  • Spinning with atomic writes. A test-and-set loop without the read-only "test" phase floods the cache line. Spin on a read.
  • No pause hint and no backoff. Tight loops can waste power and execution resources; simultaneous retries amplify contention. Hints and jitter mitigate these effects but guarantee neither fairness nor faster execution.
  • Doing slow or blocking work under a spinlock. I/O, memory allocation (which may take its own lock), logging or anything that can page fault turns nanoseconds into milliseconds for every waiter.
  • A relaxed store in unlock (C++). locked_.store(false, std::memory_order_relaxed) lets writes from the critical section become visible after the next owner enters: a data race. Unlock must be a release.
  • Using volatile bool as the flag in C++. It is neither atomic nor ordered. Use std::atomic<bool> or std::atomic_flag.
  • Locking it twice in one thread. A spinlock is not reentrant; the second lock() spins forever on a lock the thread itself holds.
  • Spinning in a Java virtual thread. This busy loop does not park or unmount it, so it occupies a carrier thread. Enough spinning virtual threads can delay an owner waiting for a carrier. Prefer the parking behaviour of library locks, and use lockInterruptibly() when acquisition must respond to interruption.

In the interview

Spinlocks come up in systems-flavoured rounds: Nvidia system software interviews have asked about spinlocks next to mutexes, semaphores and interrupt handling, and "mutex versus semaphore versus spinlock" is a standard theory question. In coding rounds the shape is "implement a spinlock" followed by "what is wrong with it?" and "when would you use it?"

A spinlock waits by polling rather than parking. I would poll with atomic reads and a CPU hint, then attempt an acquire exchange when the flag looks free, and release-store on unlock. Randomized backoff reduces collisions but does not guarantee fairness. Spinning is worth measuring when the remaining wait is very short and the owner is likely running. Preemption or oversubscription can make it burn CPU and hurt tail latency, so I would start with a mutex for this application.

Follow ups: "Is it lock-free?" (no, progress depends on the owner), "How do you make it fair?" (ticket order, with scheduling caveats below), "What about one CPU?" (a spinner cannot run concurrently with the owner), and "Make it reentrant" (track owner identity and a hold count, releasing only at zero).

That last extension needs care: contenders inspect the owner before acquiring the lock, so a plain shared owner field is not safe. Use a race-free identity protocol with correct publication and thread-lifetime handling; only the confirmed owner may touch its hold count. A production reentrant lock also checks illegal unlocks. For the full design, see reentrancy.

Senior depth

Fair spinning: the ticket lock. A plain spinlock can starve a waiter. A ticket lock takes a number with fetch-and-add and waits until "now serving" reaches it. Admission follows the atomic ticket-allocation order, which need not match the order callers entered lock().

Ticket order prevents overtaking but gives no wall-clock wait bound. If the next ticket holder is descheduled, others wait behind it even though no thread is in the critical section. A ticket must not simply be abandoned on timeout or cancellation, or serving stalls at that number. Integer wraparound is safe only while the number of outstanding tickets stays below the counter's modulus, so no two outstanding tickets have the same value.

Ticket locks still make every waiter observe the shared serving counter. Separating the ticket and serving counters can reduce false sharing. For heavier contention, MCS-style queue locks let waiters spin on separate nodes and hand off to a successor, reducing shared-line traffic, but introduce node-lifetime complexity and still suffer when a needed owner or successor is descheduled.

Spin, then sleep. An adaptive lock can combine both phases: try briefly, then park through a coordinated waiting protocol so the thread stops actively polling.

Java can delegate the waiting protocol to ReentrantLock. C++20 adds std::atomic::wait and notify_one; their implementation may spin and use OS waits internally, but the standard does not mandate a futex or direct waiting on a particular atomic's address. The fixed 100 attempts below are illustrative, not tuned.

In C++, wait(true) rechecks the value before blocking, closing the race where unlock happens before the waiter starts waiting. Unlock changes the value before notifying. A wakeup does not grant ownership: the thread must win an acquire exchange, and may wait again if another contender wins. A brief false-to-true change can be missed; the new owner must eventually release and notify. Keep the lock alive until all waiters have finished.

The Java wrapper inherits ReentrantLock's reentrancy, while the C++ example is non-reentrant; both are unfair. A plain library lock is usually enough without the explicit spin phase. In C++17 use a mutex, or a condition variable with a mutex-protected predicate for a custom waiting protocol, rather than trying to emulate atomic waiting with a check followed by sleep.

Key takeaways

  • A spinlock is a flag taken with test-and-set; acquire on lock and release on unlock make it a correct lock.
  • Spin on a read (test-and-test-and-set) with a pause hint and randomized exponential backoff, so waiters stop flooding the cache line.
  • Spinning bets that the wait is short and the holder is running; preemption breaks both bets, and priority scheduling can turn it into a hang.
  • Evaluate spinning using remaining wait, runnable contention and tail latency; affinity alone is no running guarantee.
  • Start with a mutex in application code: its free path is usually cheap, it can park waiters, and some implementations spin briefly first.

Quick check

Answer each question, then read why. Your score is saved for your module test.

In the runnable example, four workers each increment the plain counter 50,000 times under the same correctly implemented spinlock. The main thread joins all workers before printing. Assuming the workers finish, what count must it print?
Why. Mutual exclusion prevents lost updates, release/acquire orders accesses between owners, and joins order the final read. Thus 4 times 50,000 is 200,000 in both languages. Contention changes speed, not the count, and a correctly synchronized plain variable is valid. The assumption that workers finish matters because this unfair spinlock does not guarantee each waiter's progress.
What does test-and-test-and-set improve over a plain test-and-set spinlock?
Why. Read polling lets waiters reuse cached shared copies while the line stays unchanged; test-and-set repeatedly requests write ownership. Cache eviction and false sharing can still cause traffic. TTAS does not order waiters or prevent priority inversion, and the final acquisition still needs an atomic RMW.
Which situations favour spinning over blocking? Select all that apply.Select all that apply
Why. A very short wait with a running owner can avoid parking overhead; a non-sleeping kernel context imposes a separate constraint. Neither is a universal timing guarantee. Network latency is far too long for this style of polling, and 200 actively contending threads can oversubscribe 16 cores and delay their owner. Pinning alone would not guarantee progress.
On a single core with strict priority scheduling, low priority thread L holds a spinlock. High priority thread H becomes runnable, preempts L and spins on the same lock. What happens?
Why. Under the stated strict scheduler, runnable H always wins over L, so L cannot reach unlock. The user-space flag does not register an owner or request priority inheritance. Hints do not make H block or yield to L. A mutex configured for priority inheritance could address this dependency, but it is not an automatic property of all mutexes.
Why does a good spinlock add randomness (jitter) to its exponential backoff?
Why. Identical delays can synchronize retry bursts; random delays spread them out and may reduce collisions. This is not necessarily livelock, because one contender can still succeed on every handoff. Jitter does not impose admission order, prevent preemption or preserve the loop against compiler removal; atomic accesses supply the required semantics.
C++ onlyA C++ spinlock guards a shared non-atomic counter, using acquire exchange to lock but locked_.store(false, std::memory_order_relaxed); to unlock. There is no other synchronization between the writers. What is wrong?
Why. The relaxed store is atomic, but does not publish the previous critical section to the next owner. Conflicting non-atomic accesses lack a happens-before edge, giving a data race and undefined behaviour even if a particular CPU appears to run the code correctly. A torn flag and inevitable deadlock are not the issue.
Java onlyWhat does Thread.onSpinWait() do inside a spin loop?
Why. This is an optional performance hint, potentially a no-op. It does not park until notification, request a scheduler yield, release a monitor or provide memory ordering. Any benefit to power or execution resources depends on the CPU and JVM.

Checkpoint

Write your own answer first. Then compare with the model answer, or ask Bar Raiser to mark it like an interviewer would.

Write code
Write a test-and-test-and-set spinlock with lock(), a nonblocking attempt (tryLock() in Java, try_lock() in C++), and unlock(). Use try and finally in Java or std::lock_guard in C++. Keep the memory ordering correct.
Model answer

Usage: in Java, lock.lock(); try { ... } finally { lock.unlock(); }; in C++, std::lock_guard<SpinLock> guard(spin); with <mutex> included. The successful acquire exchange reads a release from the previous owner. The preliminary read never grants ownership: two threads can both see false, but only one exchange can take it before the next unlock. Adding try_lock enables the try-to-lock forms of std::unique_lock and algorithms such as std::try_lock. A production choice would start with library locks and measurements before considering custom backoff.

Explain
An interviewer asks: "We have a cache guarded by one lock in a web service with 200 request threads on 16 cores. The critical section is short. Should we use a spinlock instead of a mutex?" Answer.
Model answer

I would keep the mutex and measure the remaining wait and runnable contention. Two hundred threads may mostly be asleep, but if many contend actively on 16 cores, spinners compete with the owner for CPU. A short critical section does not bound the wait when its owner is preempted, and this user-space spinlock has no priority-inheritance protocol.

A normal mutex usually has a cheap uncontended path and can park waiters; some implementations also spin briefly. If profiling confirms a bottleneck, shorten protected work, shard the cache or use a suitable concurrent map. Any proposed replacement must improve throughput and tail latency under representative oversubscription and preemption, not just on an idle machine.

Debug
A team replaced a std::mutex (or ReentrantLock) with a hand-written spinlock because a microbenchmark showed it was faster. In production, CPU usage went up and p99 latency got much worse, while the average barely changed. Explain what is going on.
Model answer

My leading hypothesis is active spinning amplified by preemption or oversubscription. An idle microbenchmark may rarely interrupt owners; production may have many more runnable threads, faults or longer critical sections. If an owner stalls, waiters burn CPU and can delay its return. Rare stalls can worsen p99 much more visibly than the mean, but the symptom alone does not prove that cause.

I would check CPU stacks for spin loops, runnable thread counts, and hold-time and wait-time distributions. Reproduce the workload with scheduling pressure and compare against the mutex. If spinning is responsible, restore parking and reduce contention with shorter critical sections, sharding or a suitable concurrent structure.

Out loudabout 90 s
"Walk me through a correct spinlock, then explain why repeatedly exchanging the flag is expensive."
What a strong answer covers
  • An atomic exchange grants ownership to exactly one contender until release
  • Acquire on successful acquisition and release on unlock order protected data
  • TTAS polls with atomic reads and uses an RMW only when the flag looks free
  • CPU hints provide neither ownership nor synchronization; jitter reduces retry collisions
  • The algorithm remains unfair, non-reentrant and blocking in the progress-guarantee sense
How a senior would say it
I would use an atomic exchange to acquire the flag, with acquire semantics, and a release operation to clear it on unlock. Only one contender can observe that the old flag was clear before the next release. That gives mutual exclusion. The release and the next successful acquire also order the protected data, so the counter inside the critical section can be plain. A simple test-and-set loop exchanges on every attempt, including while another thread owns the lock. Those read-modify-write operations compete for write ownership of the same cache line. With several waiters, the traffic can even delay the owner's unlock. I would instead use test-and-test-and-set. Waiters first poll with atomic reads, allowing shared cached copies. When a read sees clear, they attempt the exchange. Two threads can both see clear, so the read alone never grants ownership. In C++, that polling load can be relaxed because the successful exchange supplies acquire ordering. I would add a CPU spin hint and consider capped randomized backoff after collisions. Neither replaces synchronization or prevents preemption. The lock remains unfair and non-reentrant. It is also a blocking algorithm despite using atomic instructions: if the owner stops running, every waiter can stop making useful progress.

Likely follow up: What exactly breaks if the C++ unlock store is relaxed?

Out loudabout 90 s
"A spinlock improved our microbenchmark, but production CPU and p99 latency got worse. How would you diagnose that, and what would you use?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Keep a parking mutex as the baseline and treat preemption or oversubscription as hypotheses
  • Explain how a paused owner and runnable spinners can amplify one another's delay
  • Measure runnable load, hold and wait times, CPU stacks and tail latency under realistic contention
  • Explain why pinning, hints and backoff do not guarantee owner progress
  • Consider library adaptive waiting or reduced sharing; ticket fairness still depends on scheduling
How a senior would say it
I would use the parking mutex as the baseline and investigate whether spinning is amplifying owner delays. The microbenchmark may have short critical sections and idle cores. Production can have more runnable workers than cores, page faults, interrupts or occasional slow work under the lock. If the owner is paused, waiters keep consuming CPU without advancing the protected operation. They can occupy the capacity the owner needs to resume. That creates long waits for unlucky requests and can damage p99 much more visibly than the average. Repeated test-and-set operations can add cache traffic too, but I would confirm the cause rather than infer it from latency alone. I would inspect CPU stacks, runnable load, lock hold times and wait-time distributions, then reproduce the workload with realistic contention and forced scheduling pressure. Pinning restricts placement; it does not prevent preemption. Hints and jitter reduce some costs but cannot make a stopped owner run. My first implementation choice would be a library mutex that can park, possibly with its own bounded spin phase. I would then shorten the critical section or shard the shared state. A ticket lock orders admission, but a descheduled next ticket can still stall everyone. Fairness is not a latency bound.

Likely follow up: What changes if the waiters are Java virtual threads rather than platform threads?

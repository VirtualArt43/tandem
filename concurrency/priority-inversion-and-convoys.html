Priority inversion and lock convoys

Explain priority inversion, inheritance and ceilings, diagnose lock convoys, and distinguish portable guarantees from platform behaviour.

Before this:Livelock, starvation and fairnessThe scheduler, time slices and context switches

Sometimes a lock is barely contended and still ruins latency, because of who holds it and how it changes hands. A low priority thread holding a lock can stall the most urgent thread in the system, and a lock that hands itself over politely can make four cores slower than one.

In plain words

A clerk has given the only office key to a cleaner. A doctor urgently needs it, but the cleaner must finish putting supplies away before returning it. Now the manager keeps sending the cleaner on errands that are less urgent than the doctor's work. The doctor waits even though the errands do not need the key.

The fix is obvious once you see it: while the doctor waits, give the cleaner's remaining key-holding work the doctor's urgency so the errands cannot interrupt it. That is priority inheritance.

What priority inversion is

Take three threads sharing one CPU: H (high priority), M (medium) and L (low), with strict fixed-priority scheduling and an ordinary mutex without priority inheritance. L takes a lock; H wakes up, needs it and blocks. If L can now run and its remaining critical section has a known maximum duration, this is a bounded priority inversion: H waits for that remaining work.

Now M wakes up. M does not need the lock, but outranks L, so the scheduler runs M instead. L cannot release the lock while M, or further medium-priority work, keeps running. This is unbounded inversion: H's delay is no longer bounded by L's critical-section execution time.

The Mars Pathfinder resets

Mars Pathfinder suffered resets after landing in 1997. Flight software lead Glenn Reeves corrected the popular retelling: the shared mutex was inside VxWorks select() and pipe support, not an application-level information bus.

  • The low priority ASI/MET task held the resource while medium priority tasks preempted it, blocking the higher priority bc_dist task.
  • The bc_sched task detected that bc_dist had missed its completion deadline and initiated a reset.
  • Engineers enabled priority inheritance through a global configuration change affecting the relevant select() mutexes, tested the wider effects, then patched the spacecraft.

Reeves says the problem had been seen before landing, and already collected data survived resets. The lesson is to trace the actual dependency and validate a configuration change, rather than assume a small patch has small consequences.

The fixes

Fair admission is not priority inheritance. FIFO chooses who acquires next; inheritance helps the current owner run soon enough to release. An ordinary fair mutex cannot by itself stop medium-priority work from preempting its low-priority owner.

  • Priority inheritance. While a thread holds a lock that a higher priority thread is waiting for, the holder temporarily runs at the waiter's priority, so medium priority threads cannot preempt it. With inheriting locks along the dependency chain, the boost propagates to owners the holder is waiting for. Its effective priority reflects its base priority and all outstanding donations; releasing one lock need not remove donations from another. POSIX exposes it as the PTHREAD_PRIO_INHERIT mutex protocol, Linux implements it with priority inheritance futexes, and the real-time Linux kernel uses inheriting mutexes internally.
  • Priority ceiling. Each lock gets a ceiling at least as high as any thread that may use it. With immediate ceiling protection, its holder runs at least at that ceiling from acquisition, even without a waiter. Where supported, POSIX configures this with pthread_mutexattr_setprotocol(..., PTHREAD_PRIO_PROTECT) and pthread_mutexattr_setprioceiling(...). This is different from the original ceiling protocol, which also checks a system ceiling before permitting acquisition. Deadlock and blocking bounds require the chosen protocol's scheduling and resource-use assumptions; a ceiling attribute is not a general cure for arbitrary lock cycles.
  • Do not share locks across priority levels. If urgent and background threads must exchange data, consider immutable snapshots, separate ownership or a queue with suitable progress guarantees. A concurrent queue may itself use locks; even a lock-free queue gives no per-caller step bound. If H needs a result produced by L, the dependency survives message passing, so design scheduling and admission for that dependency too.
  • Keep critical sections short and free of anything that can block, so even a bounded inversion stays tiny.
  • A raw user space spinlock has no automatic inheritance. A loop around an atomic flag provides no owner and waiter relationship to the scheduler, so it cannot donate priority through that relationship. With strict priorities on one core, a high priority thread spinning for a lock held by a preempted low priority thread can spin forever. Raw kernel spinlocks normally disable preemption of their holder; other kernel lock types, including Linux PREEMPT_RT substitutions, have different rules. User space code should rarely spin for long (see spinlocks).

Java

Java 21 exposes no portable priority-inheritance or ceiling setting for synchronized or ReentrantLock. Platform-thread priorities do not promise execution order across OS and JVM configurations. Virtual threads always have Thread.NORM_PRIORITY; setPriority is ignored for them. Prefer short critical sections and deliberate ownership boundaries, and inspect a queue's implementation and producer dependencies before treating it as a latency solution.

Lock convoys

A lock convoy is a queue of threads repeatedly delayed behind a contended lock. A long hold or a preempted owner can create the queue; repeated blocking, wakeups and scheduling delays can then dominate the useful work. It can happen with fair or unfair locks and needs no priority difference.

One possible mechanism with a fair lock and tiny critical sections is:

  1. A releases while the next eligible waiter B is parked or not currently scheduled.
  2. B is made runnable, but may not run immediately. Depending on the lock, it receives ownership or must acquire the now-free lock.
  3. A is already running and wants to reacquire. Fair admission prevents it from passing B, so the lock may sit unused until B runs.
  4. Repeated handoffs can cost more than the protected work. This is workload dependent, not a context switch on every acquisition.

Barging permits an already running caller to acquire before a queued waiter, potentially reducing this idle time at the cost of overtaking. Default ReentrantLock, synchronized and std::mutex make no FIFO promise; the C++ standard does not prescribe a particular handoff implementation. Compare two policies with the following experiment.

Timing has no required ordering: startup, scheduling, JIT compilation and ThreadSanitizer affect these short runs. The C++ ticket lock also wakes all waiters and uses an internal mutex, so this is not an isolated measurement of fairness cost. For performance claims, use repeated warmed trials without sanitizers, representative work and per-thread wait distributions. Possible convoy mitigations are:

  • Shorter critical sections, so the lock is free more of the time.
  • Fewer acquisitions: remove a bounded batch per acquisition, then process items outside the lock. For example, Java ArrayBlockingQueue.drainTo(collection, maxElements) can batch removals. Bound batch size so long holds do not erase the benefit.
  • Spin briefly before sleeping. A lock implementation may benefit from brief adaptive spinning if the holder is running on another core and will release soon. There is no universal microsecond threshold; preemption, oversubscription and strict priorities can make spinning much worse. Measure before adding a custom spin loop.
  • Consider a barging lock if its overtaking behaviour fits the latency and fairness requirements; this can reduce handoff overhead but does not eliminate long-holder convoys.
  • Split the lock: striping, per-thread state merged later, or lock-free structures.

Pauses that hold locks

Any pause that hits a thread while it holds a lock is added to the wait of every thread queued behind it. The usual culprits:

  • Preemption. The holder's time slice ends in the middle of its critical section, and the lock stays held until it is scheduled again, which can be milliseconds on a busy machine. Waiters that spin instead of sleeping waste whole time slices.
  • Page faults and I/O. Touching memory that was swapped out, writing a log line to a slow disk, or doing a DNS lookup inside a critical section turns microseconds into milliseconds or seconds.
  • Garbage collection. A stop-the-world collection pauses application execution, including lock holders. Elapsed hold times and waits can span the pause even though the pause itself is not lock arbitration. Correlate timestamps with GC events rather than infer a cause from parked stacks alone.
  • Across machines. A process that holds a lease or a distributed lock and then pauses (a long GC, a VM migration) can wake up after the lease has expired and another node has taken the lock, then write as if it still owned it. A fencing token lets the storage system reject stale owners' writes; see distributed locks and fencing.

To find these, look at where threads wait, not where they run. Java Flight Recorder records threads blocked on monitors above a threshold (the jdk.JavaMonitorEnter event) and parked threads (jdk.ThreadPark); on Linux, scheduler tracing (perf sched) and off-CPU flame graphs show time spent waiting rather than running. Correlate the spikes with GC logs.

Worked example: p99 got worse after "fixing" starvation

Suppose a team changes a hot cache lock to fair admission after observing long waits. Throughput falls and p99 doubles. These are illustrative symptoms, not measurements from the sample above.

  1. Observe: CPU use falls and repeated dumps show many parked waiters with changing owners. This fits a convoy, but long critical sections or a slow dependency could also explain it.
  2. Test the hypothesis: measure hold times, wait times and wake-to-run delays, then correlate with GC and scheduler activity. In a toy model, 1 microsecond of useful work plus 9 microseconds of serial handoff delay permits about 100,000 acquisitions per second, versus 1,000,000 with no handoff delay.
  3. Reduce contention: move I/O and callbacks outside the lock when the invariant permits, stripe independent keys, and try bounded batches. These help long waits but do not prove starvation freedom.
  4. Preserve the requirement: compare fair and barging policies under load. If overtaking must be bounded, retain fair admission or introduce explicit request scheduling instead of discarding that requirement to improve throughput.

Common mistakes

  • Sharing one lock between urgent and background threads and expecting priorities to guarantee timely acquisition. Lowering the holder's priority can make delays worse.
  • Raising the waiter's priority to "fix" an inversion. The problem is the holder's priority, not the waiter's.
  • Spinlocks in user space under real-time priorities. An ordinary atomic-flag loop has no automatic donation; a spinning high priority thread can starve its holder forever on one core.
  • Making a hot lock fair and then wondering where the throughput went.
  • Logging, allocating heavily or doing I/O while holding a lock. Every pause inside is paid by every waiter.
  • Assuming Java thread priorities do anything reliable. Platform-thread behaviour varies; virtual-thread priorities are fixed.

In the interview

Priority inversion is a classic operating systems question, especially for systems and embedded roles, and Mars Pathfinder is a useful documented illustration. Lock convoys come up when you propose a fair lock or when an interviewer asks why latency got worse after a change.

Priority inversion is when a high priority thread waits for a lock held by a low priority thread, and medium priority threads keep the low one off the CPU, so the urgent work effectively runs at the lowest priority. A concrete fix is priority inheritance: the lock holder temporarily runs at the waiter's priority. The alternatives are a priority ceiling per lock, or not sharing locks across priority levels at all.
A lock convoy is repeated waiting behind a contended lock. A preempted holder can start it, and handoffs to parked waiters can sustain it. Fair admission can increase handoff delay, but fairness is neither necessary nor sufficient for a convoy. I measure hold times and wake-to-run delay, then reduce contention without abandoning the waiting guarantees the service needs.

Follow ups: "what is the difference between inheritance and ceiling?", "can a spinlock suffer priority inversion?", "does Java have priority inheritance?", "why would a fair lock lower throughput?", and "how do GC pauses interact with locks and leases?"

Senior depth

Inheritance is not deadlock prevention. If L holds X and waits for Y while Y's owner waits for X, boosting both leaves the cycle intact. If L is blocked on disk, extra CPU priority cannot finish the disk request. Restoring priority must account for every remaining donation and ceiling, not simply reset to the base value after any unlock.

The classic ceiling proofs assume a single processor with fixed-priority scheduling, correctly assigned ceilings, bounded critical sections and disciplined resource use. Immediate-ceiling proofs additionally depend on how equal effective priorities are scheduled and on holders not suspending arbitrarily. Multicore systems and mixed protocols require their own analysis; keep a global lock order unless you have established the applicable protocol's proof.

Key takeaways

  • Priority inversion: H depends on L; intervening medium-priority work can extend blocking beyond L's own critical-section time.
  • Mars Pathfinder (1997): deadline-check resets, fixed by enabling inheritance in the relevant select() mutex configuration.
  • Fixes: priority inheritance, priority ceilings, no locks shared across priority levels, short critical sections. Raw atomic-flag spinlocks provide no automatic inheritance; Java exposes neither protocol as a portable setting.
  • Convoys arise from contention and holder or handoff delays. Reduce sharing, shorten holds and bound batches; changing fairness is a tradeoff, not a universal cure.
  • Any pause while holding a lock (preemption, page faults, I/O, GC) is paid by every waiter, and across machines it can break a lease.

Quick check

Answer each question, then read why. Your score is saved for your module test.

Threads H (high), M (medium) and L (low) share one CPU with a strict priority scheduler. L holds lock X. H blocks on X. X has no inheritance or ceiling protocol. M becomes ready; M never uses X. What happens?
Why. H is blocked, so it cannot run. Of the ready threads, M outranks L, so M runs, and L never gets to release X. H is effectively demoted below M: an unbounded priority inversion. It is not a deadlock cycle: M is runnable and has no dependency on H or L. M need not finish; an endless stream of medium work can delay L indefinitely. Merely holding X does not change L's scheduling priority.
A low priority thread owns one mutex and a higher priority thread blocks on it. What does priority inheritance do?
Why. The problem is that the holder runs at a low priority, so the boost goes to the holder. For this single-lock case it loses the donation when the dependency ends. With several locks it may retain another donation, so its effective priority must be recomputed. Raising the blocked waiter alone does not schedule the owner; inheritance neither lowers unrelated threads nor promises FIFO acquisition.
In a toy convoy model, each acquisition serially costs 1 microsecond of useful work plus 9 microseconds of handoff delay. Ignore all other costs. What is the maximum acquisition rate?
Why. One complete cycle costs 10 microseconds, so the model permits 1,000,000 / 10 = 100,000 acquisitions per second. The first option ignores handoff delay; the third ignores useful work. Four contenders cannot multiply the capacity of one serialized lock. These are supplied model inputs, not measured OS timings.
Four threads hammer one lock with tiny critical sections. Switching it from barging to fair makes throughput collapse. Profiling shows short holds and long wake-to-run delays for queued waiters. What best explains it?
Why. The profile supports a convoy dominated by handoff delay. Barging can let a runnable caller acquire sooner, at the cost of bypassing queued callers. The evidence does not implicate per-waiter memory, disabling the JIT or a deadlock, and fairness does not require sleeping on every acquisition.
Which change best reduces a lock convoy on a hot queue whose consumer takes one item per lock acquisition?
Why. Fewer, shorter acquisitions mean fewer hand-overs and less time with the lock in transit. Processing the batch outside the lock keeps holds short. Fairness can add handoff delay, more consumers can add contention, and priority changes do not address the excessive acquisition count.
On one CPU with strict priorities, L has set an atomic flag to hold a spinlock. H preempts L, executes while (!flag.compareAndSet(false, true)) {} in Java or while (flag.test_and_set()) {} in C++, then prints acquired. Only L can clear the flag, and there are no priority boosts. What can H print?
Why. The atomic flag remains set: Java's CAS fails and C++'s test-and-set keeps returning true. H does not block, so strict priority scheduling keeps L off the CPU. These atomic operations are race-free but supply no scheduler-visible ownership or priority donation. Visibility does not clear a flag, and atomics alone provide neither fairness nor completion.
Java onlyA Java service shares one lock between latency-critical request threads and a low priority background thread. A teammate proposes background.setPriority(Thread.MIN_PRIORITY) and request.setPriority(Thread.MAX_PRIORITY). What is the best response?
Why. For platform threads the scheduling effect depends on the OS and JVM; for virtual threads setPriority is ignored. Neither makes the lock fair or ensures requests enter first, and these changes do not create a dependency cycle by themselves. A queue may contain locks or still depend on the background producer, so simply changing the data structure is not a proof of bounded latency.

Explain
In about a minute: explain priority inversion with the Mars Pathfinder example, and compare priority inheritance with a priority ceiling.
Model answer

Priority inversion means urgent work depends on a lower priority holder. Medium-priority work can keep that holder off the CPU, extending the urgent thread's wait beyond the holder's remaining critical-section work. Pathfinder's dependency caused a missed deadline and resets; enabling inheritance fixed that configuration. Inheritance donates a waiter's priority along supported lock dependencies and removes donations as those dependencies end. It does not resolve deadlock, disk waits or unlimited critical-section work. Immediate ceiling protection raises a holder to a configured resource ceiling on acquisition, even with no waiter. It requires knowing which priorities use each resource, and real-time blocking bounds depend on the scheduler and protocol assumptions. The original ceiling protocol also imposes an admission rule based on the system ceiling. Neither POSIX attributes nor a queue substitution justify an unconditional deadline guarantee in arbitrary Java or C++ code.

Debug
After a deploy, a service's throughput halved and CPU usage dropped from 70 percent to 30 percent. Thread dumps taken a few seconds apart show 40 of 48 worker threads parked waiting for the same ReentrantLock, with a different owner in each dump. The diff shows the lock was changed to new ReentrantLock(true). What happened and what do you do?
Model answer

A convoy from increased handoff delay is a strong hypothesis. Changing owners show some progress but do not identify its cost. I would measure hold time, wait time and the interval between waking a waiter and its running, and correlate with GC pauses and slow I/O. Then compare the old and new policies under the same load. If fairness addressed a real requirement, reverting blindly could reintroduce starvation. I would shorten holds, move callbacks and I/O out where safe, stripe independent keys, and remove bounded batches for processing outside the lock. If overtaking must be bounded, retain fair admission or introduce an explicit fair dispatcher. Validate throughput and per-request waiting tails together; aggregate CPU and average latency alone cannot establish that every request progresses.

"The highest priority thread is missing deadlines while a medium priority thread runs. Walk me through the inversion and choose a fix."
What a strong answer covers
  • H blocks on a mutex held by L, allowing runnable M to preempt L on one CPU
  • Explain why medium work extends blocking beyond the critical-section execution time
  • Donate priority to the owner and propagate through supported lock dependencies
  • Recompute donations on release and distinguish inheritance from immediate ceilings
  • State that deadlock, I/O and unbounded work survive a priority boost
  • State portable Java and C++ limits and evaluate remaining data dependencies
How a senior would say it
I would check whether the urgent thread is blocked on a resource held by a lower priority thread. On one CPU with strict priorities, L takes the mutex, H arrives and blocks, then runnable M preempts L. M never needs the mutex, but it prevents L from releasing it. That extends H's wait beyond the time needed for L's protected work. With priority inheritance, H donates its priority to L while the dependency exists, allowing L to outrank M and finish. If L waits on another inheriting mutex, the donation follows that chain. When a dependency ends, the scheduler recomputes the holder's priority from its base and any remaining donations. Immediate ceiling protection instead raises the holder to a configured resource ceiling from acquisition, whether or not anyone is waiting. Both need platform support and appropriate scheduling assumptions. Neither fixes a lock cycle, a disk operation or an unbounded critical section. Java exposes no portable setting for these protocols, and standard C++ mutexes do not promise them. I would also consider separating ownership or publishing snapshots, but check whether urgent work still needs a result from the background task. The fix must address the dependency, not just change a priority number.

Likely follow up: What happens if L owns two locks and releases only the one H was waiting for?

Out loudabout 75 s
"We made a hot lock fair. Throughput fell and CPU usage dropped. Would you revert the change?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Treat handoff overhead as a hypothesis and establish why fairness was introduced
  • Explain the idle gap while a fair lock's next eligible waiter is unscheduled
  • Measure hold, wait and wake-to-run delays and correlate pauses
  • Reduce sharing and use bounded batches processed outside the lock
  • Compare throughput and individual waiting tails without claiming starvation freedom from shorter holds
How a senior would say it
I would first establish why fairness was introduced and measure where time went. A fair lock can prevent a running caller from passing the next eligible waiter. If that waiter is parked or not scheduled, the lock may remain unused while useful work could otherwise run. Repeated handoffs can create a convoy, but falling CPU usage and changing owners alone do not prove it. I would measure critical-section hold times, acquisition waits and wake-to-run delays, then correlate those with GC and scheduler events. Long holds could point to I/O or callbacks inside the lock instead. I would move that work out where the invariant permits, stripe independent data, and try bounded batches that are processed after releasing the lock. Then I would compare fair and barging policies under representative load. If fairness protects a requirement, blindly reverting can bring starvation back. Shorter holds improve opportunities but do not prove bounded overtaking. I would accept a change only after checking throughput and individual waiting tails, including the oldest outstanding request, against the service's requirements.

Fair read-write lock

Build a primitivehard45 minThe readers-writers problem with no starvation on either side, built as a first come, first served monitorRubrik

Reader preference starves writers; writer preference starves readers. The answer to "neither may starve" is to serve threads in the order they arrive while still letting neighbouring readers share, and it is the follow up waiting behind every read-write lock answer.

The task

Build a read-write lock in which nobody can be overtaken. Every call to lockRead() or lockWrite() takes a place in a single line at the moment it is called, and threads enter in the order of that line:

  • A reader at the front of the line enters as soon as no writer is inside. Because the next reader in line then becomes the front, a run of readers that arrived one after another enters together and shares the lock.
  • A writer at the front of the line enters when nobody is inside, and holds the lock alone.
  • Nobody enters ahead of someone who arrived earlier: a reader that arrives while a writer is waiting waits behind that writer, even though readers are inside; and a writer that arrives while readers are waiting waits behind those readers.

With this rule both sides have bounded waiting: a thread waits only for the threads that arrived before it, so neither a stream of readers nor a stream of writers can starve the other.

Rules.

  • Build it from one lock and condition variables. No library read-write locks, StampedLock, AbstractQueuedSynchronizer or semaphores; no std::shared_mutex or POSIX rwlocks. The runner rejects code that names them.
  • The lock methods are not interruptible, like lock() on Java's library locks: an interrupted waiter keeps its place in line and keeps waiting. If you use wait() or await(), catch the interrupt, keep waiting, and restore the interrupt flag before returning (awaitUninterruptibly does this for you). A follow up asks why cancellation is hard here.
  • The lock is not reentrant and calls are balanced; you do not need to detect misuse.

What the tests check

  • Three readers share the lock; a writer holds it alone while a reader and a second writer wait.
  • A reader inside, then a writer waiting: a reader that arrives next waits behind the writer, and gets in only after it.
  • A writer inside, then a reader waiting, then a second writer waiting: when the first writer leaves, the reader goes before the second writer. (A writer preferring lock fails here.)
  • A writer inside and a line of seven arrivals, R R W R W R R: they enter in exactly that order, the first two readers share the lock, and the last two share it too.
  • Four readers keep the lock busy: a writer that arrives gets in after a few reader admissions. Three writers keep the lock busy: a reader that arrives gets in after a few writer admissions. Both bounds are checked by counting admissions, not by timing.
  • Java: a waiting reader that is interrupted keeps its place, enters in turn, and still has its interrupt flag set.
  • A stress run, 5 readers and 3 writers in random start order: never a writer with anyone else, a counter that only writers change (a plain field) ends exact, nothing deadlocks. In C++, ThreadSanitizer must stay quiet.

Taught in: Read-write locks and StampedLock

Open them one at a time. Each one gives away a little more.

You need a notion of "my place in line" that every thread gets at the moment it arrives, under the lock. Think of the ticket machine at a bank counter, the idea behind a ticket lock: what two numbers does it need?
Keep nextTicket (handed out on arrival) and nowServing (the ticket allowed to try entering next), plus the usual readers and writing. A thread takes ticket = nextTicket and adds one to nextTicket, then waits until ticket == nowServing and the lock is compatible: no writer inside for a reader, nobody inside for a writer.
The step that makes readers share: a reader that enters adds one to nowServing as it enters, not as it leaves, and wakes the others, so the next ticket can try at once; if it is a reader, it joins. A writer also advances nowServing when it enters, and the next ticket then waits for it to leave. Since every waiter waits for a different ticket, wake them all (signalAll, notify_all) and let each re-check.

What the interviewer asks after your first version works. Try to answer each one out loud before you open it.

Every change wakes every waiter. How would you avoid that thundering herd?
Give each waiter its own wakeup: a FIFO queue of nodes, each with its own condition variable (or a parked thread), and wake only the head of the line, plus the run of readers right behind it when the head is a reader. That is how production fair locks work: Java's AbstractQueuedSynchronizer keeps a queue of waiting threads and unparks only the next one. The ticket version is the simplest correct answer; the queue version is the efficient one.
Add tryLockWrite(timeout). Why is that harder with tickets?
A thread that gives up leaves a ticket behind that will never enter, and everyone behind it waits forever for nowServing to pass it. You need cancellation: either remember abandoned tickets and have nowServing skip them (waking the next thread when the head gives up), or switch to an explicit queue of nodes so a leaving node can unlink itself. The same problem is why interruption is not offered in this version.
First come, first served versus phase fair: what is the difference?
Phase fair locks alternate: when a writer leaves, every reader waiting at that moment enters together, then the next writer goes. Readers wait for at most one writer phase and get more parallelism, but arrival order is not kept (a reader that came after a writer can enter before it). Strict arrival order is easier to state, reason about and test; phase fair is often better for read heavy workloads. Say which one you built.
What does Java's library read-write lock do in fair mode?
Its documentation describes an approximately arrival order policy: when the lock is released, either the longest waiting single writer gets it, or, if a group of readers has been waiting longer than every waiting writer, that group gets the read lock. That is the same idea as this problem, built on a queue of waiting threads instead of tickets. The default (non fair) mode allows barging and makes no ordering promise.
The Little Book of Semaphores fixes writer starvation with a turnstile that writers hold while waiting and readers pass through. Is that fair?
Only as fair as the turnstile itself. If the underlying mutex or semaphore wakes waiters in an arbitrary order (C++'s std::mutex promises nothing), a thread can still be overtaken repeatedly. With a FIFO turnstile, such as Java's ReentrantLock in fair mode, it gives arrival order between readers and writers. Tickets make the order explicit instead of borrowing it from a primitive.
What does fairness cost?
Throughput. With no barging, every handover goes to a thread that is asleep, so it costs a wakeup and a context switch, and the lock sits idle meanwhile; an unfair lock lets a running thread grab it at once. Under heavy contention fair locks are commonly several times slower (an order of magnitude estimate; measure your workload). Fairness is worth it when bounded waiting matters more than throughput, as with interactive requests stuck behind batch jobs.

Try for at least twenty minutes first. Reading a solution feels like learning; writing one is learning.

The idea

Turn arrival order into numbers. Each thread takes a ticket when it arrives; nowServing is the oldest ticket that has not entered yet. The invariants: threads enter in ticket order; a writer inside means nobody else is inside; and only the ticket equal to nowServing may try to enter. Readers share because a reader advances nowServing the moment it enters, so the next ticket can try straight away; if it is also a reader, it enters too.

Why each line is there

  • Take the ticket under the lock. Two threads must never get the same ticket, and the ticket order is the arrival order by definition.
  • A reader waits for ticket == nowServing and no writer inside. It ignores other readers, so a run of readers flows in one after another while the first ones are still inside.
  • A writer waits for ticket == nowServing and nobody inside. It is at the front, so nobody new can enter ahead of it; it only waits for the readers already inside to drain.
  • Advance nowServing on entry, for both kinds. For a reader this is what lets the next reader join. For a writer, the next ticket becomes the head, sees writing and waits; it is woken when the writer leaves.
  • Broadcast. Waiters wait for different tickets, so a single signal could wake the wrong thread, which would go back to sleep while the right one never hears about it. A broadcast is correct; per-waiter conditions are the optimization.
  • Uninterruptible waits. A thread that leaves the line would strand its ticket and block everyone behind it, so it stays, and the interrupt is reported by restoring the flag.

Cost and trade-offs

Each operation is O(1) bookkeeping plus a broadcast, so a change wakes O(waiters) threads, most of which go back to sleep. That is acceptable for an interview and for modest contention; production fair locks keep an explicit queue and wake only the head. Fairness gives bounded waiting for everyone at the price of throughput, because no thread can barge in while the lock is free and the next in line is still waking up.

How to say it in the interview

I serve threads in arrival order with a ticket counter. Under one mutex I keep nextTicket, nowServing, the number of readers inside and a writing flag. A thread takes a ticket and waits until its ticket is being served and the lock is compatible: no writer for a reader, nobody at all for a writer. On entry it advances nowServing and broadcasts, so if the next in line is a reader it can join; that is how consecutive readers share. Every thread waits only for those who arrived earlier, so nobody starves. The costs are a broadcast on every change and no barging; the next step would be a queue of per-thread conditions so only the head is woken.

What the Concurrency section leaves out, and why

Tandem keeps a topic when it passes one of three tests:

  1. Asked: at least one interview report from Rubrik, Databricks, Google, Uber, Meta, Rippling or a similar company (2023 to 2026) asked it.
  2. Needed: you need it to solve or explain something that is asked.
  3. One step away: an interviewer can reach it with one natural follow up from something that is asked.

Everything else is listed below, in three groups: left out completely, kept as a name only (one paragraph, so you can recognise it and say one correct sentence), and saved for a later Tandem section. The sources are your own Concurrency-syllabus (its list of topics that are optional for L5), the master guide (v1.95) and the interview research in docs/research/.

If an interviewer ever asks you something on this page, tell Navigator. It goes into the course the same week.

Left out completely

Go, Python and Node runtime internals

The goroutine scheduler (G, M and P), the GIL, asyncio and libuv internals (master guide sections 10.2 to 10.4, syllabus 11.1 and 11.2). Interviewers ask about the language you code in, and you code in C++ and Java. What stays: the M:N idea in Kernel and user threads, the event loop in Server threading models and I/O models, and goroutines as a comparison point in Virtual threads and coroutines.

The Disruptor and lock free queues

The LMAX Disruptor and writing a multi producer lock free queue (syllabus 10.1). Your syllabus already marks these as only for infrastructure and low latency roles, and no report for the target companies asked them. What stays: the bounded ring buffer with a lock (ring-buffer-queue), CAS loops, and the lock free stack with the ABA problem (lock-free-stack), which is the one lock free structure that does get asked (Rubrik US reports lean toward atomic stacks).

Safe memory reclamation

Hazard pointers, epoch based reclamation and the kernel's RCU implementation. This is research grade. What stays: one paragraph in The lock free stack and ABA on why a C++ lock free stack cannot simply delete a popped node, and the read, copy, update idea in Copy on write and snapshots.

Lock internals inside the JVM

The object header mark word, biased locking and lock inflation stages (syllabus 11.3). Biased locking was deprecated in JDK 15 and removed in JDK 18, and your syllabus calls this too deep for L5. What stays: what synchronized guarantees, the monitor's entry set and wait set, and how any lock is built from an atomic word plus a wait queue in How locks work.

Formal memory model theory

Proofs, causality rules, out of thin air values and memory_order_consume. What stays: visibility, reordering, the happens before rules, safe publication, and the practical choice between seq_cst, acquire and release, and relaxed, all in module 06.

Proving algorithms correct

Formal linearizability proofs, consensus numbers, wait free universal constructions and model checking with TLA+. What stays: what linearizability means and how to name the linearization point of an operation in Linearizability.

Transactional memory

Software and hardware transactional memory. Not asked anywhere in the research.

Real time scheduling theory

Rate monotonic and earliest deadline first scheduling, and the priority ceiling protocol in depth. What stays: priority inversion and priority inheritance, which show up in OS flavoured online assessments, in Priority inversion and convoys.

Interrupt context and driver concurrency

Interrupt handlers, spinlocks taken in interrupt context, and kernel driver locking. These come up only in embedded and driver roles (Nvidia 2026 and a Google embedded loop in the research). What stays: spinlocks, how a mutex is built, and futexes, in Spinlocks and How locks work.

GPU, SIMD and cluster parallelism

CUDA, SIMD intrinsics, OpenMP and MPI. These are data parallel topics for other roles. What stays: CPU parallel speedup and Amdahl's law in Fork join and parallel work and Queueing math.

Framework tours

Akka, Erlang OTP, RxJava, Project Reactor and Kotlin coroutines. What stays: the ideas they are built on: actors and single writers (Single writer and actors) and backpressure (Backpressure and load shedding).

C++20 coroutines in depth

Writing coroutine types, promise types and awaiters. Interviews do not ask you to write them. What stays: what a coroutine is, how it differs from a thread, and when you would pick one, in Virtual threads and coroutines.

Kept as a name only

These get a short paragraph where they belong, enough to recognise the term and say one correct sentence about it. They are never in a test as more than that.

  • Work stealing internals. Know that fork join pools give each worker its own deque, a worker takes from its own end and steals from the other end of someone else's, and why that balances load. The steal protocol itself is out. See Fork join and parallel work.
  • The Universal Scalability Law. Know that it adds a coherence cost to Amdahl's law, so throughput can fall after a peak as you add threads. No curve fitting. See Performance and contention.
  • The Michael and Scott lock free queue. Know that it exists, that it uses two CAS steps, and why it is hard (ABA, reclamation). See Progress guarantees.
  • Seqlocks and StampedLock optimistic reads. Know the idea: readers do not lock, they check a version before and after. See Read write locks.
  • Deterministic simulation testing. Know what FoundationDB style testing is and why it finds bugs that stress tests miss. See Testing concurrent code.
  • Hedged requests. Know the idea and the warning: only for idempotent reads, and they add load. See Bulkheads, timeouts and retries.
  • NUMA, thread affinity and thread priorities. Know what they are and that they matter for latency critical systems. See Caches and false sharing.

Saved for later Tandem sections

These are real interview topics, but they belong to sections that come after Concurrency. The Concurrency section teaches the part that touches threads and shared state, and stops there.

  • Distributed Systems section: consensus (Raft, Paxos, Zab), leader election, two phase commit and sagas in depth, vector clocks, CRDTs, and consistency models across replicas. Concurrency keeps idempotency, exactly once effects, optimistic and pessimistic control, distributed locks with fencing tokens, and ordering by ownership (module 16).
  • System Design section: full design rounds such as a ticketing system or a job scheduler end to end. Concurrency keeps the concurrency part of those rounds: Design cases covers flash sales, seat holds, double booking, job claims with leases and counters at scale.
  • Databases (inside System Design): MVCC implementation, the lock manager and B tree latching. Concurrency keeps isolation levels and their anomalies (lost update, write skew), SELECT ... FOR UPDATE, SKIP LOCKED and conditional writes in Database isolation and anomalies.
  • LLD section: full object oriented design rounds. Concurrency keeps the thread safety follow ups that LLD rounds add (parking lot, KV store with transactions, seat booking) in the practice bank.

How this compares with your syllabus

Your Concurrency-syllabus lists three optional buckets for L5. Tandem treats them like this:

  1. Hardware and memory model depth: kept at the working level (visibility, reordering, fences as a concept, false sharing), because Rubrik, Databricks and Google follow ups reach it. Proofs are left out.
  2. Lock free internals: CAS loops, ABA and the lock free stack are kept and practised. The Disruptor and lock free queues are left out.
  3. Runtime internals: kept for Java and C++, your two languages. Go, Python and Node are left out.

Additions the research forced, beyond your syllabus:

  • System coding rounds (module 17). Rubrik and Databricks ask you to build executors, job runners, durable log writers and file processing code without library help. Your syllabus did not list these, and they are now a full module with their own practice problems.
  • Operating system basics that phone screens and online assessments ask next to concurrency: Virtual memory and paging, CPU scheduling arithmetic in The scheduler and context switches, and process states.
  • Timed drills: online assessment style sets on a clock (mixed threading and OS, predict the output, code review), because Rubrik and others screen with timed MCQs.
  • About 30 more practice problems taken from interview reports (LinkedIn's producer consumer with shutdown, Google's timer multiplexer and reorder buffer, Uber's ride dispatcher and circuit breaker, Rubrik's page cache, node rebalancer and bank debugging round, and others).

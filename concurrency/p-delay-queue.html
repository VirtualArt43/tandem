Delay queue

Thread-safe structureshard40 minCore of the delayed scheduler question

A delay queue hands out items only when they are due, earliest first. It is a common building block for scheduled executors and timers, and the delayed scheduler question that LinkedIn, Netflix and Confluent candidates report is this structure plus worker threads. A subtle part is waking a sleeping taker when an earlier item arrives.

The task

Build a thread-safe queue whose items become available only after a delay:

  • put(item, delayMs) adds an item that is due at clock() + delayMs.
  • take() blocks until the earliest item is due, then removes and returns it. It never returns an item before its due time.
  • poll() removes and returns the earliest item if it is due now, otherwise returns none without waiting for an item or its deadline.
  • size() counts every item, due or not.

Items with the same due time come out in the order their insertions take effect under the lock. Non-overlapping puts retain their call order; concurrent puts may take effect in either order. An item is due when clock() >= dueTime. Each removal is atomic, including when poll races take. There is no required FIFO order among waiting threads.

Rules:

  • Measure every due time on the injected clock. When the earliest item is not due yet, take waits on a condition for dueTime - clock() milliseconds of real time, then reads the clock again. The tests pass a fake clock that moves only when the test moves it, which makes deadline comparisons exact while thread scheduling remains real; one test uses the real clock (System.nanoTime() / 1_000_000, or std::chrono::steady_clock).
  • A put that becomes the new earliest item must make waiting takers re-plan. When several items become due, one returning taker must not strand other takers on an obsolete deadline. Broadcasting is sufficient; signalling one with a correct handoff is also allowed.
  • The injected clock is thread-safe, monotonic, quick, non-throwing and does not call back into the queue. Its origin may be negative. Use signed 64 bit time values and comparisons. Delays are nonnegative, Java items are non-null, and time arithmetic, duration conversions and sequence counters do not overflow in this exercise.
  • A fake-clock advance does not itself notify the queue. A taker observes it on its next timeout or queue notification. put, poll and size may wait to acquire the mutex, but never wait for an item to become due.
  • Java take propagates InterruptedException when interrupted while waiting with no competing ready item, without consuming an item or leaving the lock held. If readiness races interruption, either returning the item or throwing is acceptable, but the queue must remain consistent. If already interrupted on entry, it throws even when an item is ready. C++ has no cancellation API in this exercise.
  • No library delay queues, blocking queues or scheduled executors. A plain heap is fine: PriorityQueue in Java, std::priority_queue or std::push_heap and std::pop_heap in C++, all used under your own lock.

What the tests check

  • Due order, exact expiry boundaries and sizes, including empty poll and 64 equal deadlines drained through alternating poll and take calls.
  • Every put uses the clock at insertion time. Puts at fake times 1000 and 1040 with delays 50 and 20 become due at 1050 and 1060.
  • Signed 64 bit clocks, including a negative origin and a gap of 3,000,000,000 ms between deadlines. Narrowing a comparison to 32 bits must not reverse their order.
  • take keeps waiting after 300 ms of real time when its item is due at fake time 100 and the fake clock is still zero. Advancing the clock then lets the next timed check return it.
  • An earlier put wakes a taker waiting for a later item, and an empty queue blocks until a put arrives.
  • Four takers waiting for a distant head must all finish after an earlier batch becomes due. Only the first put in that batch changes the head; the original future item must remain queued.
  • Java interruption during empty and timed waits, plus an interrupt already set on entry. Items remain available and later queue operations still work.
  • Mixed contention with a seeded start order: four producers, two takers and two pollers transfer 1,200 unique items while calling size. Every item is returned exactly once and the queue ends empty.
  • Four producers and four takers transfer another 1,200 items with seeded random delays. The fake clock advances after production; items are never early and each consumer sees nondecreasing due times.
  • The real clock: three items with delays of 300, 100 and 200 ms come out in due order, none early, with a one-second allowance for scheduling delay.
  • Bounded waits and a watchdog report stalled implementations. C++ also runs under ThreadSanitizer. These tests exercise specific schedules; they do not prove every possible interleaving correct.

Taught in: Scheduled executors and timers

Open them one at a time. Each one gives away a little more.

Keep the items in a min-heap ordered by due time, with a sequence number to break ties, all under one lock. poll is then easy: look at the head, compare with the clock.
take loops: if the heap is empty, wait on the condition; if the head is due, remove and return it; otherwise wait on the same condition with a timeout of due - now. When the wait ends, for whatever reason, go round the loop and look again.
A taker sleeping until the old head's due time must hear about an earlier item. So put signals the condition when the new item becomes the head of the heap. Several takers may be waiting for that head, so wake them all (signalAll, notify_all); each re-plans its wait. A plain sleep cannot react to an earlier put, even if you release the lock first.

What the interviewer asks after your first version works. Try to answer each one out loud before you open it.

When many takers wait, they all wake for the same head. What does Java's DelayQueue do about that?
It uses the leader-follower waiting pattern. One taker, the leader, does the timed wait for the head; every other taker waits with no timeout. When a taker exits, including on interruption, it signals a follower if no leader remains and the heap is nonempty. A put that installs a new head clears the leader and signals, so someone re-plans. This reduces redundant timed waits; it does not promise exactly one wakeup per item.
Turn this into a scheduled executor.
Put tasks in the delay queue and start N worker threads that loop on take() and run each task, catching its exceptions so a worker never dies. cancel marks the task and workers skip cancelled tasks. A fixed-rate task advances its planned deadline from the previous planned deadline; a fixed-delay task measures the delay from completion. Reinsert with the remaining delay, clamped to zero if already overdue. Define whether shutdown drains or discards pending tasks, reject new submissions, and wake workers so they can observe that policy. This is the delayed scheduler question asked at LinkedIn, Netflix and Confluent.
Make it a bounded blocking priority queue instead: no due times, highest priority first, at most N items.
Same heap and lock, with two conditions: producers wait on "not full", consumers on "not empty"; take returns the head at once instead of waiting for a due time. A LinkedIn candidate (2022) was asked for a blocking queue written by many threads and read by one, then many readers, and backed it with a priority queue; an Amazon candidate (2019) had ranked producers whose items consumers must take in rank order and used one queue per rank, each with a mutex and two conditions. Mention starvation: a steady stream of high-priority items starves low ones, so add aging if that matters.
Why inject a clock instead of reading the wall clock?
The wall clock can jump backwards or forwards (NTP corrections, manual changes), which makes delays wrong; a delay needs a monotonic clock such as System.nanoTime or std::chrono::steady_clock. Injecting it also makes tests deterministic: a fake clock lets you check "not early" exactly at the boundary instead of sleeping and hoping.
How do you cancel an item that is already in the heap?
Either mark it cancelled and let take discard it when it reaches the head (lazy deletion: marking a known handle is O(1), but cancelled items can retain memory until they reach the head), or remove it from the heap. A plain heap removal is O(n) to find the item; storing each item's heap index in the item makes it O(log n), which is what Java's ScheduledThreadPoolExecutor does for its default task objects when remove-on-cancel is enabled.
What if there are millions of timers?
A heap costs O(log n) per insert and its single lock becomes hot. Timing wheels put timers into buckets by expiry time. Bucket insertion can be O(1), while cancellation needs a direct handle for efficient removal; expiry work still grows with the number of timers in a bucket. Hierarchical wheels cover larger time ranges using several levels; choose the resolution and account for bucket maintenance. Sharding the queue by key is another way to split the lock.

Try for at least twenty minutes first. Reading a solution feels like learning; writing one is learning.

The idea

A min-heap ordered by (due time, sequence number) under one lock, and one condition that means "the head may have changed". The safety invariant is that removal takes the earliest (due time, sequence number), and only when the injected clock says it is due. In the reference, a taker waits indefinitely only while the heap is empty; otherwise it requests a timed wait for the remaining delay. An earlier insertion signals the takers to re-plan. Scheduling and lock contention can delay the actual return, so this is not a real-time guarantee.

Why each line is there

  • A sequence number in the ordering. Heaps are not stable, so equal due times would come out in an arbitrary order without it.
  • A timed wait on a condition, not a sleep. The timed wait releases the lock, so producers can put, and it can be cut short by a signal. A plain sleep does not release a held mutex and cannot be cut short by a queue signal. Manually releasing the mutex before sleeping still misses earlier puts.
  • Re-check after every wakeup. The wait can end because time is up, because the head changed, because another taker took the item, or spuriously. In every case the loop looks at the head and the clock again, so an item is never returned early.
  • Signal when the head changes. This is the bug the tests are built around:
  • Wake all takers on a new head. Each of them was waiting for the old head's time; all must re-plan. The leader-follower pattern in the follow ups reduces this to one.
  • The clock is read for every insertion and readiness check. Reusing the constructor's time mis-schedules later puts. The clock read and heap mutation are protected by the same lock; poll and size need that protection too.
  • Cancellation is part of Java's contract. lockInterruptibly handles an interrupt already set on entry, and condition waits propagate interruption. The finally block releases the lock on every exit.

Cost

Heap insertion is amortized O(log n), and removal is O(log n). An empty or not-yet-due poll and size take O(1) work, excluding lock contention. Storage is O(n). take may wait indefinitely, and broadcasting wakes up to all waiting takers. A frozen fake clock can cause repeated timeouts for the same head, so wakeups are not bounded by the number of items alone.

How to say it in the interview

I keep a min-heap ordered by due time, with a sequence number to keep equal times in put order, under one lock with one condition. Take loops: if the heap is empty it waits; if the head is due it returns it; otherwise it waits on the condition with a timeout of due minus now, then re-checks. Put adds to the heap and, if the new item became the head, signals all waiters so they re-plan for the earlier time. That signal is the subtle part: a taker that just slept until the old due time would miss an earlier item. With many takers I would use the leader-follower trick from Java's DelayQueue so only one thread does the timed wait.

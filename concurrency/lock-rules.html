The rules of locking

Seven rules that decide which lock, around what, and what never to do while holding one, with the deadlock that alien calls cause.

Asked atLinkedInUberRubrik
Before this:Mutexes and RAII guardsInvariants, critical sections and finding shared state

Taking a lock is easy. Knowing which lock, around exactly what, and what you must never do while holding it is what interviewers grade, and what separates a correct design from one that is correct and slow, or correct until the day it deadlocks. Seven rules cover nearly all of it.

In plain words

A library lends out the keys to its study rooms. Its rules: one key per room, not one per chair and not one master key for the whole building. Take the key even if you only want to peek inside. Do not take a phone call while holding a key, because people are queuing for it. Keep keys behind the counter, never on a hook by the door where anyone can pocket them. If you ever need two rooms, always collect the keys in room number order. And the rules are written on the wall, not kept in the librarian's head.

Rule 1: one lock per invariant

A lock exists to protect an invariant. Fields that take part in the same invariant share one lock; fields that are independent should not be dragged under it. One lock per field breaks multi-field invariants (you saw the upside down range in the previous lesson). One lock for everything is correct but serializes unrelated work, and it hides which state actually belongs together.

These sketches assume positive amounts, no arithmetic overflow and a history insertion that cannot fail. A production implementation must also preserve the balance/history invariant on exceptions; releasing a lock does not undo a partial update.

A useful corollary: the cheapest invariant is the one you delete. A separate activeCount field that must always equal sessions.size() is an invariant you have to protect; asking the map for its size under the lock removes it.

Rule 2: guard every access, reads included

The lock's visibility guarantee only reaches threads that take it. Every read of guarded state (getters, size(), isEmpty(), toString(), iteration, logging) takes the same lock as the writes. An unlocked read can see a stale value or a half-finished update, and in C++ it is a data race. If something must be readable without the lock, use an atomic field, Java volatile for an independent value, or safely published immutable state, and say so. C++ volatile does not synchronize threads.

Rule 3: short critical sections, but no shorter than the invariant

Everything inside a critical section runs one thread at a time, so its length directly limits throughput. A lock held for 10 microseconds per operation allows at most about 100,000 operations per second, however many cores you have: one over the hold time. Worse, waiting time grows long before that ceiling, the way a queue at a single counter does:

At 50 percent utilization an operation waits about one hold time on average; at 90 percent, about nine. The model is an approximation (it assumes random arrivals and hold times), but the shape is real, and it is why queueing math matters for locks too. So: prepare outside, commit inside. Parse, validate, allocate, format and compute without the lock; take it only to check and change shared state. But never split the check and the change across two critical sections, which is the bug from the previous lesson.

Rule 4: nothing slow and nothing foreign under a lock

Slow: disk and network I/O, database calls, RPCs, sleep, waiting on a future or on another thread, and logging that might block. Each one turns a microsecond critical section into milliseconds, and every other thread that needs the lock queues behind it; one slow holder can line up the whole service behind it (a lock convoy). The exception is a condition wait, which releases the lock while waiting.

Foreign: any code you do not control, called while holding your lock: listeners and callbacks, user-supplied lambdas, overridable methods, even equals and hashCode of keys someone else wrote. You do not know what that code does. It may block, take other locks, or call back into your object.

The listener did it on the account's behalf, while reconcile takes them in the opposite order: a classic deadlock, assembled from two pieces of code that each look harmless. One consequence to accept: listeners may now run in a different order from the changes, or see a balance that has already changed again. If they need commit ordering, enqueue the events under the same lock as the state changes and drain them on one consumer thread. Enqueueing after unlocking can itself reorder events. Do not wait for queue space while holding the state lock; define what a full queue means before committing the change.

Rule 5: lock objects nobody else can reach

In Java, synchronized methods lock this, and any code holding a reference to your object can lock it too, by accident or on purpose, and stall every one of your methods. Lock a private lock object object instead, declared private final so it can never be swapped. Never lock string literals, boxed numbers or other shared constants: unrelated code may be locking the very same object. In C++, keep the mutex a private member and never hand it out: if callers need a compound operation, add a method that does it under the lock.

The documented exception is client-side locking, such as Collections.synchronizedList, which tells callers to lock the list itself while iterating. It works only if every caller remembers; offering the compound operation is safer.

Rule 6: two locks at once only in a fixed order

Holding one lock while taking another is how deadlocks are born: thread 1 holds A and wants B, thread 2 holds B and wants A. If you must hold two, every thread must take them in the same global order, called lock ordering (by account id, by address, by a level number), or take them together with a primitive that avoids deadlock. Better still, restructure so you never need two: the open call above removed the second lock entirely.

The Java sketch assumes unique, immutable account ids; equal ids for distinct accounts require a tie breaker. These sketches show lock acquisition, not complete financial validation. The full treatment (ordering by id with tie breakers, tryLock with back off, lock hierarchies, detection) is in taking several locks safely and deadlock.

Rule 7: write the policy down

A locking scheme that lives in one person's head breaks at the next change. For every class with shared state, document: which lock guards which fields (a guarded by comment on each), which invariant each lock protects, the lock order if a method ever takes two, and which private methods expect the caller to hold the lock (a common convention is a name ending in Locked, such as evictLocked()). Java's @GuardedBy and Clang's thread safety annotations let tools check part of it.

Common mistakes

  • Locking everything "to be safe". Locks around state that is immutable, confined or independent add contention and deadlock risk and hide which state matters. Lock invariants, nothing else.
  • A lock per field. Each field stays consistent; the invariant that links them does not.
  • I/O, RPCs, logging or sleep inside the lock. Turns microseconds into milliseconds for every waiting thread.
  • Calling listeners, callbacks or overridable methods while locked. Deadlocks and reentrancy surprises you cannot see from your own code.
  • Locking this, a string literal, a boxed number, or a field that can be reassigned. Outside code can share it, or threads end up locking different objects.
  • Taking a second lock with no ordering rule. It works until two threads take the pair in opposite orders.
  • Undocumented locking. The next person adds a method that touches a guarded field without the lock.

In the interview

Interviewers rarely ask "list the rules of locking". They watch you apply them. LinkedIn interviewers have asked candidates to give, for each lock they add, an example of the race it prevents; that is rule 1 spoken out loud. An Uber interviewer once complained on Blind that candidates "put synchronization everywhere"; over-locking reads as not understanding the problem. Rubrik's debugging rounds hand you code that breaks these rules, and design rounds ask "what happens to latency when this lock gets hot?".

I'll use one lock per invariant: the map and its size move together, so they share a lock, and the last-access timestamp is an atomic on its own. Every read of guarded state takes the lock too. Inside the lock I only check and update shared state; parsing happens before, and the database write and the listener calls happen after I release it, so I never hold the lock across I/O or call unknown code while holding it. The lock is a private object, and this class never holds two locks at once, so there is no lock ordering to get wrong.

Follow ups to expect: "Why not one lock for the whole service?" (it serializes unrelated work: throughput is capped at one over the hold time). "Why not call the listener inside the lock, so it sees a consistent state?" (deadlock and stall risk; copy the state it needs, or queue events). "What if two locks are unavoidable?" (a global order, or std::scoped_lock; tryLock with back off). "How would you find a hot lock in production?" (thread dumps with many threads blocked on one monitor, lock profiling with JFR or perf).

Senior depth

A lock is a single-server queue. Its utilization is the arrival rate times the hold time. Halving the hold time doubles the ceiling and cuts waiting far more than half near saturation, which is why moving work out of a critical section is often the most effective fix there is. When the hold time cannot shrink, split the lock along invariant boundaries: lock striping gives each partition its own lock.

Rules trade against each other. Shorter critical sections push you toward splitting them, which can break invariants (rule 3 against rule 1). Open calls remove deadlock risk but deliver events out of order (rule 4 against ordering needs). Saying which rule you bent and why, for example "I hold the lock across this map update and its index update because they form one invariant, but the notification happens after", is the senior signal.

Sometimes the best lock is none. Confinement (one owner thread fed by a queue), immutability with atomic swaps of whole snapshots, and atomic variables for single values all remove locks rather than tuning them. Those are the higher rungs of the safety ladder.

Key takeaways

  • One lock per invariant; independent state gets its own lock or an atomic; delete invariants you do not need.
  • Every access to guarded state, reads included, takes the lock.
  • Prepare outside, commit inside: a lock caps throughput at one over its hold time, and waiting explodes near that cap.
  • Nothing slow (I/O, sleeps, waits) and nothing foreign (callbacks, listeners) under a lock: use open calls.
  • Lock private objects; never hold two locks without a fixed global order; write the policy down.

Quick check

Answer each question, then read why. Your score is saved for your module test.

Which rule does this code break, and why does it matter?
Why. Holding a lock across I/O turns a microsecond critical section into milliseconds for everyone. The consistency argument is tempting, but a process lock cannot make a cache and a remote database consistent anyway (the save can fail or time out after the cache changed). Define the database as the source of truth, then use a cache invalidation or versioning policy that handles concurrent completions and failures. Simply moving the save outside the lock does not establish consistency.
A class has balance and history, which must always agree, and an unrelated lastLoginTime. Which design follows "one lock per invariant"?
Why. balance and history form one invariant, so they need one lock held across both updates; separate locks or separate thread-safe objects let readers see the new balance with the old history. lastLoginTime is not part of that invariant, so putting it under the money lock would only add contention.
Every request takes one global lock and holds it for 50 microseconds. Ignoring everything else, what is the highest request rate this lock allows, however many cores you add?
Why. Only one thread can hold the lock at a time, so at most 1 / 50 microseconds = 20,000 critical sections run per second, no matter how many cores wait. In practice latency explodes well before that ceiling, because waiting time grows like a queue as utilization approaches 100 percent.
withdraw holds the account's lock while it calls every registered listener. One listener writes to an audit log that has its own lock. Meanwhile another thread holds the audit lock and calls account.balance(), which needs the account's lock. What can happen?
Why. The listener silently made withdraw take the account lock and then the audit lock, while reconcile takes them in the opposite order: a lock order cycle. Reentrancy only helps one thread re-taking a lock it already holds (and std::mutex is not reentrant anyway). Plain locks never time out.
When is it acceptable for a thread to hold two locks at the same time?
Why. Deadlock needs a cycle in the order locks are taken. A single global order (or an all-at-once acquisition with deadlock avoidance) makes the cycle impossible. Reentrancy and short sections do nothing about cycles: two threads can deadlock on two microsecond-long sections.
Java onlyA Cache class guards its state with synchronized methods. Code in another class runs synchronized (cache) { exportEverything(); }, which takes 10 seconds. What happens to other threads calling cache.get()?
Why. Synchronized instance methods lock this, and any code with a reference can lock the same object. Using a private final Object lock inside Cache makes the cache's lock unreachable from outside, so outside code cannot stall it by accident.
C++ onlytransfer(from, to) does std::lock_guard<std::mutex> g1(from.m); std::lock_guard<std::mutex> g2(to.m);. One thread runs transfer(a, b) while another runs transfer(b, a). What can happen, and what is the fix?
Why. Taking locks in argument order means opposite calls take them in opposite orders, which is a cycle. std::scoped_lock with several mutexes uses a deadlock avoidance algorithm (the same as std::lock). The balances are both locked when touched, so there is no data race. Where its deadlock detector is supported (Linux builds, for example), ThreadSanitizer can also report this lock order inversion, even on runs that happen not to hang.

Checkpoint

Write your own answer first. Then compare with the model answer, or ask Bar Raiser to mark it like an interviewer would.

Write code
Rewrite the session store from the worked example so it follows all seven rules. Keep addListener, login, logout, activeCount and a way to get all sessions.
Model answer

One private lock guards the one invariant-bearing structure, the map, and the redundant counter is gone, so there is nothing left to drift. Every read takes the lock and returns a copy.

The audit line goes to a writer that does the I/O on its own thread, and listeners are called after the lock is released, from a copied list in C++ or a CopyOnWriteArrayList in Java, so I/O and listener invocation happen outside the state lock. Copies and destruction of session values and callback targets inside the lock must also be nonblocking and non-reentrant. The class never holds two locks, so there is no ordering to document, and the comments say what each lock guards.

These snapshots copy the map, not arbitrary mutable objects reachable from its values: assume immutable sessions and user data, or give that state a separate protection policy. A listener may receive a login event after the session has logged out again. That is acceptable only if notifications describe past events.

If commit order matters, enqueue events under the commit lock for a single consumer, with an explicit policy for a full queue. Ordered delivery still does not freeze the live session state.

Numbers
A service handles 30,000 requests per second, and every request holds one global lock for 40 microseconds. What is the lock's utilization, what will you observe, and what would you do?
Model answer

Utilization is the arrival rate times the hold time: 30,000 per second times 40 microseconds is 1.2, or 120 percent. The lock can serve at most 1 / 40 microseconds = 25,000 critical sections per second, so it cannot keep up: requests queue behind it faster than they drain, latency grows without bound, threads pile up blocked on that one lock (a thread dump shows most of them waiting on the same monitor), and throughput flattens at about 25,000 per second no matter how many cores or threads you add.

Fixes, in the order I would try them: shrink the hold time by moving everything that does not touch shared state out of the critical section (parsing, formatting, I/O, logging); then split the lock along invariant boundaries, for example by striping by key so different keys use different locks; replace single-value state with atomics or striped counters; or confine the state to one owner thread fed by a queue, which also lets you batch. The target is utilization well below 1: even at 80 percent, an M/M/1 style estimate gives an average wait of about four hold times.

Explain
An interviewer says: "You put a lock around everything. Why is that not good enough?" Answer as you would out loud.
Model answer

Locking everything can be correct, but it is not good engineering, for four reasons. First, it may still not be correct: if callers combine two calls, like check then insert, each call is locked but the sequence is not, so the class has to offer the compound operations instead. Second, it serializes work that never needed it: state that is immutable, confined to one thread, or independent ends up under the same lock as everything else, and the lock caps throughput at one over its hold time. Third, locks around everything usually end up around I/O and callbacks too, which stalls every thread behind one slow call and creates deadlocks through code I do not control. Fourth, it hides the design: nobody can tell which fields actually form an invariant.

What I would do instead: classify the state, write down the invariants, put one lock around each invariant and nothing else, keep I/O and callbacks outside, use atomics for independent single values, and for every lock be able to name the race it prevents. That last part is exactly what I would expect to be asked next.

Say it out loud

Interviews are spoken. Answer each question out loud as if the interviewer just asked it. Tandem transcribes you on this Mac, word for word, measures your pace, fillers and pauses, and Bar Raiser scores what you said and how you said it.

Out loudabout 75 s
"You are reviewing a session store with a map, a separate active count, and an unrelated last-login timestamp. Where do you put the locks, and why?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Start with the invariant that the active count equals the map size, preferably eliminating the redundant counter
  • Guard the map and every related access with one private lock; give the independent timestamp its own synchronization
  • Prepare inputs outside the lock, but keep duplicate checks and insertion together
  • Return snapshots or answers computed under the lock instead of exposing guarded state
  • Keep I/O and callbacks outside, and document the fields and invariant protected by the lock
How a senior would say it
I would start with the invariant: the active count must equal the number of sessions. My first choice is to remove that counter and read the map's size under its lock. That deletes a consistency obligation. If the counter must stay, its updates and the map updates belong in the same critical section. Every getter and logout operation must follow that same private lock. The unrelated timestamp can have separate synchronization, such as an atomic field, rather than waiting behind session changes. I would prepare and validate a new session before locking. But the duplicate check and insertion stay together, because another login could otherwise slip between them. A snapshot getter copies the map while locked and returns the copy; for mutable session objects, I must also define how their state is protected. Finally, database calls and listener notifications happen outside the critical section, using the state copied for that event. I would document the guarded fields and the invariant next to them. For each lock, I should be able to name the conflicting operations it prevents.

Likely follow up: If two logins use the same session identifier, what can go wrong with an unconditional increment of the separate counter?

Out loudabout 90 s
"Withdrawals hang while a reconciliation job is running. Withdraw calls a listener under the account lock; the listener takes the audit lock. Walk me through the failure and your fix."
What a strong answer covers
  • Establish the cycle: withdrawal holds account and wants audit; reconciliation holds audit and wants account
  • Reentrancy does not resolve a cycle involving two threads and two locks
  • Commit the account update and capture notification data while locked, then invoke listeners after unlocking
  • Keep the notification snapshot valid independently of later account changes
  • Open calls can reorder notifications; preserve commit order by enqueueing under the commit lock for one consumer, without blocking for queue space
  • Check thread stacks and use controlled scheduling to exercise the cycle
How a senior would say it
I would look for a lock order cycle. The withdrawal thread holds the account lock and calls the listener, which waits for the audit lock. If reconciliation already holds the audit lock and asks for the account balance, it waits for the account lock. Neither thread can release its first lock because neither call returns. A reentrant lock would not solve this: each thread needs a lock owned by the other thread. My fix is an open call. Under the account lock, I complete the update and capture the new balance and a safe listener snapshot. Then I release the lock before invoking any listener. The event must own its data, so a later update cannot change the notification underneath the callback. The tradeoff is that notifications can arrive out of commit order. If order matters, I would enqueue each event in the same critical section that commits its update, and use one consumer for delivery. That enqueue must not block waiting for space while holding the account lock; a full queue needs an explicit policy. To confirm the original diagnosis, I would inspect both thread stacks, then use a controlled test that makes both threads hold their first lock before requesting the second.

Likely follow up: If the event queue is full, how would you reject or defer the update without losing a committed notification?

Out loudabout 75 s
"Each request holds one global lock for 40 microseconds, and arrivals reach 30,000 requests a second. Can adding worker threads fix the latency?"
What a strong answer covers
  • The lock permits at most 25,000 critical sections per second, ignoring overhead
  • Arrival rate times hold time is 1.2, so offered load exceeds capacity
  • More workers cannot parallelize the critical section and can increase the backlog
  • Move independent work outside while preserving the entire invariant inside
  • Partition independent keys only when cross-key invariants allow it; measure contention and bound overload
How a senior would say it
No. The lock is already the capacity limit. Forty microseconds per request means at most twenty-five thousand critical sections per second, even before acquisition overhead. Thirty thousand arrivals per second times forty microseconds gives a utilization demand of one point two. The offered work exceeds what one lock can serve. More workers cannot execute that critical section in parallel; they mostly add waiters and consume resources. With an unbounded backlog, latency keeps growing rather than reaching a stable average. I would first measure lock wait time and hold time, then move parsing, formatting, I/O and callbacks out of the critical section where possible. I would keep the shared-state check and update together so the performance fix does not introduce a race. If hold time cannot shrink enough, independent keys may use different locks, but a cross-key invariant needs its own coordination. Finally, I would bound admission or queueing so overload has a defined outcome. After the change, I would measure tail latency and contention again, aiming for headroom rather than running continuously at the theoretical ceiling.

Likely follow up: If the protected operation touches two keys, what must change before you introduce lock striping?

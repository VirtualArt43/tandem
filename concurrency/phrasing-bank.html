Saying it like a senior

Precise concurrency answers that name the failure, explain the guarantee, and state the limits, with worked code and spoken drills.

Asked atLinkedInGoogleUberAtlassian
Before this:How to run a concurrency coding round

Interviewers grade what they hear. Make your reasoning checkable by naming the race, the guarantee and the cost. These are the sentences, and the lesson behind each one, so you never say a sentence you cannot defend for five follow ups.

In plain words

Two mechanics inspect a bicycle whose chain slips. One says "I will tighten something". The other says "the chain slips under load because these teeth are worn; I will replace the worn parts and test it uphill". The second answer names the evidence, the fix and the check.

A senior answer has the same three parts: name the problem precisely, state the fix and why it works, state the trade-off or the condition under which you would change it. A list of tools such as ConcurrentHashMap, AtomicInteger and synchronized is incomplete until you name the interleaving each tool prevents.

How to use this page

  • Say them aloud, in your own words, until they come out without thinking. Reading them silently does not transfer to a live round.
  • Only say what you can defend. Each sentence links to its lesson; if you cannot answer two follow ups on it, read the lesson first.
  • Fit the sentence to the moment. The "When" column tells you the trigger: a question, a piece of code, or a point in the round.

Closing an answer

To summarize: the shared state is the queue and the closed flag, guarded by one lock; the invariant holds at every unlock; condition waits re-check predicates; shutdown wakes everyone; and invariant checks plus controlled interleavings and stress tests target regressions.
The design assumes overload will happen: every queue is bounded, every dependency has an in-flight cap, excess load fails fast at the front door, and retries are idempotent.

Worked example: defend an admission limit

The contract allows at most 100 admissions during this fixed window, with no releases or reset in this example. At 99, a separate atomic load and atomic increment still fail: both callers can pass the check. This is a logic race even when each individual operation is race-free.

Say the guarantee before the API. The whole admission decision must be atomic. The successful increment while holding the mutex is the decision point; rejection observes 100 while holding that same mutex. This is a linearizability argument, not merely a claim that the field is protected.

The result slots are separate memory locations and are read after both joins. The C++ version uses C++20 jthread for exception-safe ownership; a C++17 joining RAII wrapper can own std::thread. The result is independent of which caller wins. To test the real limiter, add a start gate, more callers, boundary checks, and controlled window rollover with a fake clock. A test timeout catches a hang but does not prove fairness.

Likely follow ups: a CAS loop can implement this single-counter limit, but must re-check the limit after failure. A sliding window also changes timestamps and eviction state, so start with one lock per client's full state. A metrics counter such as LongAdder cannot supply an exact atomic admission decision.

Replace an unsupported guarantee with the mechanism and its limits. The corrections below assume the stated contract; they are not universal performance claims.

In the interview

The research records candidates being asked to explain races and guarantees; it does not establish that phrasing alone decided their outcomes. A Google L5 candidate who listed ConcurrentHashMap, AtomicInteger and synchronized for a per-user counter still received No Hire and concluded that precise reasoning about race conditions and guarantees was expected. A LinkedIn cache screen asked for an example race for each lock; a separate Staff report describes walking through the interleaving where two threads both see 99 of 100. An Uber interviewer complained that candidates "put synchronization everywhere". An Atlassian candidate reported that the interviewers weighed reasoning over a complete solution.

Here is the race this lock prevents: two threads both read 99 of 100, both decide to admit, and we admit 101. With the check and the increment under one lock, the second thread sees 100 and rejects.

Key takeaways

  • A senior sentence names the problem, the mechanism of the fix, and the trade-off or condition.
  • Name the interleaving a tool prevents; never just list tools.
  • Each sentence links to its reasoning; do not say one you cannot defend for two follow ups.
  • For a design answer, close with the state, guard, invariant, waits, shutdown and test; for a narrow question, answer that question first.

Quick check

Answer each question, then read why. Your score is saved for your module test.

The interviewer points at counts.put(word, counts.getOrDefault(word, 0) + 1) on a ConcurrentHashMap and asks if it is thread-safe. Which answer is strongest?
Why. The strong answer names the interleaving (two reads of the same value), the outcome (a lost increment) and the minimal fix with its mechanism. Locking only put leaves the read outside the compound operation; locking both steps would work only if every competing updater followed that protocol; volatile on the reference changes nothing about compound updates.
"In Java, would making hits volatile make hits++ an indivisible update?" Which answer is right?
Why. Java volatile accesses to this field are individually atomic and ordered, but their read-add-write composition is not. Speed is not the reason, and x86 write atomicity does not make an increment indivisible. C++ volatile would not establish inter-thread synchronization at all.
The interviewer notices your code calls the payment service while holding the order lock. What is the best response?
Why. Naming the failure (a slow dependency turns into a queue of blocked threads) and restructuring to keep I/O outside the lock, with version validation afterwards, limits the critical section. That check cannot undo an external payment; idempotency and reconciliation address retries and stale results. Fairness and more threads do not shorten the time the lock is held.
"How do you know your bounded queue works?" Which answer best signals seniority?
Why. It names the method (start gate, many threads), the invariants checked, how hangs are caught, and the tool for data races. A single run proves little; pure reasoning without testing ignores the mistakes everyone makes.
"Can't we just use Kafka's exactly-once and skip idempotency in the consumer that sends emails?" Which reply is accurate?
Why. Kafka transactions are real but scoped: they commit Kafka outputs and consumed offsets atomically. Side effects outside Kafka are not part of the commit. Recording "sent" before sending can lose mail on a crash; recording it afterwards can duplicate mail. A provider that enforces the idempotency key closes that gap. One partition does not change the transaction boundary.
An exact LRU cache updates its shared recency list on every hit. Someone proposes a read-write lock "because gets dominate". Which response identifies the limitation?
Why. Hits in this implementation mutate shared order and cannot safely run under a shared read lock. Misses need not change recency, so the benefit depends on workload. Per-shard LRU or delayed recency updates change exact global LRU semantics. Fairness and a volatile map reference do not protect recency mutations.
In the worked example, both threads finish and are joined before printing. With used initially 99, what can the pair admitted used print?
Why. The lock covers the check and increment together. The first caller admits and changes 99 to 100; the second rejects. Both result slots are observed after joining their writers, so no success is lost. Two admissions cannot pass the locked check, and there is no path for both callers to reject at 99.

Write your own answer first. Then compare with the model answer, or ask Bar Raiser to mark it like an interviewer would.

Explain
Rewrite these three junior answers as senior ones: (1) "I'll just synchronize the methods." (2) "It works, I tested it." (3) "We'll use a Redis lock so only one worker processes the order."
Model answer
  1. "The invariant is that the balance matches the transaction log, so one lock guards both fields, including in the reads; the email call happens after the lock is released so a slow email server can't block deposits."
  2. "I tested it single-threaded for ordering, then with eight threads released by a start gate checking that every item arrives exactly once and capacity is never exceeded, with timed Java joins or a C++ process timeout to catch hangs, and the C++ build under ThreadSanitizer. The interleaving I worried about was two consumers waking for one item, which the while loop handles."
  3. Explain
Give a one-minute answer to "Is this class thread-safe?" for a class whose add(item) does if (!items.contains(item)) items.add(item); on a Collections.synchronizedList, with uniqueness required and non-null items.
Model answer

No. Each list call is synchronized, so the list itself will not be corrupted, but contains followed by add is a check-then-act across two calls: two threads can both see the item missing and both add it, so duplicates appear. The fix is to make the pair atomic: hold the list's own lock around both (synchronized (items) { ... }, since the synchronized wrapper locks on itself), or, if list ordering is not required, use a set (ConcurrentHashMap.newKeySet() with add, which reports whether it inserted). If insertion order matters, keep the list private and make every access follow one locking protocol; synchronizing on the wrapper around both steps is sufficient. The test that would catch it: many threads adding the same small set of items behind a start gate, then checking for duplicates.

Say it out loud

Interviews are spoken. Answer each question out loud as if the interviewer just asked it. Tandem transcribes you on this Mac, word for word, measures your pace, fillers and pauses, and Bar Raiser scores what you said and how you said it.

Out loudabout 90 s
"Two requests both pass a limit of one hundred when the count is ninety-nine. Explain the bug and defend your fix."
What a strong answer covers
  • The admission check and increment are one compound operation; individually atomic accesses are insufficient
  • Give the interleaving where both read 99 and increment to 100 and 101
  • Use one lock around check and update, or a CAS loop that re-checks the limit on failure
  • Name the successful increment or CAS as the admission decision point and preserve the exact limit
  • LongAdder's live sum cannot make an atomic admission decision; window state must share the protocol
  • Test boundary values and concurrent callers with a start gate, bounded test runtime and appropriate race detection
How a senior would say it
The bug is that the check and the increment are separate operations. Even if both use an atomic counter, the admission decision as a whole is not atomic. At ninety-nine, caller A reads the count and passes the check. Caller B reads the same count and also passes. Their atomic increments then take the count to one hundred and one hundred and one, admitting two requests for one remaining slot. I would start with one lock around the check and the increment. The successful increment while holding that lock is the decision point. A later caller sees one hundred under the same lock and rejects. If the state really is only a counter, a compare-and-set loop is another valid design: compute a candidate, try the update, and on failure re-read through the failed comparison and re-check the limit. I would not use LongAdder for admission, because summing and incrementing are not one atomic decision. A sliding window also involves expiration state, which belongs in the same synchronization protocol. I would test zero, one remaining slot, and full capacity, then release many callers behind a start gate and assert exactly one additional admission. I would bound test runtime and use ThreadSanitizer for C++ data races, while separately reasoning about this logic race.

Likely follow up: How does the design change when requests expire from a sliding window while new requests arrive?

Out loudabout 90 s
"We use Kafka transactions. Can the consumer safely retry an email send after a timeout?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Kafka's transaction boundary does not automatically include an external email provider
  • A timeout can mean the email was accepted but its acknowledgement was lost
  • Recording sent before the send risks loss, and recording it after the send risks duplication
  • Require durable provider-side idempotency or a transaction including the effect; otherwise state the weaker guarantee
  • An outbox atomically records intent with database state but its relay can still repeat delivery
  • Test a successful send followed by a lost response and a crash before recording completion
How a senior would say it
Only if the email operation itself supports safe retries; Kafka transactions alone do not provide that guarantee. They can commit Kafka output records and consumed offsets together, but the email provider is outside that transaction. Suppose the provider accepts the email and its response is lost. The consumer times out without knowing whether the send happened. Retrying can send a second email. A local sent flag does not close this gap. If I record sent before making the call, a crash between those steps can lose the email. If I record it afterwards, a crash after sending but before recording can duplicate it. I would use a stable operation key that the provider durably deduplicates, with the same payload and a retention period covering our retries. A transaction that actually includes the effect is another solution where available. If neither exists, I must state the weaker guarantee and choose between possible loss and possible duplicates. An outbox is useful for committing database changes and email intent together, but its relay can still repeat delivery, so the provider boundary remains. I would test successful delivery followed by a lost response, then a process crash before completion is recorded. Those failure windows are the reason for the design, not just unusual edge cases.

Likely follow up: If the provider forgets idempotency keys after a day, what retry and reconciliation policy would you choose?

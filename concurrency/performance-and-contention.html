Performance: contention, Amdahl and profiling

Predict scaling limits, distinguish lock waiting from CPU work, measure contention correctly, and redesign shared state without weakening its guarantees.

Asked atDatabricksMetaRubrik
Before this:Lock granularity, contention and lock stripingCounters at scale: contention, LongAdder and striping

Adding threads makes a program faster only until they start waiting for each other. Senior interviews probe whether you can predict that point with arithmetic, find it with the right profiler, and move it by changing the design rather than the thread count.

In plain words

A restaurant has one cash counter. Hiring more cooks speeds up the cooking, but every order still passes through the one cashier. If a tenth of each order's time is spent at the counter, no number of cooks can make the restaurant more than ten times faster, and long before that the queue at the counter is the whole story.

In a program, the cash counter is your lock, or any other piece of work that only one thread can do at a time. More cooks just make the queue longer.

Amdahl's law: the ceiling

For a fixed job, let s be the fraction of its one-worker execution time that stays serial. Assume the remaining 1 - s divides perfectly across N workers on sufficient processing resources, with no added scheduling or communication cost. Then Amdahl's law says:

speedup(N) = 1 / (s + (1 - s) / N), with an asymptotic ceiling of 1 / s when s is positive. If s is zero, the ideal speedup is N. N means useful parallel capacity, not merely the number of threads created.

Read the 10 percent row slowly: at 32 threads you get under 8 times, a quarter of what you paid for. Amdahl's law describes one job split across threads, where the serial part runs while the other threads wait: a single-threaded setup phase, a final merge, a reduction at the end. In concurrent code the serial part is also time spent holding shared locks, and anything else only one thread can do at a time (a serial writer stage or a serial I/O path). Reducing or parallelizing that serial work raises this model's ceiling; adding threads alone cannot.

Two refinements change the scaling question. Gustafson's law changes the question: for scaled workloads and a serial fraction alpha measured in the parallel run, scaled speedup is N - alpha times (N - 1). It does not speed up the original fixed job.

The Universal Scalability Law models normalized throughput as N / (1 + a times (N - 1) + b times N times (N - 1)); Here a models serialized contention and b models pairwise coordination overhead; a positive b can produce a peak followed by a decline. Fit such a model to measurements rather than treating it as a hardware guarantee.

A lock is a queue with one server

A server is not one job split up; it runs many independent requests, and one request's locked part can overlap with another's unlocked part. The right model there is simpler and just as useful in an interview: a lock can be held by one thread at a time, so its ideal acquisition ceiling is 1 / mean hold time per second, across all threads together. Use elapsed time holding that specific lock, including any blocking inside it, and count every acquisition per request. Handoff and scheduling gaps can lower the achieved rate.

  • Hold time 50 microseconds: at most 20,000 acquisitions per second, on 4 cores or 400.
  • Hold time 5 microseconds, 20 acquisitions per request: at most 200,000 / 20 = 10,000 requests per second, whatever the pool size.

For CPU-only requests with negligible overhead, let C be total CPU time per request and H its total hold time on one lock. Then N cores supply at most N / C requests per second, while the lock supplies at most 1 / H. Relative to the single-core rate 1 / C, the lock ceiling is C / H = 1 / s for s = H / C. Requests can overlap their unlocked work, so do not use the fixed-job Amdahl curve as an exact server-throughput prediction.

Waiting depends on the workload model. Let h be the mean hold time per acquisition. With Poisson arrivals and independent exponential hold times, the M/M/1 model predicts mean queue wait h times rho / (1 - rho), where rho is lock utilization. At rho = 0.8 that is 4h; constant hold times under the M/D/1 assumptions give 2h instead. Bursts, long holders, handoff gaps and finite worker pools change the answer. These are mean waits, not p99 predictions; see queueing math.

Contention costs more than the critical section

An uncontended mutex often uses a short user-space path with atomic operations. The exact instructions and costs depend on the runtime and architecture; neither acquisition nor release has a universal instruction count. Under contention:

  • A waiter may spin, yield or park, depending on the lock. Parking and wakeup can involve kernel scheduling; their cost depends on load, and wakeup does not mean immediate execution.
  • The lock word and modified shared data can move between cores' caches. Coherence latency depends on topology, which cores access the data, and whether the transfer crosses sockets.
  • Handoff can add idle gaps between holders, especially when ownership is reserved for a waiter that is not scheduled yet. Other threads may still do unrelated work. A lock convoy is the extreme case, where throughput falls to the rate of context switches.

So under lock contention, adding threads can make the whole program slower, not just flat. More threads do not remove the shared bottleneck; measure before increasing concurrency.

See it: one hot cache line

Four threads count to a total. In the first run they all increment one shared atomic. In the second, updates are spread out: Java's LongAdder adapts a set of cells, while C++ assigns each thread a padded slot. Adder cells are not permanently owned by individual threads. In the third, each thread counts in a local variable and adds its result once at the end.

After all joins, every version must print the exact expected total: 8,000,000 in Java and 1,200,000 in C++. Timing and relative ranking vary. The shared atomic can become a hot spot because successful updates serialize on the same location. These are demonstrations, not benchmark results: they include startup and joining, omit warmup and start coordination, and the C++ runner adds ThreadSanitizer overhead. The compiler may also replace the local counting loop with a single addition.

The C++ alignment is an explicit illustrative choice, not a portable cache-line-size guarantee. A 64-byte boundary is insufficient on machines with larger coherence lines. Check the target geometry, object size and placement; C++17 defines std::hardware_destructive_interference_size, but availability varies by standard library, including Apple libc++ versions. Padding consumes memory and can worsen cache locality. The demo's std::jthread requires C++20; in C++17 use std::thread and explicit joins.

The read contract matters. LongAdder.sum() is exact after writers finish and join, but is not an atomic snapshot during updates. Reading several atomic slots also does not create one point-in-time snapshot. Local batching delays visibility until publication. These choices suit approximate live metrics, not a strict admission limit or a globally ordered sequence number.

Find contention with the right profiler

The most common profiling mistake in concurrent code: a CPU profile does not account for time a thread spends parked. Blocked and parked threads are off the CPU, so a CPU flame graph of a service that is capped by one hot lock can look perfectly healthy. Spinning waiters do appear on CPU; parked waiters need lock events, wall-clock samples or off-CPU data.

The JDK 21 JFR tools expose these views. The default recording configuration gives jdk.JavaMonitorEnter and jdk.ThreadPark a 20 ms threshold. Short frequent waits need a lower configured threshold or a lock profile. Monitor-enter events measure waiting to acquire, not time holding the lock; parks also include non-lock waits, so inspect stacks and blocker types.

When you have nothing installed, a few thread dumps (reading thread dumps) are a crude wall-clock profile: repeated BLOCKED stacks on the same monitor identify a candidate bottleneck, not a measured Amdahl serial fraction. To measure hold time, instrument immediately after successful acquisition until release, or use suitable owner-side tracing; report both wait and hold distributions.

Benchmark without fooling yourself

A microbenchmark measures one small operation in a loop. It is the easiest measurement to get wrong, because the compiler and the CPU are smarter than the loop:

  • No warmup. The JVM starts by interpreting, then compiles hot code; caches and branch predictors also need time. Early iterations measure the startup, not the code.
  • Dead code elimination. If the result is never used, the compiler may delete the work, and you time an empty loop. Constant inputs can also be folded away at compile time.
  • Too little repetition. One run says nothing about variance. Repeat, fork fresh processes, report a spread.
  • Threads that do not overlap. As in the demo above, threads started in a loop may barely run at the same time; use a start gate or a harness that synchronizes them.
  • Averages without tails. For request latency, report p50, p99 and p99.9 with sample counts and an offered load. A throughput microbenchmark alone does not measure those percentiles.
  • Coordinated omission in latency tests: a load generator that waits for each response before sending the next stops sending during a stall, so it never records the requests that would have queued, and the tail looks far better than users see.
  • The wrong machine or build. A laptop with turbo boost, a debug build, or a ThreadSanitizer build does not predict a server.

JMH for Java and Google Benchmark for C++ help with timing, warmup, repeated measurements and optimization barriers. They cannot choose a realistic workload, preserve your application semantics, or fix coordinated omission automatically. For an externally driven service, schedule arrivals independently of completions and measure from intended arrival to completion; bound outstanding load and report rejected or dropped requests too.

These fragments need their external harness dependencies. Build the named Java class in a JMH project with annotation processing enabled. The C++ fragment uses Google Benchmark's warmup and repetition settings and must link its library. Run optimized builds without sanitizers for performance measurements; use sanitizers separately for correctness.

The benchmarks compare different contracts: atomicIncrement() returns a unique atomic sequence value, while adderIncrement() records a statistic, and the C++ local loop publishes no shared total. Choose a contract before calling one a replacement. Google Benchmark's documentation explicitly cautions that DoNotOptimize does not prevent optimizing the expression that produced the result: a constant expression can still be folded. Check generated code when a result looks implausible.

Lock-free is not automatically faster

A correctly designed lock-free CAS algorithm removes a lock-owner dependency, not contention. A CAS loop alone is not proof of lock freedom: its atomic type, allocation and helper operations also matter. Every successful CAS on a hot variable still needs exclusive ownership of its cache line, so the cores still take turns.

Under heavy contention, failed CAS attempts can add retries and coherence traffic; a good mutex, which lets one thread finish a chunk of work while the others sleep, can win. Dynamic pointer-based lock-free structures may need allocation and safe memory reclamation; fixed-capacity arrays can avoid both. Mutex fast paths are also optimized, so measure the complete operation before choosing.

What lock-free code buys is a progress guarantee: no thread can block the others by being descheduled while holding a lock. This does not promise bounded completion for one thread, eliminate every priority effect or guarantee better p99 latency. A useful design direction is: stop sharing. Striping, per-thread accumulation and batching often scale better when their aggregation and visibility semantics meet the contract.

The fixes, in the order to try them

  1. Do not share. Per-thread or per-request state, merged at the end (the "local, then one add" line).
  2. Shrink the critical section. Move independent I/O, logging, allocation and computation out of the lock. If computation depends on protected state, copy a snapshot and revalidate a version before committing, or keep the required work protected.
  3. Split the lock. One lock per independent piece of state, or lock striping: hash the key to one of N locks or shards.
  4. Read-mostly data: measure a read-write lock against immutable snapshots with safe publication and lifetime management. Copying costs and reader bookkeeping can outweigh the gain. Atomic shared ownership in C++ need not be lock-free; do not promise lock-free readers solely from an API name.
  5. Batch. Take the lock once per batch: group commit is the classic case. Bound batch size and flush delay so throughput gains do not violate latency or durability requirements.
  6. Counters and flags: atomics, or LongAdder and per-thread slots for counters many threads hit.
  7. Funnel to one owner. A single writer owns the state, but queue synchronization, one owner's capacity and an extra hop remain. Bound its queue and define overload behaviour.

Worked example: why throughput stopped at 6 cores

Consider a hypothetical service whose requests each use about 1 ms of CPU. It scales from 1 to 6 cores, then flattens at about 6,000 requests per second on a 32-core box. CPU is only about 20 percent busy. A wall-clock profile (async-profiler -e wall) shows most request threads waiting in MetricsRegistry.record, a synchronized method. Separate instrumentation inside the synchronized body measures about 8 microseconds of hold time per call, and each request records 20 metrics.

The arithmetic. One lock with an 8 microsecond hold time allows at most 1 / 0.000008 = 125,000 acquisitions per second. At 20 per request, that is 125,000 / 20 = 6,250 requests per second, the ceiling we hit, regardless of cores. Seen the other way: each request spends 160 microseconds of its 1 ms under the lock, a serial fraction of 0.16, and 1 / 0.16 is about 6 cores' worth. The low CPU fits: threads are waiting, not working.

The fix. The metrics are independent counters and histograms that happen to share one lock. If approximate live reads are acceptable, give each counter a LongAdder (or transfer ownership of per-thread histogram buffers before merging), so counter recording no longer takes the global metrics lock. Then re-measure: the ceiling should move to the next bottleneck, and you name that one too.

Common mistakes

  • "It is slow, add threads." If the bottleneck is a lock, more threads lengthen the queue and can lower throughput.
  • Profiling contention with a CPU profiler. Parked time is missing; correlate CPU work, lock events and wall-clock or off-CPU profiles.
  • Timing a loop with System.nanoTime() once and trusting the number. Use JMH or Google Benchmark, with warmup and repetition.
  • Switching to lock-free for speed without measuring. Remove the sharing first.
  • Reporting only averages. Rare long holders can dominate p99 while changing the mean little; measure both under a stated load.
  • Fixing the wrong layer. If the database allows 50 concurrent queries, no in-process cleverness gets you past 50 divided by query time.

In the interview

It comes up as "how do you know multithreading will help here?" (a Databricks report lists that follow up before a candidate parallelized a crawler), "how would you improve performance in a multithreaded environment?" (a Rubrik follow up), or inside a single machine design: one Meta candidate designing a memcache for a box with many cores was praised for sharding the hash table, roughly one shard per core, so locks contend less.

Before adding threads I would ask what the serial part is. If the work is I/O bound and independent, threads help until the downstream saturates. For a fixed CPU job I use Amdahl with its one-worker serial fraction. For a service I separately calculate cores divided by CPU time per request, and one divided by total hold time on the bottleneck lock per request. So I would shrink or split that critical section first: shard by key, keep I/O out of the lock, batch updates. And I would measure with a lock or wall-clock profiler, because a CPU profile omits parked waiting time.

Follow ups: "What speedup do 16 cores give with 5 percent serial work?" (about 9). "Why might throughput drop when you add threads?" (contention and coherence costs grow with the number of threads, the Universal Scalability Law). "Is a lock-free queue faster?" (not necessarily; it removes blocking, not contention).

Senior depth

Measure the serial fraction instead of guessing it. Run the same job at 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 threads and compute the Karp-Flatt metric from each measured speedup S on p threads: e = (1 / S - 1 / p) / (1 - 1 / p). Use p greater than 1. A measured speedup of 6.4 on 16 threads gives e = 0.1, a 10 percent effective serial fraction including overhead. A roughly constant e is consistent with Amdahl-like scaling, but is not proof of a particular lock or phase. A rising e suggests increasing overhead or resource limits such as contention or memory bandwidth; profiles distinguish them.

Hardware effects that look like contention. False sharing (independent variables on one cache line, see CPU caches and false sharing), NUMA (memory attached to another socket costs more to reach), and hyperthreads (two logical cores sharing one physical core's units) all bend scaling curves without any lock in sight. Combine supported hardware counters with topology-aware runs and memory-bandwidth measurements. perf c2c can locate contended lines; it does not by itself diagnose all these effects.

Key takeaways

  • For fixed work under Amdahl's assumptions, speedup is 1 / (s + (1 - s) / N), with ceiling 1 / s for positive s.
  • For a server, compare CPU capacity with 1 / total mean hold time per request on each hot lock. Queueing estimates need stated arrival and service assumptions.
  • Contention costs more than the critical section: parking, wakeups and cache line transfers. Throughput can fall as threads are added.
  • CPU profiles show spinning and retries; use lock events or off-CPU data for parked waits, and measure hold time separately.
  • Benchmark with JMH or Google Benchmark; the best fix is usually to stop sharing, not to go lock-free.

Quick check

Answer each question, then read why. Your score is saved for your module test.

A fixed job spends 5 percent of its one-worker time in serial work. Assuming ideal parallelism on 16 cores with no overhead, roughly what speedup do 16 workers give, and what is the most any number of threads can give?
Why. 1 / (0.05 + 0.95 / 16) is about 9.1, and 1 / 0.05 is 20. Even a small serial fraction costs a lot at 16 threads, which is why shrinking critical sections matters more than adding cores.
Every request takes a global lock once and holds it for 40 microseconds. What upper bound on requests per second does that lock impose, regardless of the number of cores?
Why. One lock serves one holder at a time, so it allows at most 1 / 0.00004 = 25,000 acquisitions per second in total. Cores do not change that; only a shorter hold time, or splitting the lock, does. Actual throughput can be lower because of CPU limits, handoff gaps or other bottlenecks; waiting depends on arrival patterns and hold-time variance.
A service's throughput is flat and its p99 is bad. A CPU flame graph shows nothing unusual and the CPUs are 30 percent busy. What should you look at next?
Why. A CPU profiler samples threads that are on a core. Threads blocked on a lock or parked on a pool are off the CPU, so parked waiting time is missing there. Spinning and CAS retries do consume CPU and can appear in the profile. Low CPU plus flat throughput is the classic signature of waiting: profile the waits (async-profiler -e lock or -e wall, JFR lock events, off-CPU profiling in C++).
A team replaces a mutex-protected counter with a correct increment CAS loop on one shared lock-free atomic, expecting a big win under 64 threads. Which statement is justified without benchmarking this change?
Why. The implementation and workload determine the winner. CAS is atomic, so a correct increment loop does not lose updates, but retries still cost time. A simple lock-free counter loop has no lock cycle; that does not prove arbitrary CAS-based code cannot deadlock or starve. Striping may help when relaxed live reads are acceptable.
In the runnable counter demo, all worker joins complete normally. Which statements about its printed results are guaranteed by the code, assuming the stated iteration counts? Select all that apply.Select all that apply
Why. Four workers perform the stated number of increments, and joins complete before each result is read. The totals are therefore exact. LongAdder.sum() lacks an atomic snapshot during concurrent updates, but here no writer remains. Startup, scheduling, JIT compilation, optimization and sanitizer overhead can change or tie the elapsed times, so no speed ranking is guaranteed.
A profile shows threads queued on one global lock that updates 20 independent metric counters. The report permits a non-atomic live snapshot. Which change most directly removes serialization on that global lock?
Why. The stated reporting contract permits distributing writes and summing later. This removes the global lock, though hot cells and aggregation still cost work. More threads, fair admission and spinning all retain one shared serialization point; fairness does not automatically cause a convoy.
C++ onlyIn Google Benchmark, what does benchmark::DoNotOptimize(x) do?
Why. DoNotOptimize makes a value observable to the benchmark barrier; it does not preserve every instruction that produced that value. It neither selects -O0, disables caches nor makes accesses atomic. ClobberMemory addresses compiler-visible memory effects; neither is an inter-thread synchronization primitive.

Checkpoint

Write your own answer first. Then compare with the model answer, or ask Bar Raiser to mark it like an interviewer would.

Numbers
Assume ideal scaling, enough concurrent requests, CPU-only hold times and negligible overhead. (a) A nightly batch job takes 100 s on one core, and 5 s of that is a single-threaded merge at the end. How long does it take on 16 cores, and what is the fastest it can ever run? (b) A request handler does 2 ms of CPU work, of which 0.1 ms is inside one global lock, on a 16-core server. What caps its throughput first, the CPUs or the lock? (c) The business wants 12,000 requests per second from that server. What do you tell them?
Model answer

(a) This is one job split across threads, so Amdahl's law applies with s = 5 / 100 = 0.05: time is 5 + 95 / 16, about 10.9 s, a speedup of about 9.1. With that serial work unchanged, runtime approaches 5 s as parallel capacity grows, a speedup ceiling of 20. To go faster we must reduce or parallelize the merge.

(b) Two ceilings. The CPUs: 16 cores / 2 ms per request = 8,000 requests per second. The lock: held 0.1 ms per request, so at most 1 / 0.0001 = 10,000 per second. CPU capacity is the tighter ideal ceiling, 8,000. At that rate lock utilization is 80 percent; actual waits and p99 depend on arrival patterns and hold-time variance, so 8,000 is an upper bound, not a guaranteed achieved rate.

(c) 12,000 is impossible in one process with the same global lock and hold time however many cores we add, because the lock alone caps us at 10,000 (one over the 5 percent serial fraction, 20 cores' worth). I would shrink or remove the global lock first (shard the state by key, or replace the shared update with an atomic or a per-thread aggregate), then reduce CPU cost or provide at least 24 cores for 24 CPU-seconds of work per second, plus headroom. More instances help only if state can be partitioned without reintroducing one shared bottleneck. Re-measure throughput and latency under load.

Design
A shared in-memory map from user id to request count is updated on every request by 64 threads and read once a minute by a reporting job that permits a non-atomic snapshot; keys are not removed or reset during updates. Profiling shows threads queued on the map's single lock. Redesign it and say why each choice helps.
Model answer

The access pattern is write heavy with independent keys and a rare reader that tolerates slightly stale data, so one global lock imposes unnecessary serialization between independent keys.

First choice: a concurrent map whose values are themselves cheap concurrent counters. In Java, ConcurrentHashMap<String, LongAdder> with computeIfAbsent(id, k -> new LongAdder()).increment(): updates to existing keys avoid one global lock, though colliding bins, insertion and resizing can contend, and a hot key spreads its increments over the adder's cells. In C++, a fixed array of N shards, each a mutex plus an unordered_map, chosen by hashing the user id, so 64 threads spread over N locks; for very hot keys, per-thread maps reduce sharing, but the reporter must obtain ownership of frozen buffers through a synchronized handoff before reading them. Reading an ordinary map while its owner mutates it is unsafe, and is a data race in C++.

The reporting job avoids a global pause (LongAdder.sum() per key, or locking one shard at a time and briefly blocking that shard's writers), which meets the stated non-atomic reporting contract. If the report needed one exact point-in-time snapshot, independent shard reads or map swaps would not suffice. I would coordinate all writers through a barrier or epoch protocol, or acquire all shard locks in a fixed order while copying. That strengthens semantics at a latency cost. Then I would re-profile to check that global-lock waiting has fallen and check p99, not just throughput.

"Throughput stops growing after six cores, but the machine has thirty-two cores. What would you check?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Compare fixed-work or offered-load measurements and inspect CPU utilization and latency
  • Use CPU profiles for running work and retries, and lock or off-CPU data for parked waits
  • Measure lock hold time separately from acquisition wait time
  • Calculate the per-request lock ceiling and compare it with CPU capacity
  • Change the measured bottleneck while preserving semantics, then repeat the load test
How a senior would say it
I would first establish what limits useful work before adding threads. I would repeat the same workload at several concurrency levels and record throughput, CPU utilization, queue depth and tail latency. Low CPU with flat throughput suggests waiting, although that wait might be a lock, a dependency or a pool limit. A CPU profile shows running work, including spinning and failed retries. For parked threads I would add lock events or a wall-clock or off-CPU profile, then identify the shared resource and its owner. I would measure hold time separately: time waiting to acquire a lock is not time holding it. Suppose each request takes one metrics lock twenty times and each hold averages eight microseconds. That is one hundred and sixty microseconds of serialized work per request, giving an ideal ceiling of six thousand two hundred and fifty requests per second. I would compare that with cores divided by CPU time per request. If the metrics lock is the measured bottleneck and live totals may be approximate, I would distribute counter updates or hand off local batches safely. Then I would rerun the same load test and check both throughput and p99. The next bottleneck may be bandwidth, CPU or a downstream service, so the measurement must continue.

Likely follow up: Would a lock profile showing eight microseconds of waiting justify that throughput calculation?

Out loudabout 75 s
"Why not replace every contended counter with a striped counter? How would you decide whether it is an improvement?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Choose according to the read and update contract before comparing speed
  • Explain how striping reduces writes to one shared cache line
  • State that LongAdder sums and multiple slot reads are not atomic live snapshots
  • Distinguish metrics from sequence generation, admission limits and exact snapshots
  • Benchmark the required contract under representative contention and verify correctness separately
How a senior would say it
I would use striping when the read contract permits aggregation. It spreads updates across cells or per-thread slots, reducing the need for every writer to take ownership of one shared cache line. That is attractive for request metrics, but it changes what a read means. A LongAdder sum during updates is not an atomic snapshot, and reading several C++ atomic slots separately does not make the combined result atomic. After writers stop and join, the final total can be exact. A unique sequence number or a strict admission limit needs a single atomic decision, so an adder is not a replacement. An exact snapshot across shards needs extra coordination. To compare performance I would benchmark the contract the application actually requires, including reads, merges and realistic hot-key skew. I would use JMH or Google Benchmark with warmup, repeated measurements and optimized builds, then measure service latency under representative load. I would check correctness separately under stress and sanitizers. A faster update loop is useful only if the complete design still delivers the required result.

Likely follow up: How would you obtain an exact snapshot from per-thread maps without reading them while their owners mutate them?

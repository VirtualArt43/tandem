Livelock, starvation and fairness

Diagnose livelock and starvation, break retry symmetry, and explain the guarantees and costs of fair admission, bounded waiting and aging.

Asked atRubrikLinkedInConfluent
Before this:Deadlock: the four conditions and how to break eachtryLock, timeouts, interruptible locking and fairness

A system can look alive, with busy CPUs and active threads, while some or all of its work never finishes. You need to distinguish livelock from starvation and defend a fairness policy, as Rubrik's reported playground and bathroom questions require.

In plain words

Two people meet in a narrow corridor. Both step left to let the other pass, then both step right, then both left again. Nobody is stuck in the deadlock sense; they are both moving, politely, and nobody gets anywhere. That is livelock.

Now picture a bus stop with no queue. When a bus arrives, whoever is closest to the door and quickest gets on. A shy passenger who arrived first may watch ten buses leave without them, even though buses keep coming and people keep travelling. That is starvation: the system makes progress, just never for you.

Safety and liveness

Two useful kinds of concurrency property are safety and liveness; a specification can require both. Safety says nothing bad ever happens: no two threads in the critical section, no lost update. Liveness says something good eventually happens: every request eventually completes. Races break safety. Deadlock, livelock and starvation break liveness.

The critical section problem has three familiar requirements: mutual exclusion (only one thread inside), progress (entry is not postponed indefinitely when somebody wants to enter), and bounded waiting (after a thread asks to enter, only a bounded number of others may enter before it). Bounded waiting is stronger than starvation freedom: eventual entry alone gives no uniform bound on overtaking.

Progress claims assume holders finish and eligible threads get scheduled; admission order alone promises no deadline in milliseconds. Peterson's classic two-thread proof assumes sequentially consistent shared operations, so copying it with plain fields into Java or C++ is not correct.

CPU and thread states are clues, not proofs. Other threads can keep a deadlocked service busy. Compare repeated stacks, ownership dependencies, retry counts and completed operations; distinguish a loop that changes state uselessly from a thread waiting for an owner that can never run.

Livelock: busy, polite, and stuck

A livelock is a set of threads that keep reacting to each other without any of them making progress. The cleanest example is two diners sharing one spoon, each too polite to eat while the other is hungry. Both stay hungry forever, handing the spoon back and forth. The demo stops after 20,000 hand-overs so it can finish; the real thing would not.

Both threads repeatedly execute without eating in the polite run. Atomics make the shared state race-free, but do not make the protocol live; onSpinWait() and yield() provide no fairness guarantee. The fix is to break the symmetry: the lower id eats first when both are hungry, then passes the spoon.

Each diner eats only once here, so both finish. Repeatedly favouring a fixed id across unlimited rounds could starve others; removing livelock does not automatically establish individual fairness.

Livelock in the wild

  • Lock retries in lockstep. The take one, try the other, release and retry scheme from the deadlock lesson can repeat forever if two threads retry on the same schedule.
  • Optimistic updates that keep aborting each other. An abort policy can repeatedly roll back both transactions. A conventional lock-free CAS update loop is different: one caller may starve while others successfully update the value. System-wide livelock would contradict its lock-free progress guarantee under the stated execution assumptions.
  • Retry storms between services. Clients time out and retry, the extra load makes the service slower, more requests time out, and the system stays saturated doing work nobody will use. That is a retry storm, covered in thundering herd.
  • The classic fix comes from networking. Early Ethernet stations that collided on the wire waited a random number of slots, doubling the range after each collision: randomized binary exponential backoff.

Backoff with jitter

Exponential backoff with jitter spreads retries out in time: after each failure, double the maximum delay up to a cap, and wait a random time below it. The AWS comparison of retry strategies calls the simplest one "full jitter": wait a uniformly random time between zero and the current bound. Without the randomness, everyone who failed together retries together.

Release every lock held by the failed attempt before sleeping. This delay reduces retry collisions; it is not synchronization or a starvation-freedom proof. Cap retries and use an overall deadline so a lost cause fails instead of retrying forever.

Prefer a deterministic rule when one fits: consistent blocking lock order removes this symmetric retry cycle, though it does not make admission fair. For remote retries, also require idempotency or deduplication because a timeout does not prove that the operation failed.

Starvation: progress for everyone except you

Starvation means a pending operation keeps losing access to CPU time or a resource indefinitely while other work progresses. The victim may be blocked, runnable or actively retrying. Its common causes:

  • Barging locks. When a lock is released, a thread that is already running can grab it before the waiter that was just woken gets scheduled. That is barging, and it is permitted by synchronized, default ReentrantLock and std::mutex; none promises FIFO admission. A thread that releases and immediately re-locks in a loop can win thousands of times in a row while another thread waits.
  • Greedy readers. A read-write lock that admits new readers while a writer waits lets a steady stream of overlapping readers keep the writer out forever: writer starvation. The C++ standard does not specify a reader or writer preference for std::shared_mutex; library and OS choices differ. Java's non-fair ReentrantReadWriteLock explicitly permits indefinite postponement of readers or writers; do not mistake an implementation heuristic for a fairness guarantee.
  • Priorities without limits. In a priority queue of tasks, low priority work never runs while high priority work keeps arriving. The same happens with OS thread priorities.
  • Retry losers. Lock-free algorithms guarantee system-wide progress when threads keep taking steps, not completion for each caller: one unlucky thread can lose its CAS again and again. Only wait-free algorithms bound each thread's steps (see progress guarantees).
  • Shared pools. One tenant can monopolize workers or admission. A finite burst followed by eventual service is a long delay, not proof of starvation; an unbounded monopoly can prevent other work from ever running.
  • notify() picks arbitrarily. Java does not say which waiter notify wakes, so an unlucky waiter can be passed over repeatedly.

Starvation hides in averages. The median looks fine and the 99.9th percentile, or the maximum wait for one tenant, is terrible. Measure per thread, per tenant and per request type before believing a mean.

Here is priority starvation in a tiny deterministic scheduler: every tick one task runs and a new urgent request arrives. A low priority report that arrived first never runs under strict priority. With aging, it does.

The trick in the aging comparator is that the key, arrival time minus a bonus per priority level, is fixed when the task is queued. Urgent work still jumps ahead, but only by its bonus (here 5 levels times 20 ticks), so arrivals more than 100 ticks later cannot overtake a priority-zero task when priorities are restricted to 0 through 5. Existing backlog can still delay it beyond 100 ticks. The demo runs the report at tick 100 because all earlier urgent tasks have already finished and the tie favours the report's earlier arrival.

This fixed key is equivalent to ranking by priority + (now - arrival) / 20.0, because the common now term cancels. No heap update is needed. Other aging policies that change relative order need explicit reordering, not a comparator that silently changes beneath a heap. Eventual service assumes bounded priorities, finitely many arrivals in each finite interval, continuing dispatch and finite task runtimes.

Fairness tools

  1. Fair locks. new ReentrantLock(true) favours the longest waiter; fair ReentrantReadWriteLock uses approximate arrival order and can admit a group of readers together. new Semaphore(n, true) orders permit requests at internal queueing points, not by method-call timestamps. This is fair admission, not fair CPU scheduling or a wall-clock bound. Untimed tryLock() and semaphore tryAcquire() may barge even in fair mode; timed variants respect fairness. A sleeping next waiter can reduce throughput, as lock convoys explains.
  2. FIFO by construction: a ticket lock. Each thread takes the next ticket number and waits until the "now serving" counter reaches it, so a thread waits only for earlier assigned tickets to finish. C++'s std::mutex promises no fairness. This teaching lock enforces ticket order after ticket assignment:

    These are non-reentrant teaching locks: only the owner may unlock, every acquisition must be released with finally or std::lock_guard<TicketLock>, and the object must outlive its users. They omit ownership checks. The internal monitor or mutex publishes the previous owner's writes to the next owner, even though it is not held throughout the user's critical section.

    FIFO begins when the ticket is assigned, and a descheduled next ticket holder stalls everyone. The internal mutex must eventually admit both new callers and awakened waiters; the ticket policy cannot establish that from the standard mutex contract. Also assume holders finish, waiters get scheduled, and fewer tickets are outstanding than the counter's wraparound range.

    Note the Java version's interrupt handling: a thread may not walk away from its ticket, or every later ticket waits forever, so it keeps waiting and restores the interrupt flag afterwards. Supporting timeouts and cancellation needs more machinery, such as an explicit cancellable waiter queue. Waking every waiter on release is a small thundering herd; a condition per waiter can signal just the next one, which must still reacquire the internal lock and check its predicate.

  3. Writer preference for read-write locks. Once a writer is waiting, new readers queue behind it. Continuous writers can then starve readers, and individual writers still need fair admission. To protect both sides, serve arrivals in order, grouping consecutive readers, or use bounded alternating phases. You build these in build it yourself: read-write lock and compare policies in readers and writers.
  4. Turns and batch limits for groups. When groups exclude each other (the bathroom and playground problems), let the current group admit at most K more members once another group is waiting, then drain and switch turns. Use FIFO within each group as well; fairness between groups alone does not protect individual members.
  5. Aging for priority schemes, as above.
  6. Per-tenant queues served round robin or by weighted fair queueing, plus per-tenant concurrency caps, so one tenant's burst cannot fill a shared pool (see bulkheads).

Worked example: Rubrik's playground, and several ways to stop starvation

Rubrik asked: a playground admits players from one team at a time, at most 10 players inside; players from many teams arrive concurrently. The follow up was several different ways to prevent starvation of any player or team. Walk through the options and what each costs.

  1. The naive rule: admit a player if the playground is empty, or holds their own team and has room. The current team can hold the playground forever: as long as one teammate is inside, new teammates keep entering, and other teams starve.
  2. Strict FIFO admission: arrivals join one queue, and only the head may enter, when it is compatible. Later arrivals cannot overtake a queued player. There are finitely many players ahead, so entry follows if each visit finishes and eligible threads run; a time bound additionally needs bounds on visit and scheduling delays. The cost is concurrency: a waiting player from team B at the head blocks team A players behind it even when there is room and A is inside.
  3. Turns with a batch limit: the current team may keep admitting players, but once another team is waiting, it may admit at most K more; then admissions stop, the playground drains, and the turn passes to the team that has waited longest. For two teams this limits further opposing admissions to K before the current team drains. With more teams, count every turn ahead, its batch and its drain; FIFO within a team is also required for individual fairness.
  4. Time slices: a team's turn lasts at most T seconds while others wait. Stop new entries at the end, let current players leave, then choose the oldest waiting team. This limits admissions, not visit duration; without a maximum stay it supplies no hard time bound.

For two teams, if C players are inside when the other team queues, a positive batch limit K allows at most C + K further departures before the next team's turn. Here C is at most 10. This counts admissions and departures, not seconds; converting it to elapsed time needs bounded visit durations and bounded scheduling gaps. A particular player may also have teammates ahead of them.

The senior move is to name the trade-off out loud: FIFO bounds overtaking per arrival; team batching may improve utilization but needs a separate bound for every turn and every player ahead. The fair bathroom problem drills exactly this.

Common mistakes

  • Retrying in lockstep. Release-and-retry without random backoff, or with a fixed delay, invites livelock.
  • Making every lock fair "to be safe". Throughput can fall under contention. Use fairness where controlling overtaking matters, and measure it.
  • Assuming locks or notify are FIFO. synchronized, default ReentrantLock and std::mutex make no such promise.
  • A reader-preferring read-write lock in front of data that must be updated. The update may never happen under heavy read load.
  • Strict priority without aging in a system that is ever busy.
  • Fixing starvation with sleep or yield in the greedy thread. It changes the odds on one machine, not the guarantee.
  • Measuring only averages. Starvation lives in the maximum and the far tail, per tenant.

In the interview

The question "what is the difference between deadlock, livelock and starvation?" is a staple of phone screens and online assessments, and a LinkedIn senior report lists deadlocks, starvation and race conditions as the follow ups. Rubrik goes further and asks for fairness in code: the most eligible person enters the bathroom, no team starves at the playground, and the searchers, inserters and deleters constraints. Preventing deleter starvation is an additional practice follow up, not an independently verified interview requirement.

Deadlock means dependencies prevent the participants from progressing. Livelock means they keep reacting without completing useful work. Starvation means other work completes while one request never gets its turn. CPU usage helps triage, but cannot prove which one I have. I break retry symmetry with ordering or jitter, and control overtaking with FIFO admission, bounded batches or aging. I state the scheduling and finite-work assumptions behind any fairness claim, and measure throughput as well as waiting time.

Follow ups: "is a fair lock always better?", "how does your read-write lock avoid starving writers?", "why does full jitter work better than a fixed retry delay?", "what does lock-free guarantee about starvation?", and for the group problems, "can a team be starved? prove the bound".

Key takeaways

  • Safety: nothing bad happens. Liveness: something good eventually happens. Deadlock, livelock and starvation are liveness failures.
  • Livelock: busy threads, no progress. Break the symmetry with a rule or with randomized, capped exponential backoff.
  • Starvation: the system progresses, one party never does. Causes: barging locks, greedy readers, unbounded priorities, retry losers, shared pools.
  • Control overtaking with fair admission, bounded team turns, aging and per-tenant queues. Writer preference alone can starve readers.
  • Fairness can cost throughput and does not guarantee a deadline. State the assumptions and measure per-request tails as well as aggregate throughput.

Quick check

Answer each question, then read why. Your score is saved for your module test.

A service is stuck: throughput is zero, CPU is at 100 percent on four cores, and thread dumps show the same threads RUNNABLE inside retry loops in every dump. What is the most likely diagnosis?
Why. Repeated retries with no completions make livelock the strongest hypothesis. A blocking deadlock or lost wakeup usually leaves participants asleep, and starvation of one caller allows others to finish. CPU alone is not proof: inspect dependencies to exclude a spinlock deadlock or an impossible predicate.
Two threads use "lock one, try the other, release the held lock on failure and retry". They retry after exactly 10 ms every time, and under load they keep colliding. What fixes it best?
Why. A fixed delay can preserve lockstep, whether it is 10 ms or 1 ms. Immediate retries can do the same. Randomness breaks the symmetry, and the growing bound handles heavier contention; blocking acquisition in a global lock order removes this retry cycle. Fair locks do not help, because each thread fails on the lock the other already holds.
A read-write lock admits new readers whenever no writer holds the lock. Readers arrive constantly and their read sections overlap. What happens to a writer?
Why. Reader preference with overlapping readers means the read count never drops to zero, so the writer starves. The fix is writer preference (once a writer waits, new readers queue behind it) or alternating batches. Nothing in a plain read-write lock times out on the writer's behalf.
In the scheduler demo, one priority-5 request arrives and one task runs each tick. The priority-0 report arrived at tick 0. With key arrival - 20 * priority, smaller first, and earlier arrival breaking ties, what does the aging run print?
Why. The report's key is 0. Requests arriving at ticks 0 through 99 have negative keys and run first. At tick 100 the new request also has key 0, but the report wins the arrival tie. Tick 0 ignores the priority bonus, tick 101 ignores the tie-breaker, and indefinite postponement describes the strict-priority run. This exact tick depends on the demo's one-task-per-tick workload; aging does not promise it for arbitrary backlogs.
What does it mean that an algorithm is lock-free but not wait-free?
Why. Lock-free promises system-wide completion, not a per-caller step bound. Wait-free adds that bound on each operation's own steps, not elapsed time. CAS is compatible with either property; a failed weak CAS can even be spurious, so failure alone does not prove another operation completed. The progress guarantees exclude a system-wide deadlock under their execution assumptions.
Java onlyA ReentrantLock is constructed with new ReentrantLock(true). A thread calls tryLock() with no timeout while other threads are queued. What happens if the lock happens to be free at that instant?
Why. The documentation is explicit: the untimed tryLock() does not honor the fairness setting. tryLock(0, TimeUnit.SECONDS) does honor it. The timed form also detects interruption, so the caller must handle or propagate InterruptedException. The untimed form neither queues nor throws merely because waiters exist.
C++ onlyWhat does the C++ standard promise about the order in which threads blocked on a std::mutex acquire it?
Why. The standard specifies mutual exclusion and synchronization, not fairness. FIFO, highest-priority-first and newest-first are all outside its contract. Explicit tickets can order admission after ticket assignment, but the scheduler and the internal mutex still need progress assumptions.

Checkpoint

Write your own answer first. Then compare with the model answer, or ask Bar Raiser to mark it like an interviewer would.

Explain
Explain deadlock, livelock and starvation to an interviewer: what each looks like in production, and how you fix each.
Model answer

All three violate progress requirements even when shared data remains consistent. For deadlock I reconstruct dependencies, such as a cycle of owned locks, and remove a dependency with global lock ordering. Blocking participants often consume little CPU, but a spinlock cycle need not. For livelock I look for retries or rollbacks increasing while completions stay flat, and break symmetry with ordering or randomized capped backoff plus a retry budget. For starvation I track one request or tenant while others complete, then control overtaking with FIFO, bounded batches or aging. Writer preference protects writers from reader floods but can starve readers. Repeated dumps, ownership information and per-request wait metrics matter more than CPU alone; Java deadlock detection covers supported monitor and ownable-synchronizer cycles, not every possible dependency. A fairness argument must state that holders finish and eligible threads get scheduled.

Design
One thread pool of 32 workers serves 50 tenants. Whenever a large tenant runs a batch import, every other tenant's p99 latency explodes, although their own traffic has not changed. Design a fix.
Model answer

This suggests interference through a shared pool, not yet proof of indefinite starvation. I would measure queue delay and active workers per tenant. Use per-tenant bounded queues and dispatch round robin or by weights among tenants that have available concurrency capacity. Reserve the slot before submitting work: if workers instead block on a tenant semaphore, all 32 workers can still be occupied by that tenant. An illustrative cap of 8 leaves room for others, but I would tune it to demand and service times. Isolate batch imports in a smaller pool if their runtimes differ greatly. Aging gives imports eventual opportunities; interactive requests cannot always win under a permanent flood if imports must finish too. Enforce deadlines and load shedding for overload, and measure per-tenant completions and oldest pending request age. Fair selection does not preempt tasks already running or guarantee latency when task durations are unbounded.

Out loudabout 75 s
"The service is busy but barely finishing requests. How would you distinguish deadlock, livelock and starvation?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Distinguish blocked dependencies, useless repeated reactions and one request losing while others finish
  • Use repeated stacks, ownership, retry counts and completion counts rather than CPU alone
  • Give a concrete symmetric retry sequence for livelock
  • Choose ordering or jitter with a retry budget, without promising success from randomness
  • Measure individual wait age and state fairness assumptions
How a senior would say it
I would classify the failure by progress and dependencies, not CPU alone. Deadlock means participants cannot resolve their dependencies. Livelock means they keep reacting but complete no useful work. Starvation means other work finishes while one request keeps losing. I would compare several thread dumps with lock ownership, retry counters and completion counters. A blocking deadlock often uses little CPU, but a spinlock cycle can keep a core busy, and backoff can make livelock look quiet. For example, two threads each take one lock, fail to take the other, release and retry together. A consistent blocking lock order removes that retry cycle. Randomized backoff reduces repeated collisions, but I would release held locks first and cap retries with a deadline; randomness is not a success guarantee. If only one tenant is stuck, I would inspect admission order and oldest pending request age. FIFO or bounded batches can prevent overtaking, assuming holders finish and eligible threads run. I would verify individual completions as well as aggregate throughput before calling the issue fixed.

Likely follow up: Could a lock-free algorithm still starve one request?

Out loudabout 90 s
"Your playground serves one team at a time. Prove that nobody starves, then tell me what fairness costs."
What a strong answer covers
  • Separate team fairness from fairness for each player
  • Use FIFO team turns, FIFO within teams and bounded admissions when another team waits
  • Explain the drain and finite-predecessor argument
  • Distinguish an admission bound from a time bound and state scheduling assumptions
  • Describe batching versus strict FIFO and the sleeping-next-waiter cost
  • Handle cancellation without leaving a blocking queue position
How a senior would say it
I need fairness both between teams and within each team. Rotating teams alone does not stop new teammates from repeatedly passing one older player. I would keep FIFO queues of players, queue each waiting team once, and admit eligible queue heads whenever capacity remains. I would choose a positive batch limit K. When another team is waiting, the current team gets at most K further admissions, then admissions stop until everyone inside leaves. The next waiting team takes its turn. A team that still has players queues again behind other waiting teams. For a particular player, there are finitely many players ahead in their own queue and finitely many turns ahead of each service opportunity. Later arrivals cannot bypass those positions. Assuming every visit finishes and eligible threads get CPU time, that player eventually enters. This bounds overtaking, but a time bound also needs limits on visit duration and scheduling delay. Strict global FIFO is easier to reason about but can leave spare capacity unused when the head belongs to another team. Bounded batches can improve utilization, while a descheduled next player can still stall admission. I would remove cancelled waiters under the queue lock and advance the turn when necessary. The final check is per-player waiting age and completions under continuous competing arrivals, alongside throughput.

Likely follow up: What changes if one player is allowed to stay inside forever?

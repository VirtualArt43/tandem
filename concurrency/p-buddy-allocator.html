Thread-safe buddy allocator

Thread-safe structuressenior45 minRubrik system coding phone screen (alloc and free over a large arena, steered to a buddy allocator)Rubrik

Rubrik's system coding rounds are not only about threads: a senior candidate was asked for alloc(size) and free(index) over a 16 GB region and steered toward a buddy allocator. It is a beautiful little structure: sizes are powers of two, a block's partner is one XOR away, and freeing merges partners back into bigger blocks. Then you make it safe for many threads.

The task

Manage an arena of 2^order units (think of a unit as a page), at offsets 0 to 2^order - 1, all free at the start. The caller guarantees 0 <= order <= 30; behaviour outside that range is not tested. Track allocation metadata and return integer offsets; you do not need to reserve the arena as a byte array.

  • alloc(size) rounds size up to the smallest power of two at least as large, finds a free block of exactly that size, and returns its offset. Blocks are aligned: a block of size s starts at a multiple of s. If no block can be found, or size is below 1 or larger than the arena, return -1 without changing the state. An exact arena-sized request is valid, including alloc(1) at order 0.
  • When no block of the right size is free, take the smallest larger free block and split it in halves, again and again, until a half has the right size. The other halves become free blocks. You may choose either half and any address among free blocks of the same size; lowest address first is not required.
  • free(offset) frees the block that starts at offset. Then, while its buddy (the other half of the block it was split from) is also free and whole, merge the two and try again one level up. Freeing something that is not the start of a currently allocated block throws (IllegalArgumentException, std::invalid_argument) without changing the state. This includes an interior offset, an out of range offset, and a repeated free before that offset is allocated again. Any thread may free a block allocated by another thread.
  • largestFreeBlock() returns the largest whole free block, or 0 when exhausted. freeUnits() returns the total unallocated units, accounting for rounded block sizes rather than requested sizes.
  • All methods must be safe when called from many threads at once. Each call takes effect atomically at some point between its invocation and return. Two separate statistics calls need not describe the same instant. If several threads free the same live block, with no intervening allocation, exactly one succeeds and the others throw.

Worked example

An arena of 16 units. alloc(3) needs a block of 4. Only the whole block of 16 is free, so split it into two 8s (0 to 7 and 8 to 15), split the first 8 into two 4s (0 to 3 and 4 to 7), and hand out the block at 0. The free blocks are now 4 at offset 4 and 8 at offset 8. The buddy of the block at offset o with size s is at o XOR s: the buddy of 0 (size 4) is 4, and the buddy of 0 (size 8) is 8. When block 0 is freed, its buddy 4 is free, so they merge into 8 at 0, whose buddy 8 is free, so they merge into the whole 16 again.

What the tests check

  • Orders 0, 1, 2, 6, 10 and 30: initial statistics, negative and extreme integer inputs, exact arena-sized allocations, rounding up to the whole arena, half-arena offsets, splitting down to one unit, exhaustion, and reuse after freeing.
  • Invalid requests and invalid frees preserve state. Interior offsets, never allocated offsets, out of range offsets and repeated frees must be rejected with the specified result or exception.
  • Rounding, alignment and non-overlap across 14 mixed requests. A separate two-allocation case checks that an existing one unit buddy is used before a larger block is split.
  • 64 single units exhaust a 64-unit arena. Freeing only even offsets leaves 32 free units but a largest block of 1; freeing the rest must restore a whole block of 64.
  • An independent occupancy model runs 1200 operations for each seed 17, 91 and 2027. It reconstructs maximal aligned free blocks and checks availability, the smallest source order, alignment, overlap, exact statistics after every operation, and full reuse after draining. It accepts any address choice allowed by the contract.
  • Eight threads free blocks allocated by the main thread. Eight competing frees of one live block must yield exactly one success and seven exceptions, with no duplicated or lost capacity.
  • Eight workers run 1200 mixed operations each in 1024 units, with seed 31 * workerId for ids 1 to 8. Atomic ownership markers detect overlaps; a ninth thread queries both statistics 4000 times during mutation. All blocks must coalesce when the workers finish.
  • Timed joins and watchdogs bound failures. C++ also runs under ThreadSanitizer, including concurrent statistics reads. Stress tests exercise schedules; they do not prove the absence of every possible race.

Taught in: Memory allocators: free lists, buddy allocation and thread safety

Open them one at a time. Each one gives away a little more.

Keep one free list per size: freeLists[k] holds the offsets of free blocks of 2^k units. Use an ordered set so you can both take any block quickly and remove a specific offset (the buddy) quickly. Also remember the size of each allocated block, in a map from offset to k, because free only gets the offset.
Alloc: compute k, the smallest power with 2^k >= size. Find the smallest j >= k with a non empty list, take a block, and while j > k, lower j by one and put the right half (offset + 2^j) in freeLists[j]. Free: look up k, then loop: the buddy is offset ^ (1 << k); if it is in freeLists[k], remove it, set offset to the smaller of the two, raise k, and repeat; else stop. Put the result in freeLists[k].
For thread safety, one lock around every method is the right first answer: a single free can merge through every level, touching many lists, so finer locks would need careful ordering. Do not leave free unlocked because "it only touches my block": the lists, the map and the counter are shared.

What the interviewer asks after your first version works. Try to answer each one out loud before you open it.

What does a buddy allocator waste?
Internal fragmentation: a request of 2^k + 1 units takes 2^(k+1), wasting almost half in the worst case. And external fragmentation: free memory split into blocks whose buddies are busy cannot merge, as the test with the even units shows. Splitting and merging visit at most order levels. With the ordered sets used here, each level also has a logarithmic set cost; a bitmap or tree design can change that bookkeeping cost.
The arena is 16 GiB in 4 KiB pages. How much bookkeeping is that?
2^22 pages, so 23 orders. An allocation map with one entry per allocated block, plus free lists that hold one entry per free block. A compact alternative is a bitmap or a complete binary tree over the arena storing, at each node, the largest free block below it: about 2^23 small entries (about 8 MiB for one byte per entry, or 32 MiB for four bytes per entry), with O(log n) alloc and free and no pointers.
One lock serializes everything. How would you scale it?
Put per thread (or per CPU) caches of small blocks in front: most allocs and frees hit the local cache without any lock, and only refills and flushes take the global lock. That is what jemalloc and tcmalloc style allocators and the kernel's per CPU page lists do. Another option is several independent arenas chosen by thread, trading some fragmentation for less contention.
Could you use a lock per order?
A free that merges walks up from order k to higher orders, and an alloc that splits walks down. Taking order locks one at a time lets two threads see inconsistent states (one removes a buddy while another is splitting it); taking them in a fixed order (always increasing order) avoids deadlock but a merge does not know in advance how high it will go. It can be done, but the complexity rarely pays off compared with per thread caches.
How does free know the size if the caller passes only an offset?
The allocator must remember it: a map from offset to order, as here, or a small header stored just before the block, or (in tree or bitmap versions) the tree itself marks where an allocated block starts. The map also lets you reject double frees and frees of offsets that are not the start of a block.
Can an offset-only API detect a stale free after that address has been allocated again?
No. The offset identifies the current allocation, so a stale caller can accidentally free its replacement. Detecting this requires a handle with a generation number or another allocation identity checked during free. The repeated-free tests here have no intervening allocation.

The idea

Every block size is a power of two and every block is aligned to its size, so a block's buddy is found with one XOR: offset ^ size. Allocation splits a bigger block down to the size you need; freeing merges with the buddy for as long as the buddy is free. Keep one free list per order and a map of allocated blocks. Splitting and merging visit at most order levels, with the ordered-set cost at each level. Thread safety is one lock around each operation, including statistics reads.

Invariants

  • Every unit belongs to exactly one block: either a free block in one of the lists or an allocated block in the map.
  • A block of size 2^k starts at a multiple of 2^k.
  • Two free buddies never sit in the same list side by side: a free always merges them, so after any operation, no block in freeLists[k] has its buddy in freeLists[k].
  • freeUnits equals the sum of the sizes of the blocks in the free lists.

Why each primitive

  • One lock: a single free can modify every level, so the whole structure is one invariant. A synchronized method or a std::lock_guard in each public method keeps it simple and correct.
  • Ordered sets for the free lists: logarithmic insertion and lookup or removal of a specific buddy. Taking the lowest address within the chosen order is a deterministic tie-breaker, not a global packing guarantee or a test requirement.
  • numberOfLeadingZeros or std::bit_width to round up to a power of two without a loop.

Pitfalls

  • Leaving free unlocked: corrupted lists, lost blocks, a wrong free count. This is the broken version the tests catch.
  • Merging once with if instead of looping with while: freeing everything does not restore the full arena.
  • Merging with a "buddy" that is free but split, or of a different size: look for the buddy only in the list of the same order.
  • Computing the buddy as offset + size: right only for left halves. XOR handles both sides.
  • Reading statistics without synchronization: read-only methods still conflict with updates to the lists and counter.
  • Rounding before validating the request: zero, negative sizes and integer overflow can produce an invalid order. Validate first.
  • Forgetting alignment: a block of 8 at offset 4 has no valid buddy and breaks merging forever.

Cost

Let h = order and let b be the number of stored free and allocated blocks. Alloc scans at most h + 1 lists and performs at most h splits; free performs at most h merges. Ordered-set operations cost O(log(b + 1)), so the reference takes O((h + 1) * log(b + 1)) per alloc or free on average, including the expected constant-time hash-map lookup. Pathological hash collisions can cost more. largestFreeBlock() is O(h + 1), freeUnits() is O(1), and metadata uses O(h + b) space. Lock waiting time is additional.

How to say it in the interview

Sizes are rounded up to powers of two and blocks are aligned to their size, so the buddy of a block is offset XOR size. I keep a free list per order and a map from offset to order for allocated blocks. Alloc takes the smallest free block that fits and splits it down, putting the right halves back on the lists. Free looks up the order and keeps merging with the buddy while the buddy is free in the same order's list. Because one free can touch every level, I protect the allocator with a single lock; to scale it I would add per thread caches of small blocks in front, which is what production allocators do.

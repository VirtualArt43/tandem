The toolkit: ReentrantLock, Condition, ReadWriteLock and StampedLock

Acquire locks with deadlines and cancellation, separate condition queues, and choose between exclusive, shared and optimistic access.

Before this:The toolkit: synchronized, wait and notifyExceptions, InterruptedException and try-with-resourcesTime: nanoTime, Duration and TimeUnit

A lock choice is also a waiting policy: can a caller give up, be interrupted, or share access with readers? These Java APIs let you express that policy without hiding the invariant your class must protect.

In plain words

Imagine a library with one key to the records room. A librarian can wait for the key, try once, or leave if the wait takes too long. People waiting for returned books sit on one bench; people waiting for shelf space sit on another.

For reading the catalogue, several people can enter together, but editing requires everyone else to leave. Another option is to copy a small notice and check that nobody edited it during the copy. That last option needs a check before you trust what you copied.

Why this matters for concurrency

You need these contracts when implementing a blocking component or making an LLD class safe for concurrent callers. Choose the state and its invariant before the lock type. Keep both the lock and guarded fields private; exposing either lets callers bypass your protocol.

The monitor toolkit teaches intrinsic locks and waiting; exceptions teaches cleanup and interrupt handling. Here you extend those skills with explicit acquisition policies and multiple condition queues. Single-variable updates belong in volatile and atomics.

API card: Lock and ReentrantLock

java.util.concurrent.locks.Lock is an interface, not a language keyword. ReentrantLock implements it with exclusive ownership and reentrancy. A Lock reference exposes the acquisition contract; implementation-specific monitoring methods require a ReentrantLock reference.

Start with synchronized when automatic scope-based release and one monitor wait set meet your needs. Choose ReentrantLock for timed or interruptible entry, multiple conditions, or an explicit fairness policy. Neither choice prevents deadlock across several locks; you still need a consistent lock acquisition order.

Method or constructorOne-line semantics for ReentrantLock
new ReentrantLock()Create a nonfair lock with no promised acquisition order.
new ReentrantLock(true)Prefer the longest-waiting contender under contention, subject to the acquisition method.
lock()Wait until acquired; interruption does not abort this wait.
lockInterruptibly()Acquire or throw InterruptedException if interrupted before or during acquisition.
tryLock()Acquire immediately and return true, or return false; it may barge even on a fair lock.
tryLock(long time, TimeUnit unit)Acquire within the waiting budget, return false on timeout, or throw on interruption.
unlock()Remove one hold; release to other threads at zero; a non-owner gets IllegalMonitorStateException.
newCondition()Create a separate waiting queue associated with this exact lock.
getHoldCount(), isHeldByCurrentThread()Inspect the calling thread's ownership for assertions or debugging.
isLocked(), getQueueLength(), isFair()Inspect occupancy, an estimated contender count, or the configured fairness policy.

The Java 21 Lock contract gives successful acquisition and release the memory effects of monitor locking. For exclusive locking, the final release happens-before a subsequent acquisition of that same lock by another thread. A failed tryLock() gives no such guarantee; reentrant acquisitions and partial releases need not add memory effects. synchronized (lock) acquires the object's monitor, a different mechanism from lock.lock().

Ownership: one acquisition, one finally

Acquire immediately before the protected try, then release in finally. If acquisition fails or throws, there is no new hold to release. Java's Lock does not implement AutoCloseable, so it does not directly work as a try-with-resources resource.

The output is inside: 2, outer still owns: true, then released: true. Nested calls can reacquire this lock, but each successful acquisition needs its own release. The core reentrancy lesson explores how callbacks can still see half-finished state; reentrancy does not repair an invariant.

isLocked() is not permission to read guarded fields or to unlock. It says neither that you own the lock nor that its state will remain unchanged. Use acquisition to coordinate; use inspection to diagnose.

Waiting with a timeout or an interrupt

Use timed acquisition when failing to obtain the lock is an ordinary outcome. Use interruptible acquisition when cancellation must work while a caller waits for entry. Neither method limits how long the eventual critical section runs, and a timeout is not a real-time return guarantee: the scheduler can delay the caller.

Main deliberately keeps ownership until both workers finish: one can finish through timeout, the other through interruption. Joining while holding a lock would deadlock if the worker needed successful acquisition to finish. The interrupt can arrive before or during lockInterruptibly(); both are supported, with no sleep needed to arrange timing.

For ReentrantLock, lockInterruptibly() and timed tryLock check an already-set interrupt even when acquisition could succeed immediately or reentrantly. Throwing InterruptedException clears the flag: propagate the exception, or restore the flag when handling cancellation at a boundary. Plain lock() keeps waiting and preserves interruption for later handling; untimed tryLock() does not act as an interruption point.

Fairness is a policy, not a deadline. A fair lock reduces overtaking among queued contenders but can reduce throughput and cannot make the operating system schedule a thread. The untimed tryLock() ignores fairness; tryLock(0, TimeUnit.NANOSECONDS) respects it while also checking interruption. See the ReentrantLock acquisition contracts and timeouts and fairness for the deeper trade-offs.

API card: Condition

A Condition is a waiting queue, not a boolean. Your fields describe what must become true. For conditions created by ReentrantLock, all waiting and signalling calls require ownership of that lock.

MethodOne-line semantics
await()Release the associated lock while waiting; reacquire it before returning or throwing on interruption.
await(long time, TimeUnit unit)Interruptible timed wait; false indicates the waiting time elapsed, not that your predicate is false.
awaitNanos(long nanos)Interruptible timed wait returning an estimate of the remaining budget.
awaitUntil(Date deadline)Wait against a wall-clock deadline; prefer relative budgets for elapsed-time limits.
awaitUninterruptibly()Wait without throwing on interrupt; preserve interrupt status on return.
signal()Select one waiter to compete for the lock again; ownership stays with the signaller.
signalAll()Make all current waiters eligible to reacquire and recheck, one owner at a time.

When await() enters a wait, it atomically releases all reentrant holds on its associated lock and restores the hold count on reacquisition. An interrupt already set on entry may cause an exception without releasing it; either way, the caller still owns the lock when handling InterruptedException. It releases no unrelated lock.

Signals are not stored for future waiters, so check the predicate under the lock and wait in a while loop. Spurious wakeups and another thread consuming the opportunity both require that recheck. If interruption wins over a signal, the condition implementation must redirect that signal to another waiter if one exists. These are the Condition contracts.

Timed waits still reacquire the lock, potentially after the nominal timeout. Do not restart the full timeout after a wakeup; use the remaining budget as taught in time units and deadlines. If your public deadline includes initial lock acquisition, subtract that time too.

One budget across entry and condition waiting

This gate remembers that it has opened. awaitOpen spends one elapsed-time budget across lock entry and every condition wait; zero or negative time allows an immediate check only. Once entry succeeds, an observed open gate wins over timeout, so success does not prove that the method returned before the deadline.

Recomputing from System.nanoTime() charges acquisition, reacquisition and loop work to the original budget. If only condition waiting is budgeted, carrying awaitNanos's returned remainder through the loop is the simpler pattern. Neither approach imposes a real-time bound on scheduling or lock reacquisition.

One lock, two conditions, an explicit close rule

This one-slot mailbox accepts integers, blocks producers while full, and lets consumers drain accepted work after closure. null means closed and empty; it cannot collide with an int item. The two predicates share the same protected state, so they need two conditions on one lock, not two independent locks.

Worked example: why closure broadcasts to both queues

Suppose the slot is full and two producers are waiting. Closing rejects both, even if no consumer ever removes the item. Waking just one producer leaves the other asleep; every exit predicate must include closed, and close() must signal all potentially affected waiters.

For ordinary one-item operations, signal() selects the right kind of waiter because producers and consumers have separate queues. For shutdown, every waiter can finish. With different batch sizes on one queue, use signalAll() unless you can select an eligible waiter: waking a producer needing five spaces can strand one needing only one.

Deepen the argument in condition variables, spurious and stolen wakeups, and lost wakeups and signalling. Build a full buffer in producer and consumer; choose a library queue in collections and synchronizers.

API card: ReadWriteLock and ReentrantReadWriteLock

ReadWriteLock is an interface exposing two related Lock objects. A ReentrantReadWriteLock permits multiple readers or one exclusive writer. The modes coordinate through one underlying lock; acquiring its read mode also observes writes published by prior write-mode release.

Method or constructorOne-line semantics
new ReentrantReadWriteLock() / (true)Choose default nonfair admission or approximately arrival-ordered fair admission.
readLock()Return the shared Lock; its holders must not mutate guarded state.
writeLock()Return the exclusive Lock; it excludes other readers and writers.
lock(), lockInterruptibly(), tryLock(...), unlock()Acquire or release the chosen mode using the familiar Lock methods.
writeLock().newCondition()Create a condition associated with the write lock.
readLock().newCondition()Throw UnsupportedOperationException; shared-mode conditions are unsupported.
getReadLockCount(), getReadHoldCount(), getWriteHoldCount()Inspect total read holds or this thread's holds for diagnostics.

There is no read-to-write upgrade. A reader calling writeLock().lock() without releasing its read hold can wait forever for itself. Release read mode, acquire write mode, then recheck because another writer could have won in between. Both modes are reentrant, but that does not create an upgrade operation.

Nonfair admission promises neither reader nor writer priority and can postpone either group indefinitely. Fair admission approximately follows arrival order, admitting an older group of readers together or the oldest writer. New non-reentrant blocking readers wait behind a queued writer, but untimed readLock().tryLock() and writeLock().tryLock() ignore fairness.

Either A or B wins. Both callers must return that winner, so rechecking is part of the operation's correctness, not just an optimization. This small example assumes non-null arguments and returns immutable strings; returning a live mutable map would let callers modify protected state after unlocking.

A write-to-read downgrade is supported: acquire the read lock while still holding the write lock, then release write mode. The retained read hold prevents another writer slipping in. Releasing write mode first and then taking read mode leaves a gap. The Java 21 read-write lock specification defines these asymmetrical rules and the fairness policy.

Use shared reads only for actual reads: an access-ordered LRU get() changes recency and needs exclusive protection. A high read percentage alone does not justify this lock; tiny reads can cost less than managing shared ownership. Compare contention and throughput in read-write locks, and keep broader class design in OOP design for LLD.

API card: StampedLock

StampedLock has shared and exclusive modes plus an optimistic read. Acquisition returns a long stamp used for release or validation. It directly implements neither Lock nor ReadWriteLock; it has no reentrancy, no conditions and no fairness option.

asReadLock(), asWriteLock() and asReadWriteLock() provide adapter views for code using those interfaces. They retain the stamped lock's non-reentrant semantics; newCondition() on either Lock view throws UnsupportedOperationException. There is no consistent reader or writer preference, so do not promise starvation freedom.

MethodOne-line semantics
readLock(), writeLock()Acquire shared or exclusive mode and return a stamp for release.
readLockInterruptibly(), writeLockInterruptibly()Acquire that mode or throw on interruption.
tryReadLock(), tryWriteLock()Return a stamp on immediate success, or zero on failure; (long time, TimeUnit unit) overloads are interruptible.
unlockRead(stamp), unlockWrite(stamp)Release the corresponding held mode with a matching stamp.
tryOptimisticRead()Return an observation stamp, or zero if a writer currently holds the lock; acquire no read lock.
validate(stamp)Report whether a nonzero stamp has survived without an intervening write acquisition.
tryConvertToWriteLock(stamp)Attempt conversion to write mode; zero means failure and leaves an existing read hold intact.
tryConvertToReadLock(stamp)Attempt conversion to shared mode, including an atomic write-to-read downgrade.
tryConvertToOptimisticRead(stamp)Release a held read or write mode on successful conversion, or retain valid optimistic mode; return zero on failure.

Copy, validate, then use the copy. An optimistic reader does not exclude writers. If validation fails, discard every copied field and recopy under a real read lock. With every mutation protected by this same lock's write mode, successful validation supplies visibility of prior published writes and rules out an intervening writer during the copy. It does not protect future reads from the live object. This is the StampedLock optimistic-read contract.

The snapshot is two independent int reads before validation, with no side effects. Do not optimistically traverse a changing HashMap, index using an unvalidated length, or invoke an arbitrary callback: an exception or external effect cannot be undone by later validation. For records themselves, see modern Java.

A conversion is an attempt, not a blocking upgrade. With other readers present, tryConvertToWriteLock can return zero; retain the original stamp, release its read hold before blocking for write mode, and recheck the predicate after acquisition. On success, replace the old stamp with the returned one. Optimistic stamps need no unlock. Real read and write stamps must be released in finally.

Unlike ReentrantLock, stamps are not tied to a thread owner, but keep them local unless your protocol explicitly transfers responsibility. Avoid nested acquisition and unknown callbacks: this lock does not track reentrant ownership. Use it only when the data representation supports safe speculative copies and measurement justifies the complexity.

LockSupport in one paragraph

LockSupport.park() is the low-level parking operation beneath many synchronizers; unpark(thread) makes at most one permit available to that thread. A permit issued to an already-started thread before it parks makes its next park return immediately, but permits do not accumulate and an intervening park can consume one. park can also return spuriously or on interruption, without throwing or clearing the flag; it releases no locks. Always recheck a predicate with a suitable visibility protocol, usually volatile or atomic state, and handle cancellation. parkNanos adds a relative time limit. Prefer higher-level synchronizers for application code; see the LockSupport API and how locks are built.

Common mistakes

  • Unconditional unlock after a failed attempt. Put try/finally only on the successful-acquisition path; an invalid unlock can hide the original interruption.
  • Confusing the object monitor with explicit ownership. synchronized (lock) does not satisfy a ReentrantLock condition's ownership check; condition.wait() is not condition.await().
  • Using if around await. A signal only invites another predicate check after reacquisition. It reserves nothing.
  • Closing one waiting queue only. Update closed state under the lock, include it in every exit predicate, and broadcast to every affected condition.
  • Upgrading while retaining a read hold. Release, acquire write mode, and recheck; a failed stamped conversion also leaves the old read hold to release.
  • Validating before the reads, or trusting live fields afterwards. Return the validated local snapshot; never perform irreversible work speculatively.

In the interview

Expect a choice question, a cancellation bug, or a component whose waiters stop progressing. Start with the invariant, then state the acquisition and wakeup policy. Do not claim a more elaborate lock is automatically faster.

I need interruptible entry and distinct producer and consumer wait queues, so I will use a private ReentrantLock with two conditions. Every state check and update uses that lock; waits loop on predicates, and shutdown wakes both queues.
For shared reads I first verify that the read path really is read-only. For an optimistic read I copy only safe fields, validate, and fall back to a read lock before exposing a result.

Likely follow-ups: what happens after a failed tryLock, why a fair lock can still be overtaken, how to move from read to write mode, and what a signal guarantees. The timed drills below require those distinctions without relying on method names alone.

Key takeaways

  • Match each successful acquisition to one finally release; choose indefinite, timed or interruptible entry deliberately.
  • A condition is a waiting queue on a lock. State predicates live in your fields; signalling does not transfer ownership.
  • Several conditions can share one lock. Wake one eligible waiter for one opportunity, and all affected waiters for closure.
  • Read-write locks permit shared reads and write-to-read downgrade, but not a retained-read upgrade.
  • Stamped optimistic reads copy, validate and fall back. They have neither reentrancy nor conditions.

Quick check

Answer each question, then read why. Your score is saved for your module test.

A thread owns no holds on a ReentrantLock and executes try { lock.lockInterruptibly(); work(); } finally { lock.unlock(); }. It is interrupted before acquisition. What is the bug?
Why. The protected try must start after successful acquisition. finally runs when acquisition throws too; there is no new ownership to release. Interruption is handled by propagation or restoration, not by unlocking someone else's lock.
A thread takes the same ReentrantLock twice, then calls await() in a predicate loop on its condition. After a normal return, with no additional acquisition or release, what does getHoldCount() print?
Why. Condition waiting releases all holds on its associated reentrant lock and restores the original count when it reacquires. A signal does not add or subtract holds. The caller still owes two unlocks.
A fair ReentrantLock is free but has queued contenders. Which statement about a newly arriving non-owner is correct?
Why. The untimed attempt deliberately permits barging. The timed form respects the fair policy and checks interruption, even with a zero waiting budget. Fair admission is not a scheduling or latency guarantee.
Three producers await notFull; two consumers await notEmpty. A close operation makes every waiter eligible to exit. What must it do under the mailbox lock?
Why. All five waiters need a chance to recheck the close predicate. One signal per queue can leave others waiting forever. These ReentrantLock conditions require ownership for signalling, and a broadcast without a changed predicate just sends waiters back to sleep.
A thread holds only a read lock from ReentrantReadWriteLock. It calls writeLock().lock() without releasing read mode. What can happen even if no other threads exist?
Why. Reentrancy is not read-to-write upgrading. Release the read hold before acquiring write mode and recheck protected state afterwards. The unsupported operation here is creating a condition from the read lock, not calling write acquisition.
A reader holds no lock, obtains a nonzero stamp from tryOptimisticRead(), calls validate(stamp) successfully, then reads low and high directly from the shared object. Writers use this same lock's write mode. Is the pair guaranteed consistent?
Why. Validation checks the interval already observed; it does not reserve future access. A reader can validate, read the old low, then read the new high after a writer updates both. Individually atomic int reads do not make the pair atomic. Optimistic mode has no held lock to unlock.
You hold a StampedLock read stamp. tryConvertToWriteLock(stamp) returns zero. Which next step is correct when you still need to write?
Why. A failed conversion leaves the original read hold intact and grants no write access. Losing its stamp leaks the hold; blocking for write mode while retaining it can deadlock. After releasing it, another writer can change the state before your acquisition.

Checkpoint

Write your own answer first. Then compare with the model answer, or ask Bar Raiser to mark it like an interviewer would.

Debug
A developer replaces a mailbox's notifyAll() with one Condition.signal() to improve speed. Producers and consumers still share that condition. Explain a progress failure and two valid repairs.
Model answer

A signal can select the wrong kind of waiter. For example, a consumer removes the last item, signals a waiting consumer rather than a producer, and exits. The selected consumer finds the slot empty and waits again. If every producer remains asleep waiting for space, no thread will fill the available slot.

Keep one condition and use signalAll(), allowing each waiter to recheck its predicate, or create separate notFull and notEmpty conditions on the same lock and signal the appropriate queue after each change. In both repairs, retain while loops and update the state under that lock. Closure still broadcasts to both groups.

Design
A cache has 99 percent reads. Its get() also moves an entry to the front of an LRU list. Would you use a read lock or an optimistic read? Describe a correct starting design and the evidence needed to change it.
Model answer

Neither access mode permits this mutation. Begin with one exclusive lock covering the map lookup, recency move and any related bookkeeping; use the same protocol for insertions and eviction. Do not expose live mutable entries or call slow external loaders while holding the lock.

Measure contention and operation duration before changing the design. A genuine read-only lookup could use a shared lock, while a short safe snapshot might use optimistic validation. If recency updates dominate, consider a different eviction policy or partitioned caches, explaining the resulting semantics. A read percentage describes method names, not necessarily state access.

Pseudo code
You hold a StampedLock read stamp and discover that a value needs updating. Describe the complete conversion and cleanup protocol, including failure.
Model answer

Keep the original stamp. Attempt tryConvertToWriteLock(stamp) and store its result in a separate variable. On nonzero success, replace the original stamp with the new write stamp; the conversion retained continuous protection. On zero failure, release the original read stamp, then acquire a write stamp in a new acquisition scope and recheck whether the update is still needed.

Mutate only while holding write mode, and release the current write stamp in finally. If the update is not needed and you still hold read mode, release that read stamp instead. Track the held mode explicitly so an exception never unlocks the wrong mode or unlocks twice. Calling blocking writeLock() while retaining a failed conversion's read hold is not a fallback.

Say it out loud

Interviews are spoken. Answer each question out loud as if the interviewer just asked it. Tandem transcribes you on this Mac, word for word, measures your pace, fillers and pauses, and Bar Raiser scores what you said and how you said it.

Out loudabout 75 s
"Why would you choose ReentrantLock here, and how will you make waiting cancellable?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Choose explicit locking for timed or interruptible entry or multiple conditions
  • State the protected invariant and keep the lock private
  • Acquire before try and release each successful hold in finally
  • Distinguish timeout false from InterruptedException and preserve cancellation
  • Explain fair admission and the untimed tryLock exception
How a senior would say it
I would choose ReentrantLock when the component needs timed entry, interruptible entry, or separate waiting queues. First I would name the invariant and keep the lock private so every method uses the same protocol. For example, a mailbox's item and closed flag belong under one lock. I acquire immediately before the protected try block and release in finally, only after successful acquisition. If callers need cancellation while waiting for entry, I use lockInterruptibly. If they also need a waiting budget, I use timed tryLock and handle false as an ordinary timeout. InterruptedException is a separate cancellation outcome. I propagate it from a component method, or restore the flag and leave at a Runnable boundary. Plain lock does not abort acquisition on interruption. I would consider a fair lock only if reducing overtaking matters enough to justify its throughput cost. It does not promise a return deadline, and untimed tryLock can still barge. My choice therefore follows the waiting contract, not a belief that explicit locks are always faster.

Likely follow up: How would a single request deadline cover both lock acquisition and a subsequent condition wait?

Out loudabout 75 s
"Your queue sometimes hangs when closing. Walk me through what you would inspect."
What a strong answer covers
  • Check closed state and all waiting predicates under the same lock
  • Identify both producer and consumer condition queues
  • Explain await releasing the lock and reacquiring before predicate recheck
  • Broadcast closure to every affected queue while owning the lock
  • Distinguish rejecting producers from draining accepted items and ending consumers
How a senior would say it
I would first check whether every blocked caller has a way to observe closure and finish. The closed flag and the queue contents must use the same lock. Producers may be waiting for space, while consumers may be waiting for an item, so I inspect both waiting queues. Each await belongs inside a while loop whose predicate also includes closure. Await releases the associated lock while waiting and reacquires it before continuing. A signal only makes a waiter eligible to compete; it does not transfer the lock or reserve an item. Close must set the flag while holding the lock and signal all waiters on both conditions. Signalling just one can leave the rest asleep forever. Then I check the intended API behaviour. Producers reject after closure. Consumers keep taking already accepted items, then return an end result once the queue is empty. I would test closure with blocked producers and blocked consumers separately, plus a race between close and put. Finally, I would join every worker so a stranded waiter fails the test.

Likely follow up: Why is signal often sufficient during ordinary put and take operations, but insufficient for closing?

Out loudabout 75 s
"Compare ReentrantReadWriteLock and StampedLock for a read-heavy service. What could go wrong?"
What a strong answer covers
  • Require genuinely read-only work and measure whether shared access helps
  • Explain shared readers and exclusive writers with ReentrantReadWriteLock
  • Reject retained-read upgrades and describe release, write acquisition and recheck
  • Describe stamped copy, validate and locked fallback in that order
  • Mention non-reentrancy, no conditions and unsafe speculative side effects
How a senior would say it
I would start by checking that the reads do not mutate anything and that their duration justifies more lock machinery. ReentrantReadWriteLock allows concurrent readers and an exclusive writer. Its rules are relatively straightforward, and its modes support reentrancy. But a reader cannot keep its read hold and block for write mode. I must release, acquire write mode, and recheck because someone else may have changed the state in that gap. StampedLock adds optimistic reads for small snapshots. I obtain a stamp, copy the fields into locals, validate, and use those locals only after success. On failure I recopy under a real read lock. Validating before the reads protects nothing that happens afterwards. I would avoid speculative collection traversal and callbacks because validation cannot undo an exception or external side effect. StampedLock also lacks reentrancy and conditions, which makes nested calls more delicate. For a two-integer snapshot it may be suitable; for an LRU lookup that changes recency, neither shared nor optimistic reading permits that write. I would measure before choosing the more complex option.

Likely follow up: What happens to your existing read hold when tryConvertToWriteLock returns zero?

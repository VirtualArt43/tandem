The dining philosophers

Ordering puzzlesmedium30 minLeetCode 1226

The classic deadlock, on purpose: five threads, five locks, each thread needs two. Interviewers use it to see whether you can name the four deadlock conditions, break one of them with a one-line argument, and then talk about starvation, which is a different problem. Apple has asked it in a concurrency round, and a D. E. Shaw interviewer asked how to avoid deadlock without starvation.

The task

Five philosophers, numbered 0 to 4, sit around a round table with a fork between each pair of neighbours. Philosopher p uses fork p on the left and fork (p + 1) % 5 on the right. To eat, a philosopher needs both forks. Each philosopher runs in its own thread and calls wantsToEat every time it is hungry, many times over.

Implement wantsToEat so that each call invokes each of its five callbacks exactly once: both picks in either order, then eat, then both puts in either order. Neighbours share a fork, so they may never hold that fork together. Prevent deadlock and finish every call in a finite workload, assuming runnable threads get scheduled and callbacks return. FIFO admission and starvation freedom under an endless stream of requests are follow ups, not requirements of this harness.

Rules: the callbacks record events; your code provides the synchronization. Call them synchronously in the calling thread. Hold each fork exclusively from its pick callback until its put callback records its release, including throughout eat. Finish all five callbacks before returning. Do not release and reacquire a fork within one call. No sleep to dodge deadlock. In C++, ThreadSanitizer must stay quiet.

Inputs are valid philosopher IDs, 0 to 4, with at most one active call per philosopher on a table. Callbacks may pause, but return normally and do not call wantsToEat recursively. Callback exceptions and Java interruption are outside the required workload. A single table lock is accepted; allowing non-neighbours to eat together is the stronger design discussed below.

For example, philosopher 4 uses forks 4 and 0. A valid callback trace is pickRightFork, pickLeftFork, eat, putLeftFork, putRightFork. Its real locks can be acquired in numerical order, 0 then 4, independently of which pick callback runs first.

What the tests check

  • Each of the five philosophers makes three isolated calls. Every call invokes both picks exactly once, then exactly one meal, then both puts exactly once, all in the calling thread. Extra release and reacquire cycles fail even if the final fork state looks correct.
  • Callback boundaries: 20 gated schedules pause eat or a put callback before it records release. Each neighbour tries to eat while the shared fork is still held, including the shared fork 0 between philosophers 4 and 0.
  • Five at once: a barrier starts all five requests together for 200 rounds. A short pause after the first pick encourages circular wait in a naive implementation.
  • A longer run: each philosopher completes 300 meals. Every call checks fork ownership, callback order, exact counts and no forks left held on return.
  • All calls must finish without exceptions. Timed waits and whole-suite watchdogs report stalls without hanging; C++ also runs under ThreadSanitizer.

The callback count checks are deterministic. Gated tests use a 30 ms observation window after the neighbour starts; interleavings still depend on the scheduler. The watchdogs allow 3.5 s in Java and 6 s in C++ for harness work. These finite tests exercise safety and progress; they cannot prove deadlock freedom for every schedule or starvation freedom under unbounded contention.

Taught in: The dining philosophers

Open them one at a time. Each one gives away a little more.

Write the naive version in your head: lock the left fork, then the right fork. Find the schedule that deadlocks it. Which of the four conditions for deadlock (mutual exclusion, hold and wait, no preemption, circular wait) is the easiest to break here?
Circular wait. Number the forks and always lock the lower-numbered fork of your two first. Philosophers 0 to 3 still go left then right, but philosopher 4 reaches for fork 0 before fork 4. Now the circle of waiting can never close.
In Java, a lock per fork (Object with nested synchronized blocks, or ReentrantLock); in C++, a std::mutex per fork with two std::lock_guards in the right order. Once both are held, call the five callbacks: pick, pick, eat, put, put.

What the interviewer asks after your first version works. Try to answer each one out loud before you open it.

Prove that your solution cannot deadlock.
A deadlock needs a cycle of threads, each holding a lock and waiting for the next one's lock. In my solution every thread holds a lower-numbered fork while waiting for a higher-numbered one, so along any chain of waiting the fork numbers strictly increase. A chain that strictly increases can never return to where it started, so there is no cycle, and no deadlock. Mutual exclusion, hold and wait and no preemption all still hold; only circular wait is gone.
Can a philosopher starve?
Deadlock freedom does not imply starvation freedom. With unfair locks, a philosopher's neighbours can keep winning the shared forks, so in theory it can wait forever while everyone else eats. For eventual service under fair scheduling and terminating callbacks, use fair locks (new ReentrantLock(true) in Java, with blocking acquisitions that respect fairness) together with global lock ordering, or use an arbiter that serves requests in ticket order. Neither gives a fixed wall-clock bound without assumptions about scheduling and meal duration. A fair admission semaphore alone does not make unfair fork locks fair. Fairness can cost throughput, so name the trade. A D. E. Shaw interviewer asked for exactly this in 2024: no deadlock and no starvation.
Give two other ways to break the deadlock.
Let at most four philosophers try at once, with a semaphore of 4: with five forks and four diners, at least one can eventually acquire both forks, so the circle cannot close. Or acquire the left lock, then tryLock the right one, and if that fails, release the left lock and retry after a random backoff. Invoke the pick callbacks only after both locks are acquired so failed attempts do not add callbacks (without the randomness, everyone can retry in lockstep forever: a livelock). An arbiter can reserve both forks atomically under one short lock, then release that lock while the philosopher eats. It can admit non-neighbours together. Holding one global lock across all callbacks is simpler but serializes meals.
How many philosophers can eat at once, and does your scheme allow it?
With five forks, at most two, because each needs two forks and neighbours share one. Lock ordering and the seat limit both allow two to eat together (for example philosophers 0 and 2). A single global lock is also deadlock free but lets only one eat at a time, which is why it is the weakest answer. With n philosophers the ordering argument is unchanged, and the seat limit becomes n minus 1.
What changes if callbacks can throw or a Java caller can be interrupted?
Specify the failure contract first. Decide whether a throwing pick means the fork was acquired, whether put callbacks can fail, and which exception wins during cleanup. Track successful acquisitions, release locks and admission permits on every exit, and only issue matching cleanup callbacks. Java needs finally and interruptible lock acquisition when cancellation while waiting is required; C++ needs RAII. Releasing a mutex automatically does not itself call a missing put callback. These behaviours are outside this problem's nonthrowing callback contract.
How would you find this deadlock in a running service?
In Java, take a thread dump with jstack or jcmd Thread.print: the JVM prints "Found one Java-level deadlock" and the cycle of threads and locks for synchronized and ReentrantLock. In C++, attach a debugger and print every thread's stack: all five sit in a mutex lock call, each holding the lock the next one wants. On Linux, ThreadSanitizer can report potential lock order inversions with deadlock detection enabled. Apple clang on this Mac does not report them. Timed tests detect a stall, but a stack dump is needed to establish its cause.

Try for at least twenty minutes first. Reading a solution feels like learning; writing one is learning.

The idea

The naive solution (left, then right) deadlocks when all five pick up their left fork at the same moment: each holds one fork and waits for its neighbour's, a cycle of five. Of the four deadlock conditions, circular wait is the one to break. Lock the two forks in a global order, lower number first (lock ordering). Philosophers 0 to 3 lock left then right as before, but philosopher 4 locks fork 0 before fork 4, so the cycle cannot form. The invariant is every thread acquires forks in increasing number order, which makes every chain of waiting strictly increasing.

Why each line is there

  • A lock per fork, not one lock for the table. Philosophers who share no fork (0 and 2, say) can eat at the same time.
  • min then max. The whole fix is the order of two lock statements. Compute it from the fork numbers, never from "left" and "right", which are relative to each philosopher.
  • The callbacks run while both locks are held. The locks enforce exclusive ownership. The callbacks must still follow the protocol: both picks, then eat, then both puts, each exactly once. Either order within the pick pair or put pair is valid.
  • Release in reverse order, automatically. Nested synchronized blocks and std::lock_guard release the internal locks on exit. Completing put callbacks after an exception needs a separate failure contract; callbacks here do not throw.

The seat limit, the other answer interviewers expect:

In C++ there is a third idiom: std::scoped_lock both(forks_[left], forks_[right]); locks both mutexes with a built-in deadlock avoidance algorithm (lock one, try the other, back off and retry). It is correct and short; say that it hides the ordering argument, so be ready to explain what it does.

Pitfalls

  • Left then right for everyone. Deadlocks as soon as all five hold their left fork; the tests pause after the first pick to encourage this schedule, but no finite run proves the absence of deadlock.
  • Ordering by "left" and "right". They are relative, so "always left first" is the naive solution again. Order by fork number.
  • One global lock. Deadlock free, but only one philosopher eats at a time.
  • tryLock and retry without backoff. Can trade deadlock for livelock when all contenders retry in lockstep; backoff reduces that risk but does not prove fairness.
  • Confusing deadlock freedom with starvation freedom. Ordering removes the first, not the second.

Cost

Each call performs O(1) lock and callback operations and the table stores five locks, O(1) space. Waiting time is not bounded by that operation count. For n philosophers, storage becomes O(n), and the same ordering proof applies for n at least 2.

How to say it in the interview

Deadlock needs four things: mutual exclusion, hold and wait, no preemption and circular wait. The forks must stay exclusive, so I break circular wait: every philosopher locks the lower-numbered of its two forks first. Philosopher 4 therefore takes fork 0 before fork 4, every chain of waiting goes to higher fork numbers, and a cycle is impossible. Two philosophers who share no fork still eat in parallel. This is deadlock free but not starvation free; if that matters I'd use fair ordered locks or ticket-based arbitration. A semaphore of four is another deadlock fix, but a fair semaphore alone does not guarantee fair access to the forks.
